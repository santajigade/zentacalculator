## How to use the combined gas law calculator

Enter **five** of the six values (initial and final pressure, volume and temperature) to get the sixth.

## Combined gas law formula

<p class="formula">P₁V₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂V₂ ÷ T₂</p>

## Worked example

10 L of air at 101.325 kPa and 25 °C is compressed to 200 kPa and warms to 60 °C:

V₂ = 101.325 × 10 × 333.15 ÷ (298.15 × 200) = **5.66 L**

It helps to split the answer into two factors. The pressure ratio P₁ ÷ P₂ = 0.5066 roughly halves the volume, and the temperature ratio T₂ ÷ T₁ = 1.1174 expands it again by about 12%, so V₂ = 10 × 0.5066 × 1.1174 = 5.661 L. The sample holds n = P₁V₁ ÷ (R T₁) = 0.409 mol throughout, but the combined law never needs that number because it cancels between the two states.

## Standard conditions

Gas volumes are often reported at standard conditions so they can be compared:

| Standard | Temperature | Pressure |
|---|---|---|
| STP (IUPAC, since 1982) | 0 °C (273.15 K) | 100 kPa |
| STP (older) | 0 °C | 101.325 kPa (1 atm) |
| NTP | 20 °C | 101.325 kPa |
| Normal (industrial Nm³) | 0 °C | 101.325 kPa |

## Correcting gas volume to standard conditions

A gas meter reads 10 m³ at 2 bar(g) (3.013 bar absolute) and 30 °C. At normal conditions:

<p class="formula">V<sub>N</sub> = 10 × (3.013 ÷ 1.01325) × (273.15 ÷ 303.15) = 26.8 Nm³</p>

This is how gas flow is billed and compared in industry.

## Adding the amount of gas

If the amount of gas changes too, use the full [ideal gas law](/physics/ideal-gas-law/): PV = nRT. The combined gas law is the special case where n is constant.

## Second example: a weather balloon climbing to 10 km

A sounding balloon is filled with 5.00 m³ of helium at 101.325 kPa and 15 °C. Near 10 km the standard atmosphere is about 26.44 kPa and −50 °C (223.15 K). If the envelope stretches freely so the gas stays at the outside pressure and cools to the outside temperature:

V₂ = 5.00 × 101.325 × 223.15 ÷ (288.15 × 26.44) = **14.84 m³**

The pressure drop alone would almost quadruple the volume; the cold air takes back part of that, leaving a factor of 2.97. Balloons are filled loosely for exactly this reason.

## Breathing gas: BTPS to STPD

Lung function labs report volumes in two ways. Exhaled air is at body temperature, ambient pressure and saturated with water vapour (BTPS, 37 °C), while oxygen uptake is quoted dry at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa (STPD). Water vapour at 37 °C exerts 6.27 kPa, so the dry gas pressure is 95.06 kPa. A 5.00 L breath at BTPS becomes

V<sub>STPD</sub> = 5.00 × (95.06 ÷ 101.325) × (273.15 ÷ 310.15) = **4.131 L**

which is 17.4% smaller. Gas meters, flow computers and spirometers all apply the same P and T ratios automatically.

## Final volume of 10 L of gas starting at 101.325 kPa and 25 °C

| Final pressure | 0 °C | 25 °C | 60 °C | 100 °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 kPa | 18.57 L | 20.27 L | 22.64 L | 25.36 L |
| 101.325 kPa | 9.16 L | 10.00 L | 11.17 L | 12.52 L |
| 200 kPa | 4.64 L | 5.07 L | 5.66 L | 6.34 L |
| 500 kPa | 1.86 L | 2.03 L | 2.26 L | 2.54 L |

Reading across a row shows Charles's law, down a column shows Boyle's law, and the default case sits at 200 kPa and 60 °C.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering gauge pressure.** If the 200 kPa final pressure were a gauge reading, the absolute value is 301.325 kPa and the volume becomes 3.76 L, not 5.66 L.
- **Dividing by Celsius temperatures.** Using 25 and 60 directly gives 12.16 L, more than double the right answer, and 0 °C would mean dividing by zero.
- **Ignoring water vapour.** Gas collected over water or exhaled breath is partly H₂O, which condenses on cooling; correct the dry gas partial pressure, not the total.
- **Letting the amount of gas change.** Leaks, reactions, gas dissolving in a liquid or condensation all break the fixed n assumption; use PV = nRT for each state instead.
- **Reading the temperature too early.** A gas compressed quickly heats up and then cools towards ambient, so the pressure drifts until the gas and vessel reach the same temperature.

## Special cases

- Constant temperature → Boyle's law (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂)
- Constant pressure → Charles's law (V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂)
- Constant volume → Gay-Lussac's law (P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂)
