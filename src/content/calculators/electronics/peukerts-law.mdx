  - q: What is Peukert's law?
    a: An empirical rule for lead-acid batteries, t = H × (C ÷ (I × H))^k, where C is the rated capacity at the rated discharge time H and k is the Peukert exponent. With k = 1 the battery would give its full rated Ah at any current.
  - q: What is a typical Peukert exponent?
    a: Roughly 1.05–1.15 for AGM, 1.1–1.25 for gel and 1.2–1.4 for flooded lead-acid batteries; lithium-ion cells are close to 1.0–1.05. The battery datasheet's capacity-vs-rate table gives the exact value.
  - q: How do I calculate the Peukert exponent from a datasheet?
    a: Take two ratings, for example the 20 h and 5 h capacities. Convert each to a current (I = Ah ÷ h), then k = ln(t₂/t₁) ÷ ln(I₁/I₂).

    a: Below the rated current it predicts more than the rated capacity, which real batteries do not reliably deliver because of self-discharge. Treat the rated Ah as the ceiling.

## How to use the Peukert's law calculator

Enter the **rated capacity** in amp-hours, the **rated discharge time** it was measured at (C20 = 20 h, C10 = 10 h, C5 = 5 h), the **load current** and the **Peukert exponent** from the datasheet. The calculator gives the rated current, the realistic runtime, the naive Ah ÷ A runtime for comparison and the effective capacity at your current. These are times to 100% discharge at a constant current and room temperature.

## Peukert's law formula

<p class="formula">t = H × ( C ÷ (I × H) )<sup>k</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; effective capacity = I × t</p>

<p class="formula">k = ln(t₂ ÷ t₁) ÷ ln(I₁ ÷ I₂)</p>

## Worked example

A 100 Ah battery rated at the 20-hour rate (k = 1.2) supplies a 10 A load:

- Rated current = 100 ÷ 20 = **5 A**
- Runtime = 20 × (100 ÷ (10 × 20))^1.2 = 20 × 0.5^1.2 = **8.71 h**
- Naive estimate 100 ÷ 10 = 10 h — about 13% too optimistic
- Effective capacity = 10 × 8.7055 = **87.06 Ah**

## Practical notes

- **Usable depth of discharge** — lead-acid batteries last much longer if discharged only to 50%; multiply the runtime by the depth of discharge you plan to use.
- **Temperature** — capacity falls by roughly 0.5–1% per °C below 25 °C for lead-acid; allow extra margin in cold locations.
- **Varying loads** — Peukert's law assumes a constant current. For mixed loads, apply it to each period or use the average current as an approximation.

## Three practical questions for the default battery

The default 100 Ah C20 battery with k = 1.2 runs a 10 A load for 8.71 h to full discharge. Building on that:

1. **To 50% depth of discharge**, the usual limit for long lead-acid life, the runtime is about 0.5 × 8.71 = **4.35 h**, not the 5 h a simple Ah ÷ A estimate suggests.
2. **For a required 5 h runtime**, invert the law: I = C ÷ (H × (t ÷ H)<sup>1/k</sup>) = 100 ÷ (20 × 0.25<sup>0.833</sup>) = **15.87 A** is the largest constant load the battery can carry.
3. **With a second battery in parallel**, each battery supplies 5 A, its rated current, so the bank runs for **20 h**, which is 2.30 times the single battery runtime rather than twice it.

## Measuring capacity and the exponent

A capacity test discharges a fully charged battery at constant current at 25 °C until the terminal voltage reaches the end voltage, usually 1.75 V per cell (10.5 V for a 12 V block). The rated current for the default battery is 5 A, so a healthy unit should last close to 20 h. Repeat the test at a higher current, such as 20 A, and the two runtimes give the exponent with the formula above. Batteries below 80% of rated capacity are normally due for replacement, and their exponent tends to rise with age.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering the wrong rating hours.** If the same 100 Ah figure were a C10 rating, the 10 A load would be the rated current and the runtime would be 10 h, not 8.71 h.
- **Converting watts to amps at nominal voltage.** A 1,000 W inverter load at 90% efficiency draws 92.6 A at 12 V but 101.0 A as the battery sags to 11 V, so use the lower voltage.
- **Using a lead-acid exponent for lithium.** A LiFePO4 battery with k ≈ 1.03 would run the default load for about 9.79 h; entering 1.2 understates it by more than an hour.
- **Ignoring temperature.** The exponent and capacity are quoted at 25 °C; a cold battery loses capacity at every current.
- **Applying it to short pulses.** Engine cranking and inrush loads recover between pulses, so the constant current law overstates their effect.
