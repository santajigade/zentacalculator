## How to use the free space path loss calculator

Enter the **distance** and **frequency** of the link. The calculator returns the free-space path loss in dB. Add the **transmit power** and both **antenna gains** to get the received power, and compare it with the receiver sensitivity. To find the maximum free-space range, clear the distance and enter the minimum received power.

## Free-space path loss formula

<p class="formula">FSPL = 20 log₁₀(4π d f ÷ c)</p>

<p class="formula">FSPL (dB) = 20 log₁₀ d<sub>km</sub> + 20 log₁₀ f<sub>MHz</sub> + 32.44</p>

<p class="formula">P<sub>r</sub> = P<sub>t</sub> + G<sub>t</sub> + G<sub>r</sub> − FSPL</p>

## Example

A LoRa sensor at 868 MHz, 2 km from the gateway, 14 dBm transmit power and 2 dBi antennas at both ends:

- FSPL = 20 log₁₀(4π × 2000 × 868 × 10⁶ ÷ 299,792,458) = **97.24 dB**
- P<sub>r</sub> = 14 + 2 + 2 − 97.24 = **-79.24 dBm** — far above LoRa sensitivity, leaving a large margin for obstacles

## Notes

- FSPL uses isotropic antennas; real antenna gains are added separately in the link budget.
- Keep the first Fresnel zone clear — at 868 MHz over 2 km its radius at mid-path is about 13 m, so mount antennas high.
- Convert dBm to milliwatts with the [dBm to watts calculator](/electronics/dbm-to-watts/).

## Link margin and maximum range from the defaults

The default link delivers **-79.24 dBm**, which is only about 11.9 pW of real power at the receiver. A LoRa receiver at 125 kHz bandwidth decodes down to roughly -123 dBm at SF7 and -137 dBm at SF12, so:

- Margin at SF7 = -79.24 − (-123) = **43.76 dB**
- Keeping a 20 dB fade margin, the allowed path loss is 14 + 2 + 2 + 123 − 20 = **121 dB** at SF7, which free space reaches at **30.8 km**
- At SF12 the allowed loss is 135 dB, or 155 km in free space

Those long distances are rarely achieved on the ground. With both antennas 10 m high, the radio horizon (4.12 × (√h₁ + √h₂) km) is only about **26 km**, so terrain, Earth curvature and Fresnel zone blockage set the real limit long before free-space loss does.

## Checking a link in the field

Most LoRa gateways and Wi-Fi access points report the RSSI of every packet. Compare it with the predicted received power: the difference is the excess loss from obstacles, cables and multipath. A survey with a portable node at the planned sensor locations, logging RSSI and SNR, is quicker than any model. A spectrum analyser with a calibrated antenna gives a more accurate absolute level when RSSI accuracy (often ±3 to 6 dB) is not enough.

The default transmitter radiates 16 dBm EIRP, or 13.85 dBm ERP since a half-wave dipole has 2.15 dBi of gain. Regulatory limits are often written in ERP, such as the 25 mW (14 dBm) ERP limit in parts of the European 868 MHz band, so check which reference your local rule uses before adding antenna gain.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the wrong unit constant.** The 32.44 dB constant is for kilometres and megahertz; with metres and megahertz it becomes -27.55 dB, and mixing them puts the answer 60 dB out.
- **Treating dBd as dBi.** An antenna sold as 3 dBd is 5.15 dBi; entering dBd figures as dBi understates the gain, while the reverse overstates it.
- **Forgetting cable and connector loss.** Five metres of thin RG58 at 868 MHz loses about 2.5 dB, which comes straight off the link budget at each end.
- **Assuming free space indoors.** Each concrete wall can add 10 to 20 dB, so a calculated indoor margin of 40 dB can vanish behind two or three walls.
- **Ignoring the Fresnel zone.** A clear visual line of sight is not enough; keep at least 60% of the first Fresnel zone free of trees, roofs and ground.
