## How to use the final temperature calculator

Enter the **mass**, **specific heat capacity** and **starting temperature** of each substance. The calculator returns the equilibrium temperature and the heat that flows from substance 1 to substance 2 (negative if substance 1 is the colder one). It assumes an insulated container and no melting or boiling.

## Final temperature formula

<p class="formula">T<sub>f</sub> = (m<sub>1</sub>c<sub>1</sub>T<sub>1</sub> + m<sub>2</sub>c<sub>2</sub>T<sub>2</sub>) ÷ (m<sub>1</sub>c<sub>1</sub> + m<sub>2</sub>c<sub>2</sub>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; Q = m<sub>1</sub>c<sub>1</sub>(T<sub>1</sub> − T<sub>f</sub>)</p>

## Example

A 0.5 kg aluminium block at 150 °C is dropped into 2 kg of water at 20 °C:

- Heat capacities: 0.5 × 897 = 448.5 J/K and 2 × 4186 = 8,372 J/K
- T<sub>f</sub> = (448.5 × 150 + 8,372 × 20) ÷ (448.5 + 8,372) = **26.61 °C**
- Heat transferred Q = 448.5 × (150 − 26.61) = **55.34 kJ**

## Notes

- Real containers absorb some heat; in a calorimeter, add the vessel's heat capacity (its "water equivalent") to the cold side.
- For heating a flowing stream rather than a batch, use the [heat duty calculator](/engineering/heat-duty/).

## Why the result sits close to the water temperature

The final temperature is a weighted average, and the weights are the heat capacities m × c. In the default example the water holds 8,372 J/K against the aluminium block's 448.5 J/K, so the water carries 94.9% of the weight. That is why a block at 150 °C only lifts the water from 20 °C to 26.61 °C. The table keeps the 2 kg of water at 20 °C and changes the mass of the aluminium block at 150 °C:

| Aluminium mass | Heat capacity m·c | Final temperature | Heat given to water |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 kg | 89.7 J/K | 21.38 °C | 11.54 kJ |
| 0.25 kg | 224.2 J/K | 23.39 °C | 28.39 kJ |
| 0.5 kg | 448.5 J/K | 26.61 °C | 55.34 kJ |
| 1 kg | 897.0 J/K | 32.58 °C | 105.33 kJ |
| 2 kg | 1,794.0 J/K | 42.94 °C | 192.06 kJ |
| 5 kg | 4,485.0 J/K | 65.35 °C | 379.66 kJ |

Doubling the block mass does not double the temperature rise, because a bigger block also stops at a higher temperature and gives up less heat per kilogram.

## Allowing for the container

A real calorimeter cup warms up too. If the 2 kg of water sits in a 0.3 kg aluminium cup that starts at 20 °C, the cup adds 269.1 J/K to the cold side and the equilibrium drops to **26.41 °C** instead of 26.61 °C. Add the vessel's heat capacity (its water equivalent) to substance 2, or measure it once by mixing known masses of hot and cold water.

## Finding an unknown specific heat

The method of mixtures runs the equation backwards. Drop 0.5 kg of an unknown metal at 150 °C into 2 kg of water at 20 °C and record a steady 23.5 °C. Then c = m<sub>2</sub>c<sub>2</sub>(T<sub>f</sub> − T<sub>2</sub>) ÷ (m<sub>1</sub>(T<sub>1</sub> − T<sub>f</sub>)) = 8,372 × 3.5 ÷ (0.5 × 126.5) = **463 J/kg·K**, which points to steel or iron. The temperature rise is small, so a thermometer error of only 0.1 °C shifts the answer to 477 J/kg·K. Use a fine resolution thermometer, stir well, and transfer the hot sample quickly so it does not cool in the air.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing kJ and J.** Specific heats here are in J/kg·K; entering 4.186 for water (the kJ value) makes water look a thousand times easier to heat than it is.
- **Using a final temperature outside the two starting values.** Without a phase change or a chemical reaction, T<sub>f</sub> must lie between T<sub>1</sub> and T<sub>2</sub>. Anything else means a typing error.
- **Ignoring steam or ice.** Metal hotter than 100 °C dropped into a little water can boil some of it off, and ice must absorb 334 kJ/kg to melt before its temperature rises.
- **Reading the thermometer too early.** Wait for the reading to peak and hold before recording it.
- **Leaving out the container.** A metal cup or beaker can absorb several percent of the heat in a small experiment.
