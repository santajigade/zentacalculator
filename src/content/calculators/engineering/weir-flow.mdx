## How to use the weir flow calculator

Choose the **weir type** and enter the **head** — the height of the water surface above the bottom of the notch or the crest. For a V-notch, set the notch angle and discharge coefficient; for a rectangular weir, set the crest length.

## Weir flow formulas

**V-notch (Kindsvater–Shen form)**

<p class="formula">Q = (8/15) × C<sub>d</sub> × √(2g) × tan(θ/2) × h<sup>5/2</sup></p>

**Rectangular, contracted (Francis)**

<p class="formula">Q = 1.84 × (L − 0.2h) × h<sup>3/2</sup></p>

with Q in m³/s and L and h in metres.

## Installation guidelines

- Sharp, thin crest (bevelled downstream).
- Free-falling, aerated nappe — the water must spring clear of the weir plate.
- Head measured at 4–5 × h<sub>max</sub> upstream with a stilling well or level sensor.
- Approach channel straight and wide enough for low approach velocity.
- Minimum head about 50 mm for V-notches to avoid surface-tension errors.

## Automating with level sensors

Ultrasonic or radar level transmitters above the weir measure head continuously. The flow computer then applies the weir equation — a common setup for effluent flow monitoring.

## Worked example: 90° V-notch at 150 mm head

The default inputs are a 90° V-notch, C<sub>d</sub> = 0.585, and h = 150 mm:

- √(2g) = √(2 × 9.80665) = 4.4287 m^0.5/s
- tan(90° ÷ 2) = 1
- h^2.5 = 0.150^2.5 = 0.008714 m^2.5
- Q = 8/15 × 0.585 × 4.4287 × 1 × 0.008714 = **0.01204 m³/s = 43.35 m³/h (12.04 L/s)**

Switching the type to rectangular with the default 0.5 m crest gives Q = 1.84 × (0.5 − 0.2 × 0.15) × 0.15^1.5 = **180.9 m³/h**, about 4.2 times as much at the same head.

Narrow notches suit small effluent flows; a 120° notch passes more water before overtopping.

## How accurately must the head be measured?

Because flow goes with h^2.5, a 1 mm head error at 150 mm shifts the V-notch flow by **1.7%**. At 50 mm head the same 1 mm error is **5.1%** of a flow of only 2.78 m³/h. That is why the head is read in a stilling well with a hook gauge or an ultrasonic sensor that has been zeroed against the notch bottom, not against the channel floor.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring head at the plate.** The surface draws down near the weir; read it 4 to 5 heads upstream.
- **Zeroing to the wrong point.** Head is measured from the notch vertex or crest, so survey that level into the sensor zero.
- **A submerged or clinging nappe.** If tailwater rises above the crest or the nappe sticks to the plate, the free discharge formulas no longer apply.
- **Using the Francis formula for a full width weir.** A suppressed weir without end contractions needs the 0.2h term removed.
- **Letting silt build up upstream.** A filled approach pool raises approach velocity and makes the weir read low.
