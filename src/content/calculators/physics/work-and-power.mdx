## How to use the work & power calculator

Enter the **force**, the **distance** it moves and the **time** taken. You get the work done in joules and the power in watts (or hp).

## Work & power formulas

<p class="formula">W = F × d   P = W ÷ t</p>

## Worked example

Pushing a cart with 500 N over 10 m in 20 s:

- Work = 500 × 10 = **5,000 J**
- Power = 5,000 ÷ 20 = **250 W** (0.34 hp)

## Human power output

| Activity | Typical power |
|---|---|
| Sitting at rest | about 100 W (body heat) |
| Walking | 250–350 W total metabolic |
| Climbing stairs briskly | 300–500 W useful |
| Cycling, recreational | 75–150 W at the pedals |
| Trained cyclist, 1 hour | 250–400 W |
| Sprint (seconds) | 1,000–2,000 W |

## Work done against gravity

Lifting an object at constant speed: W = m × g × h. Lifting a 20 kg box onto a 1.5 m shelf takes 20 × 9.81 × 1.5 = **294 J**. Done in 2 seconds, that is 147 W.

## Efficiency

No machine converts all its input energy into useful work:

<p class="formula">Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input × 100%</p>

| Machine | Typical efficiency |
|---|---|
| Large electric motor | 90–97% |
| Car petrol engine | 20–35% |
| Human muscle | about 20–25% |
| LED lighting | 30–50% (light vs heat) |

## Work at an angle

If the force is at an angle θ to the movement, only the part along the movement does work: W = F × d × cos θ. Pulling a suitcase with 100 N at 30° over 50 m does 100 × 50 × 0.866 = **4,330 J** of work.

## Climbing stairs

A 70 kg person climbs 3 m of stairs in 4 s: work = 70 × 9.81 × 3 = 2059 J, power = 515 W — similar to a bright old-style light bulb.

## Checking the default answer with P = F × v

The default case moves 10 m in 20 s, an average speed of 0.5 m/s. Power can also be written as force times speed: P = 500 N × 0.5 m/s = **250 W**, the same as 5,000 J ÷ 20 s. In horsepower that is 0.335 mechanical hp or 0.340 metric hp (PS). The 5,000 J of work equals 0.001389 kWh or 1.20 kcal, a reminder of how small a joule is.

The P = F × v form is handy for vehicles. A car at 25 m/s (90 km/h) pushing against 600 N of drag and rolling resistance needs 15 kW at the wheels just to hold its speed.

## Power to lift 100 kg by 10 m

Lifting 100 kg through 10 m always takes 100 × 9.80665 × 10 = 9,807 J of work; only the power changes with time:

| Time taken | Power | Horsepower |
|---|---|---|
| 60 s | 163 W | 0.22 hp |
| 30 s | 327 W | 0.44 hp |
| 10 s | 981 W | 1.32 hp |
| 5 s | 1,961 W | 2.63 hp |
| 2 s | 4,903 W | 6.58 hp |

This is how a hoist or lift motor is sized: work from the load and height, power from the required speed, then divide by the drive efficiency.

## How power is measured

Engine and motor power is measured on a dynamometer, which loads the shaft and records torque and speed (P = torque × angular speed). Cyclists use crank or pedal power meters with strain gauges, and sports labs use the stair climb test shown below. For electrical loads, a plug in energy meter reads watts directly, though that is input power, not mechanical output.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting distance that is not along the force.** Carrying a bag across a level floor does almost no work against gravity, because the lifting force is vertical and the motion is horizontal.
- **Entering mass instead of force.** A 50 kg load needs about 490 N to lift; typing 50 into the force box understates the work by a factor of 9.8.
- **Using minutes for time.** Power in watts needs time in seconds; 20 minutes is 1,200 s.
- **Mixing horsepower types.** Mechanical hp is 745.7 W, metric hp (PS) is 735.5 W, and electrical hp is 746 W.
- **Confusing W for work with W for watts.** Work is in joules; the watt is the unit of power, one joule per second.
