## How to use the impulse calculator

Enter the **average force**, the **contact time** and the **mass** of the object. You get the impulse, the change in velocity it produces and the average acceleration in g. Any field can be the unknown: enter the mass, the change in velocity and the time to find the average force, or the impulse and force to find the contact time.

## Impulse formula

<p class="formula">J = F × Δt = m × Δv = Δp &nbsp;·&nbsp; F<sub>avg</sub> = m Δv ÷ Δt &nbsp;·&nbsp; a = Δv ÷ Δt</p>

F is the average force over the contact time; the peak force in a real impact is usually 1.5–2 times higher. For a force that varies with time, impulse is the area under the force–time graph.

## Worked example

A 500 N average force acts on a 2 kg ball for 0.1 s:

- Impulse J = 500 × 0.1 = **50 N·s**
- Change in velocity Δv = 50 ÷ 2 = **25 m/s**
- Average acceleration = 25 ÷ 0.1 = 250 m/s² = **25.49 g**

## Practical notes

- **Bounces double the impulse:** impulse and momentum are vectors, so a ball that rebounds at the same speed has a change in velocity of twice its speed.
- **Drop tests:** an object dropped from height h hits the ground at √(2gh) — use the [free fall calculator](/physics/free-fall/) for the speed, then this calculator with the measured stopping time for the force.
- **Rockets:** total impulse (N·s) is the standard rating of a rocket motor; the change in velocity is the total impulse divided by the mass, ignoring gravity and the mass of propellant burnt.
- For the momentum of a moving object, use the [momentum calculator](/physics/momentum/); for collisions between two bodies, use the [elastic collision calculator](/physics/elastic-collision/).

## Peak force depends on the pulse shape

The calculator works with the average force, but sensors and structures respond to the peak. The default impulse of 50 N·s delivered over 0.1 s can arrive in different shapes:

| Pulse shape | Average force | Peak force | Peak ÷ average |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rectangular (constant force) | 500 N | 500 N | 1.00 |
| Half sine (typical elastic impact) | 500 N | 785.4 N | 1.57 |
| Triangular (sharp rise and fall) | 500 N | 1000 N | 2.00 |

All three have the same area under the force time curve, so all three give the same 25 m/s change in velocity of the 2 kg mass. Design a mount or a fixture for the peak, not the average.

## Dropping a phone: why the floor matters

A 0.2 kg phone dropped from 1.5 m lands at √(2 g h) = 5.42 m/s, so the floor must remove 1.085 N·s of momentum whatever it is made of. Only the stopping time changes:

| Surface | Stopping time | Average force | Average deceleration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ceramic tile | 2 ms | 542 N | 277 g |
| Wooden floor | 5 ms | 217 N | 111 g |
| Carpet | 15 ms | 72 N | 37 g |
| Foam cushion | 60 ms | 18 N | 9 g |

If the phone bounces off the tile at 30% of its impact speed, the impulse rises to 1.410 N·s, because the floor must also send it back up. Protective cases work by adding a few milliseconds of crush, not by absorbing momentum.

## How impulse is measured

Force plates and instrumented hammers sample a load cell or piezoelectric sensor and integrate the force over time to get the impulse directly. The sampling rate must suit the contact: a 1 kHz plate is fine for a jump lasting 0.3 s, but its 1 ms sample interval is longer than a whole 0.5 ms golf strike, so ball impact studies use sensors read at 50 kHz or more. Without a force sensor, high speed video gives the velocities before and after, and m × Δv gives the impulse.

## Common mistakes

- **Using speed instead of velocity change.** A rebound reverses the direction, so Δv is the sum of the two speeds, not their difference.
- **Reading the average force as the peak.** As the table above shows, the peak can be about twice the average for a sharp impact.
- **Entering the contact time in milliseconds as seconds.** 5 ms is 0.005 s; typing 5 underestimates the force a thousand times.
- **Leaving out gravity during a long contact.** When a person lands from a jump over 0.3 s, the floor force is m Δv ÷ Δt plus the body weight m g.
- **Mixing lbf·s and N·s.** 1 lbf·s is 4.448 N·s, so US rocket motor data must be converted before dividing by a mass in kilograms.
