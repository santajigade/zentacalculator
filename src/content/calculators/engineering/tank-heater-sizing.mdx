## How to use the tank heater sizing calculator

Enter the **volume**, **density** and **specific heat** of the liquid, the **starting and final temperatures** and the **heat-up time** you need. Add the tank's **heat loss coefficient**, **outside surface area** and the **ambient temperature** to include losses, the **heater efficiency**, and the **supply voltage and phases** to get the current. The calculator gives the heat energy, the heat-up power, the average heat loss, the **required heater power**, the power needed to hold the tank at temperature and the current. To find the heat-up time for a heater you already have, enter its kW in the required-power field and press × on the heat-up time.

## Tank heating formulas

<p class="formula">E = m c<sub>p</sub> (T<sub>2</sub> − T<sub>1</sub>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; P<sub>heat-up</sub> = E ÷ t</p>

<p class="formula">P<sub>loss</sub> = U A [(T<sub>1</sub> + T<sub>2</sub>) ÷ 2 − T<sub>a</sub>] &nbsp;·&nbsp; P = (P<sub>heat-up</sub> + P<sub>loss</sub>) ÷ η</p>

<p class="formula">P<sub>hold</sub> = U A (T<sub>2</sub> − T<sub>a</sub>) ÷ η &nbsp;·&nbsp; I = P ÷ (√3 V) three-phase, I = P ÷ V single-phase</p>

The loss during heat-up uses the average liquid temperature, a good approximation when the losses are small compared with the heater power.

## Worked example

A 2,000 L insulated water tank (U = 0.8 W/m²·K, 10 m² of surface) is heated from 15 °C to 60 °C in 3 hours by an immersion heater on a 415 V three-phase supply; the room is at 15 °C:

- Mass = 2 m³ × 1000 kg/m³ = **2,000 kg**
- Heat = 2,000 × 4.186 × (60 − 15) = 376.74 MJ = **104.65 kWh**
- Heat-up power = 104.65 kWh ÷ 3 h = **34.88 kW**
- Average loss = 0.8 × 10 × (37.5 − 15) = **0.180 kW**
- Required heater power = (34.88 + 0.180) ÷ 1 = **35.06 kW** — choose a 36 or 40 kW heater with a margin
- Holding power at 60 °C = 0.8 × 10 × (60 − 15) = **0.360 kW**
- Current = 35,063 ÷ (√3 × 415) = **48.78 A**

## Practical notes

- **Element watt density:** keep the surface loading of immersion elements low for viscous oils and sugar solutions (often below 2–3 W/cm²) to avoid coking or scorching; water heaters can run at about 8–10 W/cm².
- **Thermostat and over-temperature cut-out:** the heater should be controlled by a thermostat or a temperature controller and protected by an independent high-limit cut-out and a low-level interlock, so it never runs dry.
- **Voltage:** a resistive heater's power falls with the square of the voltage — a heater rated at 415 V gives only about 90% of its kW at 394 V.
- For flowing liquids (heat exchangers, inline heaters) use the [heat duty calculator](/engineering/heat-duty/) with the mass flow rate instead of a volume and time.

## Second example: an uninsulated fuel oil day tank

A 5,000 L bare steel tank of light fuel oil (870 kg/m³, c<sub>p</sub> 1.9 kJ/kg·K, 18 m² of surface, U about 8 W/m²·K in still air) must go from 20 °C to 70 °C in 4 hours in a 20 °C room:

- Mass = 4,350 kg; heat = 4,350 × 1.9 × 50 ÷ 3,600 = **114.8 kWh**
- Heat-up power = 114.8 ÷ 4 = **28.70 kW**
- Average loss = 8 × 18 × (45 − 20) ÷ 1,000 = **3.60 kW**
- Heater power = **32.30 kW**, drawing **44.9 A** at 415 V three-phase
- Holding at 70 °C needs **7.20 kW**

Losses are 11% of the heater rating here. Insulating the tank to U = 0.8 cuts the holding power to 0.72 kW, which matters more than the heater rating because the tank is held hot for most of the day.

## Checking a heater in service

Measure the phase currents with a clamp meter while the heater is on and compare them with the rating. A 36 kW heater at 415 V three-phase should draw about **50.1 A** per line; a much lower current on one phase points to a failed element. Low supply voltage has the same effect on all phases: at 394 V the same heater gives only 32.4 kW. With a healthy 36 kW heater the default 2,000 L tank should reach 60 °C in about **2.92 hours**; log the temperature during heat-up and compare. A slower rise means scale on the elements, a lower supply voltage or losses larger than assumed, often from an open lid.

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing for the holding load only.** The holding power is small; the heat-up duty sets the heater rating.
- **Leaving out the steel of the tank.** A heavy steel tank or a tank full of parts also has to be heated; add its mass × 0.49 kJ/kg·K to the liquid energy.
- **Forgetting evaporation from an open surface.** A hot open tank can lose more heat from the surface than through the walls; a lid or floating balls cut it sharply.
- **Using single phase current formulas on a three-phase heater.** Divide by √3 × line voltage, not by the line voltage.
- **Choosing high watt density elements for oil.** Oil carries heat away slowly, so a water heater element can coke the oil and burn out.
