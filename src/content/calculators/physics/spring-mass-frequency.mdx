## How to use the spring-mass frequency calculator

Enter the **spring constant** in N/m and the **mass**. You get the angular frequency, the natural frequency in Hz, the period of one oscillation and the static deflection of the spring under the weight. If you know the deflection instead, clear the spring constant and enter it.

## Spring-mass formulas

<p class="formula">ω = √(k ÷ m) &nbsp;·&nbsp; f = ω ÷ 2π &nbsp;·&nbsp; T = 1 ÷ f</p>

<p class="formula">δ = m g ÷ k &nbsp;⇒&nbsp; f = (1/2π) √(g ÷ δ)</p>

## Worked example

A 5 kg mass on a 2,000 N/m spring (g = 9.80665 m/s²):

- ω = √(2000 ÷ 5) = **20 rad/s**
- f = 20 ÷ 2π = **3.183 Hz**
- T = **0.3142 s**
- Static deflection = 5 × 9.807 ÷ 2000 = **24.5 mm**

## Natural frequency from static deflection

| Static deflection | Natural frequency |
|---|---|
| 1 mm | 15.76 Hz |
| 2.5 mm | 9.97 Hz |
| 5 mm | 7.05 Hz |
| 10 mm | 4.98 Hz |
| 25 mm | 3.15 Hz |
| 50 mm | 2.23 Hz |
| 100 mm | 1.58 Hz |

## Notes

- Springs in **series** combine as 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂; in **parallel**, k = k₁ + k₂.
- Damping lowers the observed frequency slightly: ω<sub>d</sub> = ω √(1 − ζ²).
- See the [Hooke's law calculator](/physics/hookes-law/) to find k from a force and extension.

Check from the deflection alone: f = (1 ÷ 2π) √(9.80665 ÷ 0.02452) = **3.183 Hz**, the same answer, which is why mount catalogues often quote deflection instead of stiffness.

Because f depends on √(k ÷ m), doubling the mass to 10 kg lowers the frequency only to 2.251 Hz, and it takes four times the stiffness (8,000 N/m) to double it to 6.366 Hz. Two of these springs in series give 1,000 N/m and 2.251 Hz.

## Vibration isolation with this spring

For an undamped mount the transmissibility is 1 ÷ |1 − r²|, where r is the forcing frequency divided by the natural frequency (3.183 Hz here):

| Ratio r | Forcing frequency | Transmissibility | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 1.59 Hz | 1.33 | amplified |
| 1.2 | 3.82 Hz | 2.27 | amplified |
| 1.414 | 4.50 Hz | 1.00 | no change |
| 2 | 6.37 Hz | 0.33 | isolated |
| 3 | 9.55 Hz | 0.12 | isolated |
| 5 | 15.92 Hz | 0.04 | isolated |

Near r = 1 the response grows without limit in this ideal case; real mounts have damping that caps it, typically at 5 to 15 times. Isolation only starts above r = √2.

## Sizing a mount in practice

A fan running at 1,500 rpm (25 Hz) needs r = 3, so the target natural frequency is 25 ÷ 3 = 8.33 Hz. Rearranging f = (1/2π)√(g/δ), the mounts must deflect δ = g ÷ (2π f)² = **3.58 mm** under the machine weight. Pick a mount whose rated load gives about that deflection, and divide the weight evenly between mounts.

To measure k on the bench, hang known weights and record the extension, or tap the loaded system and count oscillations with a phone accelerometer app; the measured frequency then gives k = m (2π f)².

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing ω and f.** ω = 20 rad/s is not 20 Hz; f is ω ÷ 2π, here 3.183 Hz.
- **Entering the weight in newtons as the mass.** 5 kg weighs 49.03 N; using 49 as the mass lowers the frequency by a factor of about 3.1.
- **Ignoring the spring's own mass.** Add one third of the spring mass to m; a 0.6 kg spring moves this example to 3.121 Hz.
- **Choosing a mount that is too stiff.** Any forcing ratio below √2 amplifies vibration instead of isolating it, and the worst case is close to r = 1.
- **Using the catalogue stiffness of rubber mounts for dynamic work.** Rubber is stiffer under vibration than in a static test, so the real natural frequency is often 10 to 40% higher.
