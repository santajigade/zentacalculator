## How to use the cell doubling time calculator

Enter the **initial** and **final cell counts** and the **time** between them. The number of doublings and the doubling time are calculated.

## Cell doubling time formulas

<p class="formula">doublings = log₂(N ÷ N₀)   T<sub>d</sub> = t ÷ doublings</p>

## Example

Cells grow from 100,000 to 800,000 in 72 hours:

- Doublings = log₂(8) = **3**
- Doubling time = 72 ÷ 3 = **24 hours**

## Typical doubling times of cell lines

| Cell line | Approximate doubling time |
|---|---|
| HEK293 | 24–36 h |
| HeLa | about 24 h |
| CHO | 14–24 h |
| Jurkat | 25–50 h |
| Primary human fibroblasts | 24–72 h, slows with passages |
| Mouse embryonic stem cells | 12–16 h |

Doubling time depends on medium, serum, confluence and passage number.

## Population doubling level (PDL)

For primary cells, track the cumulative population doublings across passages. Normal human fibroblasts typically stop dividing after about 40–60 doublings (the Hayflick limit).

## Planning experiments

To have 5 million cells from 500,000 with a 24-hour doubling time: log₂(10) = 3.32 doublings, so about **80 hours**. Add time for the lag phase after seeding.

## Tips for accurate counts

- Count in the exponential phase, not when cells are confluent.
- Use the same counting method each time — see the [hemocytometer calculator](/biology/hemocytometer/).
- Exclude dead cells with trypan blue.

## Planning how many cells to seed

Work backwards from the number you need:

<p class="formula">N<sub>seed</sub> = N<sub>target</sub> ÷ 2<sup>t ÷ T<sub>d</sub></sup></p>

To have 2 × 10⁶ cells in 48 hours with a 24 hour doubling time, seed 2 × 10⁶ ÷ 2² = **5 × 10⁵ cells**. Most lines show a lag of 12 to 24 hours after passaging, so add that to the time or seed a little more.

## Growth rate from doubling time

Doubling time and the specific growth rate μ are two views of the same exponential curve: μ = ln 2 ÷ T<sub>d</sub>. The default result of 24 h is a growth rate of **0.0289 per hour** (0.693 per day). The table also shows how many times one cell would multiply in a week of unchecked growth, which explains why cultures must be split long before that.

| Doubling time | μ (per hour) | μ (per day) | Fold increase in 7 days |

## Worked example: fitting more than two counts

A culture is counted every 24 hours: 100,000, 185,000, 410,000, 760,000 cells at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h. Using only the first and last counts gives 72 ÷ log₂(7.6) = **24.6 h**. A least-squares line through ln(count) against time has a slope of 0.02867 per hour, so T<sub>d</sub> = 0.6931 ÷ 0.02867 = **24.2 h**. The fit uses all four counts, so one noisy count moves it less. Plot ln(count) against time first: a straight line confirms exponential growth, while a curve flattening at the end shows the culture is reaching confluence.

## Tracking population doublings across passages

When cells are split at a fixed ratio and allowed to regrow to the same density, each passage adds log₂(split ratio) doublings. A 1:4 split adds 2 doublings and a 1:3 split adds 1.58. Five passages at 1:4 therefore add **10 population doublings** to the culture's PDL. Recording this alongside passage number matters for primary cells, which slow down and stop as they approach their limit.

## How the counts are made

Viable cells are usually counted after trypsinising a sample flask, either with a hemocytometer and trypan blue or with an automated counter that images stained cells. Count replicate flasks rather than one, mix the suspension well before loading, and keep the counting method the same at every time point. For bacteria, optical density at 600 nm or colony counts on plates take the place of direct counts.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting during the lag phase.** Counts in the first day after seeding make the doubling time look too long. Start timing once growth is steady.
- **Including dead cells.** Count only viable cells, for example with trypan blue, or the growth rate will be wrong.
- **Letting cultures reach confluence.** Growth slows as cells fill the surface, so the last count gives a falsely long doubling time.
- **Using only two time points.** Count at three or more times and fit a line to ln(count) against time for a more reliable result.
- **Mixing up doubling time and growth rate.** A higher growth rate means a shorter doubling time; convert with μ = 0.693 ÷ T<sub>d</sub> before comparing values from different papers.
