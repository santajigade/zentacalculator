## How to use the battery life calculator

Enter the battery **capacity** (mAh), the device's **average current** and a **usable capacity factor**. The estimated runtime is calculated.

## Battery life formula

<p class="formula">runtime = capacity × factor ÷ current</p>

## Worked example with the default values

A **2,000 mAh** battery powers a device that draws **50 mA** on average, and **85%** of the rated capacity is usable before the device cuts out:

- Usable capacity = 2,000 × 0.85 = 1,700 mAh
- Runtime = 1,700 ÷ 50 = **34 hours** (about 1.4 days)

Without the 85% factor the same battery would be quoted at 40 hours, which few real batteries achieve.

## Sleep mode makes the difference

For microcontroller projects, average current is what matters:

<p class="formula">I<sub>avg</sub> = (I<sub>active</sub> × t<sub>active</sub> + I<sub>sleep</sub> × t<sub>sleep</sub>) ÷ total time</p>

A sensor drawing 20 mA for 1 second every minute and 10 µA asleep averages (20 × 1 + 0.01 × 59) ÷ 60 = **0.343 mA**. With 85% usable capacity a 2,000 mAh battery then lasts about 4,954 h, roughly **6.8 months**, instead of 85 h (3.5 days) if it stayed awake.

## mAh vs Wh

mAh ignores voltage. To compare batteries of different voltages, use watt-hours: Wh = mAh × V ÷ 1,000. A 5,000 mAh phone battery at 3.85 V holds 19.25 Wh.

## Power banks

A 10,000 mAh power bank is rated at its internal cell voltage of 3.7 V, which is 37 Wh. Boosting to 5 V for USB and then charging the phone both lose energy, so a phone typically receives about 60 to 70 percent of the rated mAh, or 6,000 to 7,000 mAh.

## Runtime of a 2,000 mAh battery at different loads

Usable capacity 85%, constant current:

| Average current | Runtime | Days |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 mA | 17,000 h | 708.3 days |
| 1 mA | 1,700 h | 70.8 days |
| 10 mA | 170 h | 7.1 days |
| 50 mA | 34 h | 1.4 days |
| 100 mA | 17 h | 0.7 days |
| 250 mA | 6.8 h | 0.3 days |
| 500 mA | 3.4 h | 0.1 days |

At high currents the real figure is lower again, because alkaline and NiMH cells deliver less of their rated capacity when discharged fast. At very low currents, self discharge takes over: a CR2032 coin cell of 225 mAh at 10 µA would last 19,125 h, or 2.2 years, on paper, but lithium coin cells lose around 1% a year and seals age, so plan for less.

## How to measure the average current

A multimeter on its mA range in series with the battery reads steady loads well. Devices that sleep and wake in bursts need a current logger, a shunt resistor with an oscilloscope, or a power profiler that integrates charge over a full cycle. Measure at least one complete wake and sleep cycle, including radio transmissions, which are usually the largest pulses.

## Practical notes

- Use the **cut-off voltage** of the device to choose the usable factor. A device that stops at 1.3 V per AA cell leaves much more charge unused than one that runs down to 0.9 V.
- **Cold reduces capacity.** Alkaline cells near 0 °C can deliver half their room temperature capacity; Li-ion and lithium primary cells fare better.
- **Ageing matters for rechargeables.** A Li-ion pack after a few hundred cycles may hold 80% of its new capacity, so lower the factor for an old pack.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing mAh across voltages.** A 3.7 V 3,000 mAh cell holds about 2.5 times the energy of a 1.5 V 3,000 mAh cell; compare in Wh.
- **Using peak current instead of average current.** A radio that draws 120 mA for a few milliseconds per minute averages far less.
- **Forgetting regulator losses.** A linear regulator draws the full load current from the battery whatever the output voltage, and its quiescent current adds to sleep current.
- **Assuming the full rated capacity is usable.** Ratings are measured at low current and room temperature down to a low cut-off voltage.
- **Ignoring self discharge on multi year designs.** It can use more charge than the device itself in very low power sensors.
