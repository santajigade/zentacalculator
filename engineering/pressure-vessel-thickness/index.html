    Internal pressure loads a cylindrical shell with a hoop stress twice the longitudinal stress, so the circumferential formula nearly always sets the wall thickness. This pressure vessel thickness calculator applies the ASME VIII Division 1 formulas for the shell and for hemispherical, ellipsoidal and torispherical heads.

    Mechanical engineers use it for early sizing and plate ordering, and inspection engineers use it to check the minimum required thickness and MAWP of a vessel in service.

    How to use the pressure vessel thickness calculator

    Enter the design pressure (gauge), the inside diameter of the new vessel, the allowable stress of the plate material at the design temperature, the weld joint efficiency and the corrosion allowance. The calculator gives the shell thickness for the hoop and longitudinal stresses, the minimum shell thickness including corrosion allowance, and the thickness of hemispherical, 2:1 ellipsoidal and torispherical heads. Enter the plate thickness you intend to use to see its MAWP. All formulas use the corroded inside radius, R = D/2 + CA.

    Pressure vessel thickness formulas (ASME VIII Div 1)

    Shell, hoop stress (UG-27): t = P R ÷ (S E − 0.6 P)  ·  longitudinal: t = P R ÷ (2 S E + 0.4 P)

    Hemispherical head (UG-32): t = P R ÷ (2 S E − 0.2 P)  ·  2:1 ellipsoidal: t = P D ÷ (2 S E − 0.2 P)

    Torispherical head (crown radius = D, knuckle 6%): t = 0.885 P D ÷ (S E − 0.1 P)  ·  MAWP = S E t ÷ (R + 0.6 t)

    The shell formulas are valid when t ≤ R/2 and P ≤ 0.385 S E. The head formulas give the thickness after forming — order heads with extra thickness for thinning in the knuckle.

    Worked example

    A vessel with a 1,500 mm inside diameter for 1 MPa (10 bar g), SA-516 Gr 70 plate (S = 138 MPa), spot-radiographed joints (E = 0.85) and 3 mm corrosion allowance:

    • Corroded inside radius R = 750 + 3 = 753 mm
    • Hoop stress: t = 1 × 753 ÷ (138 × 0.85 − 0.6 × 1) = 6.45 mm
    • Longitudinal stress: t = 1 × 753 ÷ (2 × 138 × 0.85 + 0.4 × 1) = 3.20 mm
    • Minimum shell thickness = 6.45 + 3 = 9.45 mm, so a 10 mm plate is chosen
    • MAWP of the 10 mm plate (corroded, 7 mm) = 138 × 0.85 × 7 ÷ (753 + 0.6 × 7) = 1.0844 MPa (10.84 bar g)
    • Pressure ratio P ÷ (S E) = 0.0085 — well within the thin-wall limit

    Heads for the same vessel (including the 3 mm corrosion allowance):

    Component Minimum thickness
    Cylindrical shell 9.45 mm
    Hemispherical head 6.21 mm
    2:1 ellipsoidal head 9.42 mm
    Torispherical head (6% knuckle) 14.37 mm

    Practical notes

    • Mill tolerance: plate is usually ordered to the nominal thickness, but pipe used as a shell can be 12.5% thinner — subtract the under-tolerance before checking MAWP.
    • Minimum thickness: UG-16 sets an absolute minimum of 1.5 mm (1/16 in) for shells and heads, excluding corrosion allowance; many owners specify 6 mm or more for fabrication and handling.
    • External pressure (vacuum), wind, seismic, nozzle loads and supports often need more thickness than internal pressure — check them separately (UG-28 for vacuum).
    • After fabrication the vessel is proved with a hydrostatic test at about 1.3 × MAWP.
    • This calculator is a design aid for preliminary sizing; a code vessel must be designed and stamped by a qualified engineer using the current edition of the code.

    Effect of weld joint efficiency

    The joint efficiency comes from the extent of radiography on the welds. For the default vessel (1,500 mm, 1 MPa, S = 138 MPa, 3 mm corrosion allowance, 10 mm plate):

    E Inspection of the long seam Minimum shell thickness MAWP of 10 mm plate
    1.00 Full radiography or seamless 8.48 mm 1.276 MPa (12.76 bar g)
    0.85 Spot radiography 9.45 mm 1.084 MPa (10.84 bar g)
    0.70 No radiography 10.84 mm 0.893 MPa (8.93 bar g)

    Full radiography costs more in the shop but saves plate on every course of a large vessel, and it also raises the MAWP of a given plate by about 18% compared with spot radiography.

    Static head in a tall vessel

    The design pressure at any course is the pressure at the top plus the liquid head above that course. A vertical vessel with the same 1 MPa at the top and 10 m of water above the bottom course sees an extra 1000 × 9.80665 × 10 = 0.0981 MPa there, so the bottom shell course needs:

    • P = 1 + 0.0981 = 1.0981 MPa
    • t = 1.0981 × 753 ÷ (138 × 0.85 − 0.6 × 1.0981) = 7.09 mm, plus 3 mm = 10.09 mm

    That is above the 10 mm plate that suits the top of the vessel, which is why tall columns and storage vessels often step up in thickness towards the bottom.

    Thickness checks in service

    Inspectors measure the wall with an ultrasonic thickness gauge on a grid of fixed points, then compare each reading with the minimum required thickness without corrosion allowance, here 6.45 mm. Suppose the 10 mm shell reads 9.2 mm after 8 years. The corrosion rate is (10 − 9.2) ÷ 8 = 0.10 mm per year, and the remaining life is (9.2 − 6.45) ÷ 0.10 = 27.5 years. Inspection intervals are usually set at half the remaining life or less, and local pitting is assessed separately from general wall loss.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the new radius. The formulas use the corroded inside radius, D/2 + CA, so the result stays valid at the end of the corrosion life.
    • Taking E from the wrong seam. Hoop stress acts across the longitudinal seams, so the hoop formula uses the joint efficiency of the long seam; the circumferential seams govern only the longitudinal stress formula.
    • Leaving out static head. On tall liquid filled vessels the bottom course can need noticeably more thickness than the top, as shown above.
    • Reading the allowable stress at room temperature. S falls as the design metal temperature rises, so take it from Section II Part D at the design temperature, not the ambient value.
    • Rounding the plate down. Always pick the next standard plate at or above the required thickness, after allowing for mill under-tolerance and forming thinning on heads.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do you calculate the thickness of a pressure vessel shell?

    For a cylindrical shell under internal pressure, ASME VIII-1 UG-27 gives t = P R ÷ (S E − 0.6 P) for the circumferential (hoop) stress and t = P R ÷ (2 S E + 0.4 P) for the longitudinal stress, where R is the inside radius in the corroded condition. Use the larger value and add the corrosion allowance.

    What is weld joint efficiency?

    A factor E from ASME Table UW-12 that reduces the allowable stress to reflect the quality of weld inspection. A double-welded butt joint is 1.0 with full radiography, 0.85 with spot radiography and 0.70 with none. Seamless parts use E = 1.

    Why is a 2:1 ellipsoidal head about as thick as the shell?

    Its formula t = P D ÷ (2 S E − 0.2 P) uses the diameter instead of the radius, so the result is almost the same as the shell's hoop-stress thickness. A hemispherical head needs only about half the shell thickness, and a standard torispherical head about 1.77 times.

    What is MAWP?

    The maximum allowable working pressure is the highest gauge pressure the weakest part of the vessel can take at its design temperature, in the corroded condition. For the shell it is MAWP = S E t ÷ (R + 0.6 t), with t the nominal thickness minus corrosion allowance (and minus any mill under-tolerance).

    How do I estimate the remaining life of a corroded vessel shell?

    Divide the measured wall loss by the years in service to get the corrosion rate, then divide the margin above the required thickness by that rate. A 10 mm shell reading 9.2 mm after 8 years with a required 6.45 mm has about 27 years left.

    Should liquid static head be added to the design pressure?

    Yes, at each course you check. 10 m of water adds about 0.098 MPa, so the bottom course of a tall vessel needs more thickness than the top course at the same top pressure.
