## How to use the orifice plate flow calculator

Enter the **pipe inner diameter**, **orifice bore**, **discharge coefficient**, **differential pressure** and **liquid density** to get the flow rate. To size a DP transmitter range, press × on Differential pressure and type the maximum flow.

## The formula (liquids)

<p class="formula">Q = C<sub>d</sub> × A<sub>o</sub> × √(2ΔP ÷ (ρ(1 − β⁴)))</p>

- **A<sub>o</sub>** = π d² ÷ 4 — orifice bore area
- **β** = d ÷ D — beta ratio
- **C<sub>d</sub>** — discharge coefficient (about 0.61 for sharp-edged plates)

## Worked example

Water (998 kg/m³) in a 100 mm pipe with a 50 mm bore (β = 0.5), C<sub>d</sub> = 0.61, ΔP = 250 mbar:

- A<sub>o</sub> = 0.001963 m²
- 1 − β⁴ = 0.9375
- Q = 0.61 × 0.001963 × √(2 × 25,000 ÷ (998 × 0.9375)) = 0.00876 m³/s = **31.5 m³/h**

## Beta ratio effects

| β (d/D) | 1 − β⁴ | Velocity of approach factor 1/√(1 − β⁴) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 0.9984 | 1.0008 |
| 0.3 | 0.9919 | 1.0041 |
| 0.4 | 0.9744 | 1.0131 |
| 0.5 | 0.9375 | 1.0328 |
| 0.6 | 0.8704 | 1.0719 |
| 0.7 | 0.7599 | 1.1472 |
| 0.75 | 0.6836 | 1.2095 |

ISO 5167-2 limits β to between 0.10 and 0.75 for standard orifice plates.

## Permanent pressure loss

An orifice plate recovers only part of the differential pressure downstream. The permanent loss is roughly:

<p class="formula">Δω ≈ (1 − β<sup>1.9</sup>) × ΔP</p>

At β = 0.5, about 73% of the measured DP is lost permanently, which costs pumping energy. Venturi tubes recover far more.

## Straight-run requirements

Orifice plates need undisturbed flow. Typical upstream straight lengths are 10–40 pipe diameters depending on the fittings and β ratio, plus about 5–8 diameters downstream. ISO 5167 tables give exact values; flow conditioners can shorten them.

## Tapping arrangements

| Taps | Location |
|---|---|
| Flange taps | 25.4 mm (1 in) each side of the plate |
| Corner taps | At the plate faces |
| D and D/2 taps | 1 D upstream, 0.5 D downstream |

The discharge coefficient depends on the tapping type, so use the matching correlation (Reader-Harris/Gallagher in ISO 5167).

## Good practice

- Keep β between about **0.2 and 0.75**.
- Provide enough **straight pipe** upstream (often 10–40 D depending on fittings) and downstream (about 5 D).
- Turndown is limited to about **3:1 to 4:1** with one DP transmitter, because flow follows the square root of DP. See the [DP square root calculator](/engineering/dp-flow-square-root/).
- For gases and steam, an expansibility factor is also needed.

## Example: setting the transmitter range

With the default plate (50 mm bore in a 100 mm pipe, β = 0.5, C<sub>d</sub> = 0.61) and water at 998 kg/m³, 250 mbar gives **31.52 m³/h**. Suppose the line must read up to 40 m³/h. Because flow follows √ΔP, the DP at maximum flow is 250 × (40 ÷ 31.52)² = **403 mbar**, so a 0 to 403 mbar transmitter range (or the next round value above it) suits this plate.

At the default flow the pipe Reynolds number is about 111,037, comfortably in the range where a fixed C<sub>d</sub> of 0.61 is reasonable. The permanent loss at 250 mbar is about (1 − 0.5<sup>1.9</sup>) × 250 = **183 mbar**.

## Square root relationship for this plate

| DP (% of range) | DP | Flow (% of max) | Flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1% | 4.0 mbar | 10.0% | 4.0 m³/h |
| 4% | 16.1 mbar | 20.0% | 8.0 m³/h |
| 10% | 40.3 mbar | 31.6% | 12.6 m³/h |
| 25% | 100.6 mbar | 50.0% | 20.0 m³/h |
| 50% | 201.3 mbar | 70.7% | 28.3 m³/h |
| 100% | 402.6 mbar | 100.0% | 40.0 m³/h |

The bottom of the table shows the turndown problem: the first 10% of the DP range covers almost a third of the flow, so small DP errors near zero become large flow errors. Most systems apply a low flow cutoff near 5 to 10% of flow.

## How it is installed and read

The plate sits between flanges with its sharp, square edge facing upstream and the bevel downstream. Impulse lines run from the taps to a DP transmitter through a three-valve or five-valve manifold, sloped so that gas vents back to the pipe on liquid service. The square root is extracted once, either in the transmitter or in the control system, and the zero is checked by opening the equalising valve.

## Common mistakes

- **Taking the square root twice.** If the transmitter output is already linear in flow, the DCS input must be set to linear, or the reading is badly low across the range.
- **Fitting the plate backwards.** With the bevel facing upstream the discharge coefficient rises and the meter reads several percent low.
- **Gas trapped in liquid impulse lines.** A bubble in one leg adds a false head and shifts the zero.
- **Using design density at a different temperature.** Hot water at 90 °C is about 965 kg/m³, which changes the flow by about 1.7% for the same DP.
- **Ignoring a worn or dirty edge.** A rounded edge or deposits on the upstream face raise C<sub>d</sub>, so an old plate reads low.
