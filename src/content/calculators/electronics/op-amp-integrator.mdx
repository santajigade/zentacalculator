## How to use the op-amp integrator calculator

Enter the **input voltage**, the **input resistor** and the **feedback capacitor**. Add an **integration time** to see where the ramp has reached, and an **initial output voltage** if the capacitor is not discharged. The **signal frequency** gives the AC gain for sine-wave inputs.

## Op-amp integrator formula

<p class="formula">V<sub>out</sub>(t) = V₀ − (1/RC) ∫ V<sub>in</sub> dt &nbsp;·&nbsp; constant input: V<sub>out</sub> = V₀ − V<sub>in</sub> t / RC</p>

<p class="formula">|G| = 1 / (2π f R C) &nbsp;·&nbsp; f₀ = 1 / (2π R C)</p>

## Worked example

1 V input, R = 10 kΩ, C = 100 nF (RC = 1 ms):

- Ramp rate = −1 ÷ 0.001 = **-1,000 V/s**
- After 1 ms the output has moved to **-1.00 V**
- Unity-gain frequency f₀ = **159.15 Hz**; at 1 kHz the gain is **0.1592**

## Design notes

- The output can only ramp until it reaches the op-amp's output swing limit; reset the capacitor with a switch for repeating ramps.
- Use a low-leakage film (C0G, polypropylene) capacitor — electrolytics leak and drift.
- For a square-wave input the integrator produces a triangle wave of peak-to-peak amplitude V<sub>in</sub> T/(2RC) for a symmetric ±V<sub>in</sub> square of period T.

## How long until the output saturates

On ±15 V supplies a typical op-amp swings to about ±13 V. At the default ramp of 1,000 V/s the output reaches −13 V after 13 ÷ 1,000 = **13 ms**, and then simply sits at the rail. Any integrator with a steady input must be reset, or placed inside a feedback loop that drives its input back to zero, as in a PID controller.

## Errors that integrate too

The circuit cannot tell signal from error. An input offset voltage of 1 mV integrates at 0.001 ÷ 1 ms = **1 V/s**, reaching the rail in about 13 s with no input at all. A bias current of 100 nA, typical of older bipolar op-amps, charges the 100 nF capacitor at I ÷ C = **1 V/s**. A FET-input op-amp with picoamp bias removes the second error, and a resistor equal to R on the non-inverting input cancels much of it for bipolar parts.

Adding R<sub>f</sub> = 1 MΩ across the capacitor limits the DC gain to −100, so the 1 mV offset now produces only about 101 mV at the output. The price is that the circuit integrates only above 1.59 Hz.

## Square wave in, triangle out

Feed a ±1 V, 1 kHz square wave. Each half period of 0.5 ms changes the output by 1 × 0.0005 ÷ 0.001 = **0.5 V peak to peak**, a triangle wave. Doubling the frequency halves the amplitude, the same 20 dB per decade slope as the sine response.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the inversion.** A positive input gives a falling output; follow with an inverting stage if a rising ramp is needed.
- **Leaving out a DC path.** Without R<sub>f</sub> or a reset switch, offset and bias current drift the output to a rail.
- **Using ceramic X7R or electrolytic capacitors.** Leakage and dielectric absorption bend the ramp and leave residual voltage after reset; polypropylene or C0G parts are better.
- **Mixing time units.** The time input is in milliseconds, so 1 means 0.001 s.
- **Ignoring the initial condition.** A capacitor not discharged at the start shifts the whole ramp by V₀.
