faq:
  - q: How do I convert a z-score to a p-value?
    a: Use the standard normal cumulative distribution Φ. Left-tailed p = Φ(z), right-tailed p = 1 − Φ(z), and two-tailed p = 2 × (1 − Φ(|z|)).
  - q: What is the p-value for z = 1.96?
    a: The two-tailed p-value is 0.0500 and the one-tailed (right) p-value is 0.0250 — which is why 1.96 is the critical value for a 5% two-sided test.
  - q: Should I use a one-tailed or two-tailed p-value?
    a: Use two-tailed unless, before seeing the data, you had a directional hypothesis (for example "the new process is faster") and a result in the other direction would be treated as no effect. Most published tests are two-tailed.
  - q: When can I use the z distribution instead of t?
    a: When the population standard deviation is known, or for large samples (roughly n > 30) and for proportions with enough successes and failures. For small samples with an estimated standard deviation, use the t distribution, which gives slightly larger p-values.

## How to use the p-value from z-score calculator

Enter the **z-score** from your test, for example z = (x̄ − μ₀) ÷ (σ/√n) for a one-sample z test or the z statistic for a difference in proportions. The calculator returns the left-tailed, right-tailed and two-tailed p-values and colours the two-tailed result by significance level. To find the z-score for a given p-value, enter the p-value and press × on z.

## P-value formulas

<p class="formula">p<sub>left</sub> = Φ(z) &nbsp;·&nbsp; p<sub>right</sub> = 1 − Φ(z) &nbsp;·&nbsp; p<sub>two</sub> = 2[1 − Φ(|z|)]</p>

Φ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

## Worked example

A z test gives z = 2.1:

- Left-tailed p = Φ(2.1) = **0.982136**
- Right-tailed p = 1 − 0.982136 = **0.017864**
- Two-tailed p = 2 × 0.017864 = **0.035729** — significant at the 5% level (p < 0.05) but not at 1%

## Interpreting p-values

- A p-value is **not** the probability that the null hypothesis is true; it is the probability of data at least this extreme if it were true.
- "Not significant" does not prove no effect — the test may simply lack power. Report the effect size and a confidence interval with the p-value.
- Running many tests inflates false positives; with 20 independent tests at α = 0.05 you expect one "significant" result by chance (consider a Bonferroni correction, α ÷ m).
