## How to use the Doppler effect calculator

Enter the **source frequency**, the **speed of sound** (343 m/s in air at 20 °C) and the speeds of the **observer** and **source**. Use positive speeds when they move **towards** each other and negative speeds when they move **apart**. The calculator gives the frequency heard and the shift as a percentage of the source frequency.

## Doppler effect formula

<p class="formula">f′ = f × (v + v<sub>o</sub>) ÷ (v − v<sub>s</sub>)</p>

Speeds are measured relative to the air. Wind changes the effective speeds, and the formula holds only while the source is slower than sound.

## Worked example

An ambulance siren at 700 Hz approaches a standing observer at 72 km/h (20 m/s), with the speed of sound 343 m/s:

- Approaching: f′ = 700 × 343 ÷ (343 − 20) = **743.3 Hz**, a shift of **+6.19%**
- Moving away: f′ = 700 × 343 ÷ (343 + 20) = **661.4 Hz**
- The pitch drops by 81.9 Hz as it passes — about 2.0 semitones, roughly a whole tone

## Moving observer versus moving source

With the default 700 Hz source and 343 m/s, the calculator gives **743.3 Hz** and a shift of **+6.19%** for a source approaching at 72 km/h. If instead the source stands still and the listener drives towards it at 72 km/h, f′ = 700 × (343 + 20) ÷ 343 = **740.8 Hz**. The two are not equal: a moving source changes the wavelength in the air, while a moving observer only meets the unchanged waves more often. If both approach each other at 72 km/h, f′ = 700 × 363 ÷ 323 = **786.7 Hz**.

| Speed | Source moving | Observer moving | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 36 km/h (10 m/s) | 721.0 Hz | 720.4 Hz | 0.6 Hz |
| 72 km/h (20 m/s) | 743.3 Hz | 740.8 Hz | 2.5 Hz |
| 144 km/h (40 m/s) | 792.4 Hz | 781.6 Hz | 10.8 Hz |
| 288 km/h (80 m/s) | 912.9 Hz | 863.3 Hz | 49.7 Hz |

The gap grows quickly with speed, because the source term v ÷ (v − v<sub>s</sub>) blows up as v<sub>s</sub> nears the speed of sound while the observer term grows only in a straight line.

## Working out a speed from a recording

Record a vehicle passing, read the steady pitch before and after it goes by, and solve the two Doppler equations together: v<sub>s</sub> = v × (f<sub>a</sub> − f<sub>r</sub>) ÷ (f<sub>a</sub> + f<sub>r</sub>). For the default siren, 743.3 Hz approaching and 661.4 Hz receding give 343 × 81.9 ÷ 1404.8 = **20.0 m/s**, which is 72 km/h. The nice feature is that you do not need to know the siren's true frequency. Temperature still matters: on a 0 °C day, with sound at 331.3 m/s, the same siren approaching at 72 km/h sounds at 745.0 Hz.

## Doppler ultrasound

Medical and industrial ultrasound measures a reflected beam, so the shift is doubled and only the speed along the beam counts: Δf = 2 f v cos θ ÷ c. A 5 MHz probe looking at blood moving at 0.5 m/s, with a 60° angle between beam and flow and c = 1,540 m/s in tissue, sees a shift of **1623 Hz**, conveniently in the audible range, which is why Doppler machines can play blood flow as sound. If the operator sets the angle as 65° instead of 60°, the machine reports 0.59 m/s instead of 0.50 m/s, so the angle setting matters a lot above about 60°.

## Common mistakes

- **Getting the sign convention backwards.** In this calculator positive speeds mean moving towards the other party; a receding source needs a negative v<sub>s</sub>.
- **Mixing km/h and m/s.** The speed of sound is in m/s while the vehicle speeds are in km/h; 72 km/h is 20 m/s, not 72 m/s.
- **Swapping the source and observer formulas.** As the table shows, the two cases give different answers at high speed.
- **Ignoring the angle.** Only the part of the velocity along the line to the listener counts. A car passing 50 m away on a straight road gives a smooth slide in pitch, and no shift at all at the moment it is closest.
- **Forgetting wind.** The speeds must be measured relative to the air, so a steady wind shifts both v<sub>o</sub> and v<sub>s</sub>.

## Practical notes

- **Radar and laser speed guns** use the Doppler shift of reflected waves; for a reflection the shift is doubled (the target acts as a moving observer and then a moving source).
- **Doppler ultrasonic flow meters** measure the shift of sound reflected by bubbles or particles in the liquid, so they need some suspended solids or gas; clean liquids need a transit-time meter.
- **Medical ultrasound** uses the same principle to measure blood flow velocity.
- At or above the speed of sound the denominator becomes zero or negative: the waves pile up into a shock wave, heard as a sonic boom.
