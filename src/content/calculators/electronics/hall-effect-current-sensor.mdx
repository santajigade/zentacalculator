## How to use the Hall-effect current sensor calculator

Enter the sensor's **zero-current output** (Vcc/2 for ratiometric sensors such as the ACS712), its **sensitivity** in mV/A, the **current** and your ADC's **reference voltage** and **resolution**. You get the sensor output voltage, the ADC reading you should expect and the current represented by one ADC count. To go from a measured voltage to current, enter the voltage in the output field and leave the current blank. Negative current gives an output below the offset.

## Hall-effect current sensor formulas

<p class="formula">V<sub>out</sub> = V<sub>q</sub> + S × I &nbsp;·&nbsp; I = (V<sub>out</sub> − V<sub>q</sub>) ÷ S</p>

<p class="formula">ADC counts = V<sub>out</sub> ÷ V<sub>ref</sub> × 2<sup>N</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; resolution = V<sub>ref</sub> ÷ (2<sup>N</sup> × S)</p>

## Worked example

ACS712 ±5 A (185 mV/A) on a 5 V supply, measuring 2 A with a 10-bit ADC and a 5 V reference:

- V<sub>out</sub> = 2.5 + 0.185 × 2 = **2.87 V**
- ADC reading = 2.87 ÷ 5 × 1024 = **587.8** counts
- One count = 5 ÷ (1024 × 0.185) = **26.4 mA**

## Practical notes

- Hall sensors are **isolated** — the current path is separate from the signal side — which makes them safer than a [shunt resistor](/electronics/shunt-resistor/) on high-side or mains circuits. Read how a [Hall-effect sensor works](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/how-a-hall-effect-sensor-works/).
- Zero the offset in firmware at start-up with no current flowing; offset drift with temperature is often the largest error at low current.
- Keep strong magnetic fields and high-current wires away from the sensor package; they shift the reading.
- For AC current, sample fast and compute RMS from the samples after subtracting the offset.

## Second example: reverse and AC current

With the default 2.5 V offset, 185 mV/A, 5 V reference and 10 bits, a current of **−3 A** gives V<sub>out</sub> = 2.5 − 0.185 × 3 = **1.945 V**, which the ADC reads as 1.945 ÷ 5 × 1,024 = **398.3 counts**. The sensor is bidirectional, so anything below 512 counts is current flowing the other way.

For an AC load of 2 A RMS, the peak current is 2 × √2 = 2.828 A, so the output swings between **1.977 V** and **3.023 V** around the offset. Subtract the offset from every sample before squaring; computing RMS on the raw voltage gives a figure dominated by the 2.5 V DC level.

## Why ratiometric output matters

The ACS712 offset and sensitivity both scale with its supply. If a USB rail sags to 4.8 V, the offset becomes 2.40 V and the sensitivity 177.6 mV/A, so 2 A gives 2.7552 V. Firmware that converts with a fixed 2.5 V and 185 mV/A would report **1.38 A**. When the ADC reference is the same 4.8 V rail, the reading stays at **587.8 counts** and converts back to exactly 2.00 A, because both sides scaled together. Reference the ADC to the sensor supply, or measure that supply and correct for it.

A higher sensitivity gives finer resolution but a smaller range before the output reaches the supply rail. Averaging 16 samples cuts random noise by √16 = 4, so the effective step on the 185 mV/A part improves from 26.4 mA towards 6.6 mA if the noise is enough to dither the readings.

## Common mistakes

- **Feeding a 5 V sensor into a 3.3 V ADC.** The input saturates at 3.3 V, which is only 4.32 A on the 185 mV/A part, and higher outputs can exceed the pin's absolute maximum. Use a resistor divider and rescale.
- **Assuming the ADC reference is exactly 5 V.** USB supplies range roughly 4.75 to 5.25 V; a fixed constant in firmware turns that into a gain error.
- **Hard coding the offset as 512 counts.** Each part has its own zero error; read it at start-up with no current flowing.
- **Choosing the wrong variant.** The 5 A, 20 A and 30 A parts look identical; check the marking before trusting 185 mV/A.
- **Leaving the filter pin open.** The bandwidth is then at its maximum and so is the noise; add the datasheet filter capacitor if you do not need fast transients.
