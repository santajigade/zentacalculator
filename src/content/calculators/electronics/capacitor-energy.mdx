## How to use the capacitor energy calculator

Enter the **capacitance** and the **voltage** it is charged to. The calculator gives the stored energy and the charge. To see how much of that energy a circuit can actually use, enter the **voltage it discharges down to** — the usable energy and its share of the total appear below. Press × on Voltage to find the voltage needed to store a given energy.

## Capacitor energy and charge formulas

<p class="formula">E = ½ C V² &nbsp;·&nbsp; Q = C V &nbsp;·&nbsp; E<sub>usable</sub> = ½ C (V₁² − V₂²)</p>

Because energy grows with V², doubling the voltage stores four times the energy, while doubling the capacitance only doubles it.

## Worked example

A 1,000 µF capacitor charged to 50 V that feeds a circuit which stops working below 25 V:

- Stored energy E = ½ × 0.001 F × 50² = **1.25 J**
- Charge Q = 0.001 × 50 = **0.05 C**
- Usable energy = ½ × 0.001 × (50² − 25²) = **0.9375 J**, or **75%** of the stored energy

## Practical notes

- **Supercapacitors vs batteries:** a 3,000 F, 2.7 V supercapacitor stores about 3 Wh — roughly what a few AA batteries hold — but can deliver it in seconds and survive hundreds of thousands of cycles, which suits short backup, regenerative braking and burst power.
- **Series strings:** capacitors in series share the voltage, so the bank voltage rises but the capacitance falls (1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂); use balancing resistors or active balancing for supercapacitors.
- **Derating:** electrolytic capacitance has a −20% tolerance and falls with age and cold; ceramic capacitors lose capacitance under DC bias. Use the worst-case value for hold-up time.
- **Safe discharge:** use a resistor sized for the energy (not a screwdriver), and allow for dielectric absorption — a large capacitor can recover a few percent of its voltage after being shorted.

## Hold-up time from the default capacitor

The default 1,000 µF at 50 V, discharging to 25 V, delivers 0.9375 J. Feeding a converter that draws a steady 5 W from it, the supply rides through for 0.9375 ÷ 5 = **187.5 ms**. If the 5 W is the converter output and it runs at 85% efficiency, only 0.797 J reaches the load, giving **159.4 ms**. With an electrolytic at its −20% tolerance limit the usable energy falls to 0.75 J, so design hold-up on the minimum capacitance.

| Load power | Hold-up (ideal) | Hold-up at 85% efficiency |
|---|---|---|
| 1 W | 937.5 ms | 796.9 ms |
| 2 W | 468.8 ms | 398.4 ms |
| 5 W | 187.5 ms | 159.4 ms |
| 10 W | 93.8 ms | 79.7 ms |
| 20 W | 46.9 ms | 39.8 ms |

## Charging always costs twice the stored energy

Charging the capacitor to 50 V from a fixed voltage source draws Q × V = 0.05 × 50 = **2.50 J** from the source. Only 1.25 J ends up stored; the other **1.25 J** is dissipated in the series resistance, whatever its value. A smaller resistor only makes the loss happen faster with a higher current peak. Inductor-based chargers avoid this by limiting the current instead of the voltage.

## Discharging safely

A 1 kΩ bleed resistor across the default capacitor gives a time constant of 1.0 s. The first instant passes 50 mA and dissipates 2.5 W, and the voltage reaches 5 V after τ × ln(50 ÷ 5) = **2.30 s**. The resistor absorbs the full 1.25 J, so a 3 W wirewound part handles it comfortably; a 0.25 W film resistor could fail open on larger banks.

## Measuring capacitance from a discharge

Supercapacitor capacitance is measured by constant-current discharge, as in IEC 62391-1: C = I × Δt ÷ ΔV, taken over the linear part of the curve after the initial ESR step. A cell discharged at 1 A that falls 1.9 V in 20 s has C = 1 × 20 ÷ 1.9 = **10.5 F**. The sudden drop when the current starts, divided by the current, gives the ESR.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering microfarads as farads.** Using 1,000 F instead of 1,000 µF gives 1,250,000 J, a million times too much.
- **Using E = QV.** That is the energy drawn from the source; the energy stored is half of it, ½QV.
- **Counting all the stored energy as usable.** Below the regulator's minimum input the remaining 25% stays in the capacitor.
- **Ignoring ESR at high discharge current.** The I × ESR drop raises the effective cut-off voltage and reduces usable energy.
- **Exceeding the voltage rating in series banks.** Without balancing, leakage differences can put one cell above its rating.
