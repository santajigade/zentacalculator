    A fan's air power is airflow times pressure rise, and its shaft power is that divided by the fan efficiency. This fan affinity laws calculator finds both, then scales flow, pressure and power to a new speed: flow with the speed ratio, pressure with its square and power with its cube.

    HVAC designers, energy auditors and plant engineers use it to size fan motors and to estimate VFD energy savings before slowing a fan instead of throttling it with a damper.

    How to use the fan power calculator

    Enter the airflow, the pressure rise across the fan and the fan efficiency to get air and shaft power. Then enter the present and new speeds to see how flow, pressure and power change — for example when a VFD slows the fan.

    Fan power and fan law formulas

    Pair = Q × Δp  ·  Pshaft = Q × Δp ÷ η  ·  Q₂ = Q₁(N₂/N₁)  ·  Δp₂ = Δp₁(N₂/N₁)²  ·  P₂ = P₁(N₂/N₁)³

    Worked example

    10,000 m³/h (2.7778 m³/s) against 1,000 Pa, fan efficiency 70%, slowed from 1,450 to 1,160 rpm:

    • Air power = 2.7778 × 1,000 = 2.778 kW
    • Shaft power = 2.778 ÷ 0.70 = 3.968 kW
    • At 1,160 rpm: Q = 8,000 m³/h, Δp = 640 Pa, shaft power = 2.032 kW

    Fan speed table (10,000 m³/h, 1,000 Pa, 70% efficiency)

    Speed Airflow Pressure Shaft power
    100% 10,000 m³/h 1000 Pa 3.97 kW
    90% 9,000 m³/h 810 Pa 2.89 kW
    80% 8,000 m³/h 640 Pa 2.03 kW

    Energy saving example

    Using the worked example above, the fan runs 6,000 hours a year at 1,160 rpm instead of 1,450 rpm:

    • Shaft power falls from 3.968 kW to 2.032 kW, a saving of 1.937 kW
    • Shaft energy saved = 1.937 × 6,000 = 11,619 kWh per year

    Motor and drive losses are not included; a VFD itself loses roughly 2 to 3% of its throughput.

    Air density and fan power

    Pressure and power scale with gas density at the same volume flow and speed. For the example fan rated at 1,000 Pa with air at 20 °C (1.204 kg/m³, sea level):

    Gas temperature Density Pressure rise Shaft power
    0 °C 1.292 kg/m³ 1,073 Pa 4.26 kW
    20 °C 1.204 kg/m³ 1,000 Pa 3.97 kW
    40 °C 1.127 kg/m³ 936 Pa 3.71 kW
    80 °C 0.999 kg/m³ 830 Pa 3.29 kW
    150 °C 0.834 kg/m³ 693 Pa 2.75 kW
    250 °C 0.675 kg/m³ 560 Pa 2.22 kW

    Using the fan laws in practice

    The laws compare two operating points of the same fan in the same system, so they hold only if the system curve passes through the origin: all of the pressure is friction that varies with flow squared. Airflow is usually measured in the field with a pitot tube traverse across the duct, pressure rise with a manometer across the fan, and power with a clamp-on power meter at the motor. In variable air volume systems that hold a fixed duct static pressure, the fan cannot follow the cube law all the way down, so real savings are smaller than the calculator shows.

    Speeding a fan up for more airflow

    The laws work just as well upwards, and the cube term is what catches people out. To raise the default fan from 10,000 to 12,000 m³/h the speed must rise to 1,450 × 12,000 ÷ 10,000 = 1,740 rpm. Pressure then climbs to 1,440 Pa and shaft power to 3.968 × 1.2³ = 6.86 kW, 1.73 times the original, so a 20% airflow increase usually means a bigger motor. Check the fan’s maximum rated speed and the belt or pulley ratio before relying on a VFD to overspeed it.

    Changing the impeller diameter

    For geometrically similar fans at the same speed, flow scales with the diameter cubed, pressure with its square and power with its fifth power. Trimming from 630 mm to 560 mm (ratio 0.8889) gives:

    Quantity Law Ratio Default fan
    Airflow (D₂/D₁)³ 0.702 7,023 m³/h
    Pressure (D₂/D₁)² 0.790 790 Pa
    Shaft power (D₂/D₁)⁵ 0.555 2.20 kW

    The diameter laws are less exact than the speed laws because a smaller impeller in the same casing has larger clearances.

    Imperial example

    A fan moving 5,000 CFM against 2 in. w.g. total pressure at 65% efficiency needs 5,000 × 2 ÷ (6,356 × 0.65) = 2.42 bhp. In SI that is 2.360 m³/s × 498 Pa ÷ 0.65 = 1.81 kW, the same answer within rounding of the 6,356 constant.

    Common mistakes

    • Applying the laws to a damper change. Closing a damper moves the fan to a new point on the same speed curve. The fan laws only describe speed (or diameter) changes.
    • Starting a hot gas fan cold. A fan sized for gas at 150 °C moves air that is 1.44 times denser at 20 °C, so on a cold start it needs 1.44 times the power and can overload its motor.
    • Mixing static and total pressure. Use fan total pressure with total efficiency, or static pressure with static efficiency.
    • Mixing units. Air power is m³/s × Pa = W. Feeding m³/h or CFM straight into that product gives an answer 3,600 or 1,700 times too large.
    • Extrapolating to very low speeds. Below about 30 to 40% speed, motor cooling, fan stall and minimum duct pressure take over, and efficiency no longer stays constant.

    Notes

    • Air density matters: pressure and power scale with density, so a fan handling hot air needs less power for the same volume flow.
    • Add motor and drive losses to the shaft power when sizing the motor.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I calculate fan power?

    Air power = Q × Δp (m³/s × Pa = W). Shaft power = air power ÷ fan efficiency. 10,000 m³/h at 1,000 Pa is 2.78 kW of air power, or about 3.97 kW at the shaft with a 70% efficient fan.

    What are the fan affinity laws?

    At constant air density, Q₂/Q₁ = N₂/N₁, p₂/p₁ = (N₂/N₁)² and P₂/P₁ = (N₂/N₁)³.

    What is the fan power formula in imperial units?

    Shaft horsepower = CFM × total pressure (in. w.g.) ÷ (6356 × efficiency).

    Should I use static or total pressure?

    Use fan total pressure with total efficiency, or fan static pressure with static efficiency — do not mix them. Both give the same shaft power.

    Why does a hot gas fan overload its motor at a cold start?

    Fan power is proportional to gas density. A fan designed for gas at 150 °C handles air about 1.44 times denser when it starts cold at 20 °C, so it draws about 1.44 times its hot running power. Size the motor for the cold start or keep the inlet damper closed until the gas warms up.

    How much more power does a fan need if I speed it up 20%?

    Power rises with the cube of speed, so 1.2³ = 1.728 times. The default fan goes from 3.97 kW to 6.86 kW to deliver 12,000 m³/h, while pressure rises 44%.

    What happens to fan performance with a smaller impeller?

    At the same speed, flow falls with the diameter ratio cubed, pressure with its square and power with its fifth power. Going from 630 to 560 mm cuts flow to 70.2%, pressure to 79.0% and power to 55.5% of the original.
