## How to use the beat frequency calculator

Enter the two **frequencies**. The calculator returns the beat frequency (beats per second), the beat period and the average pitch you hear. To find an unknown second frequency, count the beats and remember there are two answers: f₁ + f<sub>beat</sub> or f₁ − f<sub>beat</sub>.

## Beat frequency formula

<p class="formula">f<sub>beat</sub> = |f<sub>1</sub> − f<sub>2</sub>| &nbsp;·&nbsp; T<sub>beat</sub> = 1 ÷ f<sub>beat</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; f<sub>avg</sub> = (f<sub>1</sub> + f<sub>2</sub>) ÷ 2</p>

The sum of two equal-amplitude waves is 2A cos(2π·Δf/2·t) · sin(2π·f<sub>avg</sub>·t); the envelope peaks twice per cycle of Δf/2, so loudness pulses at Δf.

## Example

A tuning fork at 440 Hz and a string at 444 Hz:

- Beat frequency = |440 − 444| = **4 Hz** (four beats every second)
- Beat period = 1 ÷ 4 = **0.25 s**
- Perceived pitch = (440 + 444) ÷ 2 = **442 Hz**

## Notes

- The same principle appears in radio as the heterodyne: mixing two signals produces their difference frequency.
- Beats also occur between a moving and a stationary source because of the [Doppler effect](/physics/doppler-effect/).

## Which side of the reference is the unknown tone?

With the default 440 Hz fork and 4 beats per second, the string could be at 444 Hz or 436 Hz. Tighten the string slightly: if the beats speed up, it was already sharp (444 Hz moving to about 445 Hz); if they slow down, it was flat. In pitch terms, the default 4 Hz beat at A440 is 15.7 cents, roughly one sixth of a semitone, which most listeners hear clearly as out of tune when the two notes are played together.

## Beats in piano tuning

Tuners do not only zero out beats. Equal temperament makes most intervals slightly impure, so the coincident partials of two notes beat at a known rate that the tuner counts. For a fifth the third partial of the lower note meets the second partial of the upper note; for a major third it is the fifth and fourth partials. From A3 = 220 Hz:

| Interval | Upper note | Coincident partials | Beat rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect fifth (A3 to E4) | 329.63 Hz | 660.00 Hz vs 659.26 Hz | 0.74 Hz |
| Perfect fourth (A3 to D4) | 293.66 Hz | 880.00 Hz vs 880.99 Hz | 0.99 Hz |
| Major third (A3 to C♯4) | 277.18 Hz | 1100.00 Hz vs 1108.73 Hz | 8.73 Hz |
| Major sixth (A3 to F♯4) | 369.99 Hz | 1100.00 Hz vs 1109.98 Hz | 9.98 Hz |

The fifth beats slowly, about 0.74 Hz or one beat every 1.3 s, while the major third beats several times a second. Counting these rates against a stopwatch is how a temperament octave is set before the rest of the piano is tuned to it. Real piano strings are slightly inharmonic, so the exact rates shift a little from these ideal figures.

## Beats from machines

Two pumps on a common header running at 1,480 and 1,490 rpm vibrate at 24.67 Hz and 24.83 Hz. Their vibrations add to a level that rises and falls 0.1667 times per second, a slow throb every **6 s** that can be felt in the pipework and seen on a vibration trend as a regular amplitude swing. Twin engine aircraft and ships show the same effect, and synchronisers adjust one engine until the throb stops.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the two possible answers.** A beat count gives only the size of the difference, not its sign.
- **Counting for too short a time.** At 0.5 Hz one beat takes 2 s; count over at least 10 s and divide to get a reliable rate.
- **Expecting complete silence between beats.** With unequal amplitudes A₁ and A₂ the envelope only dips to |A₁ − A₂|, so the beats are shallow.
- **Confusing the envelope period with the beat period.** The cos(πΔf t) term repeats at Δf/2, but loudness peaks twice per cycle, so beats occur at Δf.
- **Using beats for widely separated tones.** Beyond roughly 15 to 20 Hz the ear hears roughness, then two notes, not counted beats.
