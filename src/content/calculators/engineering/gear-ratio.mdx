## How to use the gear ratio calculator

Enter the **number of teeth** on the driving and driven gears, the **input speed**, the **input torque** and the **efficiency** of the gear mesh. The calculator returns the gear ratio, the output speed and the output torque. A ratio below 1 is a speed-up (overdrive) and reduces torque.

## Gear ratio formula

<p class="formula">i = z<sub>2</sub> ÷ z<sub>1</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; N<sub>2</sub> = N<sub>1</sub> ÷ i &nbsp;·&nbsp; T<sub>2</sub> = T<sub>1</sub> × i × η</p>

## Example

A 20-tooth pinion on a 1,450 rpm motor delivering 50 N·m drives a 60-tooth gear with 98% efficiency:

- Ratio i = 60 ÷ 20 = **3 : 1**
- Output speed = 1,450 ÷ 3 = **483.3 rpm**
- Output torque = 50 × 3 × 0.98 = **147 N·m**

## Notes

- Use gear counts with no common factor (a "hunting tooth" ratio such as 17 : 68 → 17 : 69) to spread wear evenly across teeth.
- Belt and chain drives follow the same speed rule with pulley diameters or sprocket teeth — see the [pulley speed calculator](/engineering/pulley-speed/).
- Size the output shaft for the increased torque with the [shaft diameter calculator](/engineering/shaft-diameter/).

## Power balance check

Power in must equal power out plus the loss in the mesh, which is a good check on any result:

- Input power = 50 N·m × 1,450 rpm × 2π ÷ 60 = **7.59 kW**
- Output power = 147 N·m × 483.3 rpm × 2π ÷ 60 = **7.44 kW**
- Loss = 0.15 kW, about 2%, which leaves the gearbox as heat

## Two-stage example and gear geometry

Adding a second stage of 18 : 54 (3 : 1) after the first gives an overall ratio of 3 × 3 = **9 : 1**. The output turns at 1,450 ÷ 9 = **161.1 rpm** with 50 × 9 × 0.98² = **432.2 N·m**.

For spur gears the tooth size is set by the **module** m, and the pitch diameter is d = m × z. With m = 3 mm the 20-tooth pinion is 60 mm and the 60-tooth gear 180 mm across the pitch circle, the centre distance is a = m (z₁ + z₂) ÷ 2 = **120 mm**, and the pitch line velocity is π × 0.06 × 1,450 ÷ 60 = **4.56 m/s**.

## Common mistakes

- **Inverting the ratio.** The ratio is driven teeth ÷ driving teeth; swapping them turns a reduction into a speed-up.
- **Applying efficiency to speed.** Losses reduce torque and power, not speed; the output speed of a gear pair is fixed by the tooth counts.
- **Assuming high efficiency for worm gears.** A worm set can be 50% efficient or less at high ratios, so the output torque is far below T₁ × i.
- **Forgetting the service factor.** Shock loads, frequent starts and long running hours need a gearbox rated above the calculated torque.
- **Meshing gears of different module or pressure angle.** They will not run together even if the tooth counts give the right ratio.
