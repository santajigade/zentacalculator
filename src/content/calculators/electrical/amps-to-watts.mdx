## How to use the amps to watts calculator

Enter the **current** and **voltage**, choose the system and enter the power factor (1 for DC, heaters and kettles). The calculator gives the power in watts. To go from watts to amps, press × on Current and type the watts.

## Amps to watts formulas

| System | Power |
|---|---|
| DC | P = V × I |
| Single-phase AC | P = V × I × PF |
| Three-phase AC | P = √3 × V<sub>L</sub> × I × PF |

## Battery systems

For 12 V and 24 V systems (cars, solar, boats), current is high for modest power: a 1,200 W inverter at 12 V draws about 100 A from the battery, plus losses. Use thick cables and fuses close to the battery.

## Watts, VA and power factor

For AC loads with a power factor below 1 (motors, some electronics), the real power in watts is lower than volts × amps. That product is the apparent power in VA — see the [kVA to kW converter](/electrical/kva-to-kw/).

## Worked example

With the default inputs, a 10 A load on a 230 V single-phase supply at unity power factor:

- P = V × I × PF = 230 × 10 × 1 = **2,300 W**, or 2.3 kW

Switch the system to three-phase, enter 400 V and a power factor of 0.85, and the same 10 A per line gives √3 × 400 × 10 × 0.85 = **5,889 W**. The current is identical, but the three-phase circuit delivers about 2.6 times the power because three conductors share it.

| System | Power at 10 A |
|---|---|
| 12 V DC | 120 W |

| 120 V single-phase, PF 0.9 | 1,080 W |
| 230 V single-phase, PF 1 | 2,300 W |
| 230 V single-phase, PF 0.8 | 1,840 W |
| 400 V three-phase, PF 0.85 | 5,889 W |
| 480 V three-phase, PF 0.85 | 7,067 W |

The table shows why a current rating alone says little about capacity: 10 A is a small car accessory at 12 V and a 7.1 kW motor at 480 V.

## Turning a clamp meter reading into watts

A clamp meter reads RMS current, not power. Multiplying that reading by the supply voltage gives volt-amperes, which equals watts only for resistive loads such as kettles, immersion heaters and incandescent lamps. A fridge compressor drawing 4.2 A on 230 V shows 966 VA, but at a typical running power factor of 0.7 it uses only about **676 W**. For an accurate figure on motors, compressors and electronics, use a plug-in energy meter or a power analyser that multiplies voltage and current sample by sample. Read the current once the load has settled, since motors draw several times their running current for the first second or two.

## Battery and inverter currents

On the DC side of an inverter, divide the AC load by the inverter efficiency before converting. A 1,000 W load on a 12 V inverter that is 90% efficient pulls 1,000 ÷ 0.9 ÷ 12 = **93 A** from the battery, which sets the cable size and fuse rating.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the phase voltage in the three-phase formula.** √3 × 230 V × 10 A × 0.85 gives 3,386 W, far below the real 5,889 W on a 400 V line voltage. Use the line-to-line voltage with √3.
- **Leaving power factor at 1 for motors.** Pumps, fans and compressors run at 0.7 to 0.9, so watts are lower than volts × amps.
- **Reading the nameplate current as the running current.** Nameplates often show the maximum or full-load value; actual draw at part load is lower.
- **Forgetting inverter losses on battery systems.** Ignoring a 10% loss underestimates battery current by about 11%.
- **Using peak instead of RMS values.** A 230 V supply peaks at about 325 V, but power formulas use RMS voltage and current.
