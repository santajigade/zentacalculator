    Bolt Torque Calculator (T = K·D·F, Preload & Stress)

    Tightening torque is the everyday way to set a bolt's clamp force, yet most of the torque is spent on friction under the nut and in the threads. This bolt torque calculator uses T = K × D × F, with the nut factor K standing in for that friction, and checks the bolt stress.

    Mechanics, maintenance fitters and design engineers use it to set wrench values for flanges, machine frames and structural joints.

    How to use the bolt torque calculator

    Enter the nut factor K, the nominal bolt diameter, the target preload and the bolt’s tensile stress area. The calculator returns the tightening torque and the tensile stress the preload creates. To find the preload a given torque produces, enter the torque and clear the preload.

    Bolt torque formula

    T = K × D × F  ·  σ = F ÷ At

    For ISO metric threads the tensile stress area is At = π/4 × (d − 0.9382 p)², where p is the thread pitch.

    Example

    An M12 × 1.75 bolt (At = 84.3 mm²) tightened dry (K = 0.2) to a 30 kN preload:

    • T = 0.2 × 0.012 m × 30,000 N = 72 N·m
    • Bolt stress σ = 30,000 ÷ 84.3 = 356 MPa — about 61% of the 580 MPa proof stress of class 8.8

    Notes

    • Check the clamped joint as well as the bolt: soft gaskets and painted surfaces relax and lose preload.
    • Use the factor of safety calculator to compare the bolt stress with the material’s proof or yield strength.

    Where the torque goes

    In a typical dry steel joint only about 10% of the applied torque stretches the bolt. Roughly 50% is lost to friction under the nut or head and about 40% to thread friction. So a modest change in surface condition changes the preload far more than a change in the wrench setting. For the default 12 mm bolt at 30 kN, the 72 N·m from the calculator assumes K = 0.2. The table shows the torque needed for the same 30 kN at other nut factors, and the preload the same 72 N·m would give instead:

    Nut factor K Torque for 30 kN Preload at 72 N·m Stress at 72 N·m
    0.10 36.0 N·m 60.0 kN 712 MPa
    0.12 43.2 N·m 50.0 kN 593 MPa
    0.15 54.0 N·m 40.0 kN 474 MPa
    0.18 64.8 N·m 33.3 kN 395 MPa
    0.20 72.0 N·m 30.0 kN 356 MPa
    0.25 90.0 N·m 24.0 kN 285 MPa

    Oiling the threads to K = 0.15 and keeping the dry 72 N·m value raises the preload to 40.0 kN and the stress to 474 MPa, 82% of the 580 MPa proof stress of class 8.8. With MoS₂ paste the same wrench setting would take the bolt past proof.

    How much the bolt stretches

    Preload is just elastic stretch. Over a 40 mm grip the default bolt lengthens by about F × L ÷ (E × At) = 30,000 × 40 ÷ (200,000 × 84.3) = 0.071 mm (with E = 200 GPa for steel and the tensile stress area used for the whole length, a simple estimate). Ultrasonic bolt gauges and bolt-length micrometers measure this stretch directly, which removes friction from the result.

    Tightening in practice

    • Use a calibrated torque wrench; ISO 6789 allows about ±4% on most hand wrenches, much smaller than the friction scatter.
    • Tighten flanges and multi-bolt joints in a star pattern in stages, for example 30%, 70% then 100% of the final torque, then a check pass.
    • Apply torque to the nut where possible, since turning the head adds under-head friction you may not have allowed for.
    • Add the prevailing torque of nylon insert or all-metal locknuts to the calculated value; it produces no preload.

    Common mistakes

    • Mixing mm and m in a hand calculation. The calculator converts units, but working T = K × D × F by hand with D = 12 instead of 0.012 m gives 1,000 times the torque.
    • Using a dry torque chart for lubricated or plated bolts. It is the most common cause of stripped threads and snapped studs.
    • Reusing torque-to-yield bolts. Cylinder head and similar bolts are stretched past yield and must be replaced.
    • Ignoring joint relaxation. Gaskets and paint creep, so critical joints are retorqued after the first heat cycle or a set time.
    • Fitting extensions or crowfoot adapters without correction. An adapter that lengthens the wrench changes the torque actually delivered.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the formula for bolt torque?

    T = K × D × F, where K is the nut factor (about 0.2 for dry steel), D the nominal diameter and F the desired preload. Use metres and newtons to get N·m.

    What preload should a bolt have?

    A common target is 70–75% of the bolt's proof load for reusable joints. For an ISO 8.8 bolt up to M16 the proof stress is 580 MPa, so 75% is about 435 MPa on the tensile stress area.

    How much does lubrication change the torque?

    A lot. Oiled or MoS₂-coated threads have a lower K, so the same torque produces 30–60% more preload. Always use the K value matching the actual thread condition, or the bolt may be over-stressed.

    Why is torque tightening imprecise?

    Friction varies from bolt to bolt, so the same torque can give ±25% scatter in preload. Critical joints use torque-angle tightening, hydraulic tensioners or ultrasonic bolt-elongation measurement.

    How much does a bolt stretch when it is tightened?

    Stretch = F × L ÷ (E × A). An M12 steel bolt at 30 kN over a 40 mm grip stretches about 0.071 mm. Measuring this elongation directly is more accurate than relying on torque.
