## How to use the Avogadro's law calculator

Enter the **initial volume** and **amount of gas** in moles, and the **final amount**. The calculator gives the final volume at the same temperature and pressure, and the molar volume implied by your first state. Enter any three of the four values to solve for the remaining one.

## Avogadro's law formula

<p class="formula">V<sub>1</sub> ÷ n<sub>1</sub> = V<sub>2</sub> ÷ n<sub>2</sub> &nbsp;⇒&nbsp; V<sub>2</sub> = V<sub>1</sub> × n<sub>2</sub> ÷ n<sub>1</sub></p>

<p class="formula">V<sub>m</sub> = V ÷ n = R T ÷ P</p>

## Example

A balloon holds 6.0 L containing 0.25 mol of gas. Gas is added until it holds 0.40 mol, at the same temperature and pressure:

- V<sub>2</sub> = 6.0 × 0.40 ÷ 0.25 = **9.6 L**
- Molar volume = 6.0 ÷ 0.25 = **24.0 L/mol** — about room temperature at 1 atm

## Molar volume of an ideal gas

| Conditions | Molar volume |
|---|---|
| 0 °C, 1 atm (STP, older definition) | 22.414 L/mol |
| 0 °C, 1 bar (IUPAC STP) | 22.711 L/mol |
| 15 °C, 1 atm | 23.645 L/mol |
| 20 °C, 1 atm (NTP) | 24.055 L/mol |
| 25 °C, 1 atm | 24.465 L/mol |
| 25 °C, 1 bar (SATP) | 24.790 L/mol |

Calculated from V<sub>m</sub> = RT ÷ P with R = 8.314462618 J/(mol·K).

## Reading more from the default example

The default molar volume of 24.0 L/mol is itself a clue to the conditions. At 1 atm, V<sub>m</sub> = RT ÷ P gives T = 101325 × 0.0240 ÷ 8.314 = **292.5 K**, about 19.3 °C, so the balloon is at a cool room temperature.

- Molecules before: 0.25 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.506 × 10²³**
- Molecules after: 0.40 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **2.409 × 10²³**
- Gas added: 0.15 mol, which takes up 0.15 × 24.0 = 3.6 L, exactly the 9.6 − 6.0 L increase

Avogadro's law cares only about moles, so those 0.15 mol could be 0.600 g of helium or 6.60 g of carbon dioxide and the balloon would grow by the same 3.6 L.

## Following a gas reaction by its volume

When gases react at constant temperature and pressure, the volume tracks the total moles. Mix 0.25 mol N₂ with 0.75 mol H₂ at the default 24.0 L/mol: the mixture fills 24.0 L. In N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ four molecules become two, so if 20% of the nitrogen reacts the gas holds 0.20 mol N₂, 0.60 mol H₂ and 0.10 mol NH₃, 0.90 mol in all, and shrinks to **21.6 L**. Watching that contraction in a gas syringe or a pressure controlled reactor is a simple way to measure conversion.

## Standard flow rates are really molar flows

Mass flow controllers in labs and semiconductor fabs are set in sccm, standard cubic centimetres per minute. Because one standard volume always holds the same number of moles, 1 sccm referenced to 0 °C and 1 atm is 1 ÷ 22,414 mol per minute, or 7.436 × 10⁻⁷ mol/s. A 100 sccm nitrogen flow therefore delivers 0.1250 g/min. Some suppliers reference 20 °C instead, which makes the same reading 6.8% fewer moles, so always check the reference conditions.

## Density of common gases at 25 °C and 1 atm

With V<sub>m</sub> = 24.465 L/mol, density is molar mass ÷ V<sub>m</sub>:

| Gas | Molar mass | Density | Volume of 1 g |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen (H₂) | 2.016 g/mol | 0.0824 g/L | 12.136 L |
| Helium (He) | 4.0026 g/mol | 0.1636 g/L | 6.112 L |
| Methane (CH₄) | 16.043 g/mol | 0.6557 g/L | 1.525 L |
| Nitrogen (N₂) | 28.014 g/mol | 1.1450 g/L | 0.873 L |
| Oxygen (O₂) | 31.998 g/mol | 1.3079 g/L | 0.765 L |
| Argon (Ar) | 39.948 g/mol | 1.6328 g/L | 0.612 L |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | 44.009 g/mol | 1.7988 g/L | 0.556 L |

Every row holds the same number of molecules per litre; only the mass of each molecule changes.

## Common mistakes

- **Using 22.4 L/mol at room temperature.** At 25 °C and 1 atm the molar volume is 24.465 L/mol, so 22.4 understates volumes by about 9.2%.
- **Assuming equal volumes have equal mass.** Equal volumes hold equal moles; a litre of CO₂ is 21.8 times heavier than a litre of H₂.
- **Counting condensed products as gas.** Water formed in combustion is liquid at room temperature and contributes almost nothing to the final gas volume.
- **Letting temperature or pressure drift.** Adding gas to a rigid vessel raises the pressure instead of the volume, and a warm reaction expands the gas before it cools.
- **Applying it to dense or easily condensed gases.** CO₂ and NH₃ deviate by about 0.5 to 1% at 1 atm and room temperature and far more at high pressure; use a real gas model there.
