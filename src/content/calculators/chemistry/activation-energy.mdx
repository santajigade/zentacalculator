## How to use the activation energy calculator

Enter the **rate constant** measured at the first **temperature** and the rate constant at the second temperature. The calculator gives the ratio k₂/k₁ and the **activation energy** in kJ/mol. To predict a rate constant, enter k₁, both temperatures and Ea, and clear k₂ and the ratio.

## Two-point Arrhenius equation

<p class="formula">ln(k<sub>2</sub> ÷ k<sub>1</sub>) = (E<sub>a</sub> ÷ R) × (1/T<sub>1</sub> − 1/T<sub>2</sub>)</p>

<p class="formula">E<sub>a</sub> = R ln(k<sub>2</sub> ÷ k<sub>1</sub>) ÷ (1/T<sub>1</sub> − 1/T<sub>2</sub>)</p>

R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); temperatures must be absolute (kelvin). The pre-exponential factor A cancels, so only the two measurements are needed — use the [Arrhenius equation calculator](/chemistry/arrhenius-equation/) to work with A directly.

## Example

A rate constant rises from 0.0010 at 25 °C to 0.0040 at 45 °C:

- ln(0.0040 ÷ 0.0010) = ln 4 = **1.3863**
- 1/298.15 − 1/318.15 = **2.1084 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹**
- E<sub>a</sub> = 8.314 × 1.3863 ÷ 2.1084 × 10⁻⁴ = **54.67 kJ/mol**

## What the default result tells you

The default answer, E<sub>a</sub> = **54.67 kJ/mol** (or 13.07 kcal/mol), can be turned straight into predictions:

- E<sub>a</sub> ÷ R = 54,667 ÷ 8.314 = **6,575 K**, the slope magnitude of the ln k against 1/T line
- Heating from 25 °C to 35 °C multiplies k by **2.05**, so the old doubling rule fits this reaction well
- At 60 °C the rate constant becomes 0.001 × exp[6,575 × (1/298.15 − 1/333.15)] = **0.0101**, 10.1 times the 25 °C value
- Back calculating the pre-exponential factor gives A = k₁ × exp(E<sub>a</sub> ÷ RT₁) = 3.778 × 10⁶ in the same units as k

## Second example: estimating shelf life from accelerated ageing

A drug solution loses 5% of its potency in 150 days at 45 °C and in 40 days at 60 °C. Time to the same conversion is inversely proportional to k, so k₂ ÷ k₁ = 150 ÷ 40 = 3.75:

- E<sub>a</sub> = 8.314 × ln 3.75 ÷ (1/318.15 − 1/333.15) = **77.7 kJ/mol**
- Predicted time at 25 °C = 150 × exp[(E<sub>a</sub> ÷ R)(1/298.15 − 1/318.15)] = **1,075 days**, about 2.9 years
- In a refrigerator at 5 °C the estimate stretches to 10,222 days

Stability guidelines treat such extrapolations as provisional until real time data confirm them, because a second degradation route can take over at lower temperature.

## How activation energy is measured

Rate constants come from following concentration against time at a carefully held temperature, by UV/visible absorbance, conductivity, gas evolution or chromatography. Good practice uses four or more temperatures and fits ln k against 1/T: the slope is −E<sub>a</sub> ÷ R and the scatter shows whether a single E<sub>a</sub> describes the data. Thermal analysis offers a shortcut: in the Kissinger method, differential scanning calorimetry peaks at several heating rates give E<sub>a</sub> for curing resins and decomposing solids. True E<sub>a</sub> = 54.67 kJ/mol and k₁ = 0.001 at 25 °C; each row changes one input:

| Temperature span | True k₂ ÷ k₁ | k₂ read 5% high | T₂ read 0.5 K high |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 to 30 °C | 1.439 | 62.00 kJ/mol (+13.4%) | 49.78 kJ/mol (-8.9%) |
| 25 to 35 °C | 2.045 | 58.39 kJ/mol (+6.8%) | 52.15 kJ/mol (-4.6%) |
| 25 to 45 °C | 4.000 | 56.59 kJ/mol (+3.5%) | 53.42 kJ/mol (-2.3%) |
| 25 to 75 °C | 23.738 | 55.51 kJ/mol (+1.5%) | 54.20 kJ/mol (-0.8%) |

A 5 K span turns small errors into large ones, while a 50 K span keeps both under about 2%.

## Common mistakes

- **Using Celsius in a hand calculation.** With 1/25 − 1/45 the default data give 0.648 kJ/mol, wrong by a factor of about 84.
- **Taking log₁₀ instead of ln.** log₁₀ 4 in the formula gives 23.74 kJ/mol, low by the factor 2.303; with log₁₀ the constant must be 2.303 R.
- **Entering reaction times as rate constants.** Times fall as rates rise, so uninverted times give −54.67 kJ/mol; use 1/t or swap them.
- **Mixing J and kJ when predicting k.** E<sub>a</sub> ÷ R needs E<sub>a</sub> in J/mol; using 54.67 instead of 54,670 makes the predicted change almost vanish.
- **Comparing rates measured at different concentrations.** An initial rate depends on concentration as well as k, so both runs must start from the same composition or be converted to rate constants first.

## Notes

- Measure the two rates as far apart in temperature as practical — a small ΔT magnifies measurement error in Ea.
- A curved Arrhenius plot (ln k vs 1/T) means the mechanism changes with temperature; two points will then give only an average Ea.
- Typical values: enzyme reactions 30–60 kJ/mol, many organic reactions 50–100 kJ/mol, diffusion-controlled reactions below 20 kJ/mol.
