## How to use the resistor color code calculator

Choose the four **colour bands** from left to right. The calculator shows the resistance and its tolerance range. The default — brown, black, red, gold — is 1 kΩ ±5%.

For more background, read [What Is Resistance?](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/what-is-resistance/).

## E-series values

Resistors come in standard series. E12 (±10%): 1, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 2.7, 3.3, 3.9, 4.7, 5.6, 6.8, 8.2 — repeated for each decade. E24 (±5%) adds values in between, and E96 (±1%) has 96 per decade.

## SMD resistors

Surface-mount resistors use number codes: "103" = 10 × 10³ = 10 kΩ; "4R7" = 4.7 Ω; E96 parts use codes like "01C" = 10 kΩ.

## Worked example

A resistor reads **yellow, violet, orange, gold**:

- Digits: yellow = 4, violet = 7, giving 47
- Multiplier: orange = × 1,000, so R = 47 × 1,000 = **47 kΩ**
- Tolerance: gold = ±5%, so the part can measure anywhere from **44,650 Ω to 49,350 Ω**

With the calculator defaults (brown, black, red, gold) the value is 1 kΩ and the range is 950 Ω to 1,050 Ω. A multimeter reading of 987 Ω is -1.3% low, well inside the band.

## Reading 5-band and 6-band parts

Precision resistors use three digit bands. Brown, black, black, brown, brown reads 100 × 10 = **1 kΩ** at ±1%. A sixth band, when present, gives the temperature coefficient: brown is 100 ppm/K, red 50 ppm/K, yellow 25 ppm/K. On a 5-band part the tolerance band is often set slightly apart from the others, which is the most reliable clue to the reading direction.

## Checking a value in practice

Colours fade and shift with heat, so a brown that has been run hot can look orange or red. When in doubt, measure the part out of circuit, or lift one leg, because parallel paths on the board pull an in-circuit reading low. Hold the probes on the leads rather than with your fingers on both, since skin resistance in parallel affects readings above about 100 kΩ. Blue body resistors are usually metal film (±1% or better) and beige bodies are usually carbon film (±5%), which helps you tell a 4-band from a 5-band part at a glance.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading from the wrong end.** Brown, black, red read backwards is red, black, brown, which decodes to 200 Ω instead of 1 kΩ.
- **Treating a gold third band as a digit.** Gold in the multiplier position means × 0.1, so yellow, violet, gold is 4.7 Ω, not 470 Ω.
- **Confusing red and orange, or violet and grey,** under warm workshop lighting. Use a white light and compare against a known part.
- **Assuming 4 bands means 4 significant figures.** Only the first two bands are digits; the third is always the multiplier.
- **Ignoring tolerance when matching parts.** Two 5% resistors marked 1 kΩ can differ by up to 100 Ω, which matters in dividers and bridges.
