## How to use the density calculator

Enter **mass** and **volume** to get density and specific gravity, or enter density to find mass or volume.

## Density formula

<p class="formula">ρ = m ÷ V</p>

## Worked example

A 1-litre block of steel has a mass of 7.85 kg:

ρ = 7.85 kg ÷ 0.001 m³ = **7,850 kg/m³** (specific gravity 7.85)

## The default result in other units

The default 7.85 kg in 1 L gives 7,850 kg/m³. The same density in the units you will meet on data sheets:

| Unit | Value |
|---|---|
| kg/m³ | 7,850 |
| g/cm³ (= kg/L = t/m³) | 7.85 |
| lb/ft³ | 490.1 |
| lb/in³ | 0.2836 |
| lb/US gal | 65.51 |

A quick check: g/cm³, kg/L and specific gravity are numerically the same (to within the 0.2% difference between water at 4 °C and 20 °C), and kg/m³ is always 1,000 times larger.

## Will it float?

An object floats in water if its average density is less than 1,000 kg/m³. Ice (917) floats with about 92% of its volume under water. Steel ships float because their hollow hulls make the average density lower than water.

## Density and temperature

Most materials expand when heated, so density falls. Water is unusual: it is densest at about 4 °C, which is why lakes freeze from the top down. Use [thermal expansion](/engineering/thermal-expansion/) to estimate the change for solids.

## Finding volume by displacement

For irregular objects, drop them in a measuring jug and read the rise in water level. A stone that raises the level by 120 mL has a volume of 120 cm³. Weigh it and divide to get the density.

## Specific gravity

Specific gravity is density compared with water, with no units. It is used for liquids such as acids, fuels and syrups, and for gemstones and minerals.

## Second example: a block measured with a ruler

A brass block measures 50 mm × 40 mm × 30 mm and weighs 510 g on a kitchen scale.

1. Volume = 0.050 × 0.040 × 0.030 = 60 × 10⁻⁶ m³ (60 cm³)
2. Density = 0.510 kg ÷ 0.000060 m³ = **8,500 kg/m³**, specific gravity 8.50

That sits inside the 8,400 to 8,700 kg/m³ range of common brasses, so the part is very likely brass rather than bronze or plated steel.

## How density is measured in practice

- **Archimedes weighing.** Weigh the object in air, then hanging in water. The loss of weight equals the mass of displaced water. A ring that reads 10.50 g in air and 9.50 g in water has a volume of 1.00 ÷ 0.998 = 1.002 cm³ and a density of **10.48 g/cm³**, close to pure silver (10.49) and above sterling silver (about 10.36).
- **Hydrometer.** A weighted glass float sinks until it displaces its own mass of liquid; the stem scale reads density or specific gravity directly. Used for battery acid, brewing and fuels.
- **Pycnometer.** A glass bottle of exactly known volume is weighed empty and full, which gives liquid density to about 0.01%.
- **Oscillating U-tube and Coriolis meters.** The natural frequency of a vibrating tube falls as the fluid inside gets heavier, so the instrument reports density continuously in a pipeline.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the litre factor.** 1 L is 0.001 m³, so 7.85 kg in 1 L is 7,850 kg/m³, not 7.85 kg/m³.
- **Confusing bulk and particle density.** Dry sand in a heap is about 1,600 kg/m³ while each quartz grain is 2,650 kg/m³; the 40% difference is air between the grains. Use bulk density for silos and trucks, particle density for settling.
- **Entering weight in newtons as mass.** A scale reading in kg is already mass; a force gauge reading in N must be divided by 9.81 first.
- **Ignoring temperature for liquids.** Diesel loses roughly 0.07% of its density per °C, so a fuel density quoted at 15 °C does not match a hot tank reading.
- **Measuring hollow or porous parts by their outside size.** A casting with internal voids gives a lower apparent density than the solid alloy.
