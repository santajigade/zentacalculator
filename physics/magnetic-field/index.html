    An electric current produces a magnetic field that circles a straight wire and becomes strong and nearly uniform inside a coil. This magnetic field calculator gives the flux density around a long conductor and inside a solenoid, plus the field strength H.

    Students, coil designers and instrument technicians use it to size solenoid valve coils, check stray fields near busbars and set the range of a Hall-effect sensor.

    How to use the magnetic field calculator

    For a straight wire, enter the current and the distance from the conductor’s centre. For a solenoid, enter the same current, the number of turns, the coil length and the relative permeability of the core. The calculator returns both flux densities in millitesla and the solenoid’s field strength H.

    Magnetic field formulas

    Straight wire: B = μ0 I ÷ (2π r)

    Solenoid: B = μ0 μr N I ÷ L  ·  H = N I ÷ L

    μ0 = 1.25663706212 × 10⁻⁶ H/m (≈ 4π × 10⁻⁷).

    Example

    10 A flowing in a long straight wire, measured 10 mm away; and the same current through a 500-turn, 10 cm air-core solenoid:

    • Wire: B = 1.2566 × 10⁻⁶ × 10 ÷ (2π × 0.01) = 0.200 mT (200 µT)
    • Solenoid: H = 500 × 10 ÷ 0.1 = 50,000 A/m
    • Solenoid: B = 1.2566 × 10⁻⁶ × 500 × 10 ÷ 0.1 = 62.83 mT

    For comparison, the Earth’s magnetic field is about 50 µT and a fridge magnet about 5–10 mT.

    Notes

    • The wire formula assumes a long, straight conductor and a point outside it. Inside a round conductor, B rises linearly from zero at the centre.
    • The field of two conductors carrying equal and opposite currents (a cable’s live and neutral) largely cancels, which is why twisted pairs pick up little interference.
    • The force on a conductor or a moving charge in this field is given by the Lorentz force calculator.

    Short coils: correcting the long solenoid formula

    B = μ0 N I ÷ L assumes the coil is much longer than it is wide. At the centre of a real coil of diameter D the field is reduced by the factor L ÷ √(L² + D²). For the default 500-turn, 10 cm coil carrying 10 A:

    Coil diameter L ÷ D Centre factor B at centre B at each end
    1 cm 10.00 99.5% 62.52 mT 31.38 mT
    2 cm 5.00 98.1% 61.61 mT 31.26 mT
    3 cm 3.33 95.8% 60.18 mT 31.07 mT
    5 cm 2.00 89.4% 56.20 mT 30.48 mT
    10 cm 1.00 70.7% 44.43 mT 28.10 mT

    A slim 2 cm coil reaches 98.1% of the ideal 62.83 mT, but a coil as wide as it is long gives only about 71%. For short coils, use the corrected value or measure it.

    Measuring the field

    A Hall-effect gaussmeter is the usual instrument: its probe gives a voltage proportional to the field component perpendicular to the sensor face, so it must be held square to the field lines. A search coil connected to an integrating meter measures AC fields. As a reference, the Earth contributes about 50 µT, which is 25% of the default wire reading of 200 µT and must be subtracted when measuring weak DC fields.

    Energy stored in the field

    The energy density of a magnetic field is B² ÷ (2μ0). Inside the default air-core solenoid at 62.83 mT that is 1,571 J/m³. The same expression, read as a pressure, is the force per unit area that pulls a solenoid plunger into the coil, which is why doubling the current gives about four times the pull.

    Exposure reference level

    The ICNIRP 2010 guidelines set a general public reference level of 200 µT for 50 Hz fields. The default case, 10 A at 10 mm, sits right at that value, but the field falls with distance: 100 A drops to 200 µT at 100 mm. In a cable the live and neutral fields largely cancel, so real readings are far lower.

    Common mistakes

    • Measuring r from the conductor surface. The formula uses the distance to the centre; for a 4 mm² wire at 10 mm from the surface, r is about 11.1 mm.
    • Applying the long solenoid formula to a short coil. It overstates the centre field, as the table above shows.
    • Trusting a large μr. μr = 1,000 on the default coil predicts 62.8 T, but iron saturates near 1.5 to 2 T and an open core gives far less gain than its material μr.
    • Using total wire length for L. L is the length of the winding along the axis, not the length of copper in the coil.
    • Adding fields from several conductors as plain numbers. Fields are vectors; opposite currents subtract.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the magnetic field around a straight wire?

    B = μ₀ I ÷ (2π r), with μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m. A 10 A current gives 0.2 mT (200 µT) at 10 mm — about four times the Earth's field.

    What is the magnetic field inside a solenoid?

    B = μ₀ μr N I ÷ L for a long coil (length much greater than diameter). 500 turns on a 10 cm air-core coil with 10 A give about 62.8 mT.

    How do I convert tesla to gauss?

    1 T = 10,000 G, so 1 mT = 10 G and 1 µT = 0.01 G. The Earth's field is about 25–65 µT (0.25–0.65 G).

    Why is the field at the end of a solenoid weaker?

    At each open end of a long solenoid the flux density drops to about half the central value, because the field lines spread out.

    Is the magnetic field near a current-carrying cable a health concern?

    At mains frequency the ICNIRP 2010 general public reference level is 200 µT. A single conductor with 10 A reaches that at 10 mm, but in a normal cable the opposing currents cancel most of the field, so typical household exposure is well under 1 µT.
