## How to use the Coulomb's law calculator

Enter the two **charges** in microcoulombs (use a minus sign for a negative charge) and the **distance** between their centres. The calculator gives the **force** in newtons — negative for attraction, positive for repulsion — the **electric field** that charge 1 produces at the position of charge 2, and the **potential energy** of the pair. To find the distance for a given force, enter the force and press × on Distance; the same works for either charge.

## Coulomb's law formula

<p class="formula">F = k q₁ q₂ ÷ r² &nbsp;·&nbsp; E = k q₁ ÷ r² &nbsp;·&nbsp; U = k q₁ q₂ ÷ r</p>

with k = 1 ÷ (4π ε₀) = 8.9875517862 × 10⁹ N·m²/C², charges in coulombs and r in metres. The formula is exact for point charges and for spheres with a uniform charge, measured between centres.

## Worked example

+2 µC and −3 µC, 10 cm apart:

- F = 8.988 × 10⁹ × (2 × 10⁻⁶) × (−3 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 0.1² = **−5.3925 N** — attractive, about the weight of a 0.55 kg mass
- Field of charge 1 at 10 cm: E = 8.988 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.1² = **1.798 × 10⁶ N/C**
- Potential energy: U = 8.988 × 10⁹ × (2 × 10⁻⁶) × (−3 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 0.1 = **−0.5393 J** (negative: work is needed to pull them apart)

## Electric force versus gravity

In a hydrogen atom the electron and proton, 52.9 pm apart, attract each other with **8.24 × 10⁻⁸ N**. The electric force between them is about **2.3 × 10³⁹** times their gravitational attraction — gravity dominates on large scales only because matter is almost exactly neutral.

## Practical notes

- **1 coulomb is a huge static charge** — about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons. Charges from rubbing or static build-up are usually nanocoulombs to a few microcoulombs.
- For charges spread over large or close conductors, the point-charge formula is only approximate; use the [electric field calculator](/physics/electric-field/) or a capacitance model instead.
- Electrostatic forces drive dust precipitators, laser printers, powder coating and the ESD that damages electronic parts.

## Second scenario: changing the spacing

With the same +2 µC and −3 µC charges, the attraction falls to exactly 1 N at r = √(k |q₁ q₂| ÷ 1 N) = **23.22 cm**. Pulling the charges from 10 cm to 30 cm apart raises the potential energy from −0.5393 J to −0.1798 J, so you must do **0.3595 J** of work. Separating them completely takes **0.5393 J**, which is the potential energy with its sign reversed.

### Adding a third charge

Forces from several charges add as vectors. Place a +1 µC charge 5 cm beyond the −3 µC charge on the same line. Charge 1 pulls the −3 µC charge back with 5.393 N, while the +1 µC charge, only 5 cm away, pulls it forward with k × 1 µC × 3 µC ÷ 0.05² = 10.785 N. The net force is **5.393 N** towards the third charge, even though its charge is half that of charge 1, because it is twice as close.

## How much charge is realistic

A +2 µC charge means 1.248 × 10¹³ electrons removed, and −3 µC means 1.872 × 10¹³ electrons added. Air breaks down at roughly 3 MV/m, so a charged sphere can only hold a charge whose surface field k q ÷ a² stays below that. The table shows the field each charge makes 10 cm away and the smallest sphere radius that can hold it in dry air without sparking:

| Charge | Electrons | Field at 10 cm | Smallest sphere radius in air |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 nC | 6.24 × 10⁹ | 8.99 × 10² N/C | 0.17 cm |
| 10 nC | 6.24 × 10¹⁰ | 8.99 × 10³ N/C | 0.55 cm |
| 100 nC | 6.24 × 10¹¹ | 8.99 × 10⁴ N/C | 1.73 cm |
| 1 µC | 6.24 × 10¹² | 8.99 × 10⁵ N/C | 5.47 cm |

So the default +2 µC needs a sphere at least 7.7 cm in radius. For comparison, a person charged to 10 kV by walking on carpet, with a body capacitance of about 150 pF, carries Q = C V = 1.5 µC spread over the whole body.

## How electrostatic force is measured

Coulomb found the inverse square law in 1785 with a torsion balance, reading the twist of a fine wire as small charged spheres repelled each other. Today charge is measured with a Faraday cup connected to an electrometer, which collects the whole charge of an object and reads it as a voltage on a known capacitance. Field meters (field mills) measure the field near charged surfaces in clean rooms and powder handling plants, and the force between charges then follows from F = q E.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving charges in microcoulombs.** Each µC is 10⁻⁶ C, so the product q₁ q₂ carries 10⁻¹²; forgetting it gives a force a million million times too large.
- **Entering the distance in centimetres as metres.** 10 cm is 0.1 m, and because r is squared, using 10 makes the force 10,000 times too small.
- **Reading the sign as a direction on an axis.** Here a negative result only means attraction; to get the direction on a diagram, decide which way each charge is pulled and add the forces as vectors.
- **Using the point charge formula for close conductors.** Charges on nearby metal objects redistribute (induction), so the real force differs from k q₁ q₂ ÷ r² when the gap is similar to the object size.
- **Measuring r from surfaces instead of centres.** For charged spheres the distance is centre to centre, which is the gap plus both radii.
