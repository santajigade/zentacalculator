## How to use the drag force calculator

Enter the **fluid density**, the **speed** of the object relative to the fluid, its **drag coefficient** and its **frontal area**. You get the drag force and the power needed to overcome it at that speed. Clear any field to solve for it instead — for example, enter a measured drag force and clear C<sub>d</sub> to find the drag coefficient.

## Drag equation

<p class="formula">F<sub>d</sub> = ½ ρ v² C<sub>d</sub> A</p>

<p class="formula">P = F<sub>d</sub> × v = ½ ρ v³ C<sub>d</sub> A</p>

## Example

A car with C<sub>d</sub> = 0.30 and 2.2 m² frontal area at 100 km/h (27.78 m/s) in sea-level air:

- F = 0.5 × 1.225 × 27.78² × 0.30 × 2.2 = **311.9 N**
- P = 311.9 × 27.78 = **8.66 kW** just to push the air aside

## Drag force on 1 m² of frontal area in sea-level air

| Shape | Cd | 50 km/h | 100 km/h | 150 km/h |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flat plate (normal to flow) | 1.28 | 151.2 N | 604.9 N | 1,361.1 N |
| Cube | 1.05 | 124.1 N | 496.2 N | 1,116.5 N |
| Upright cyclist | 0.9 | 106.3 N | 425.3 N | 957.0 N |
| Sphere (subcritical) | 0.47 | 55.5 N | 222.1 N | 499.8 N |
| Typical car | 0.3 | 35.4 N | 141.8 N | 319.0 N |
| Streamlined car | 0.25 | 29.5 N | 118.2 N | 265.8 N |
| Streamlined body | 0.04 | 4.7 N | 18.9 N | 42.5 N |

Drag coefficients are typical values; the real Cd depends on Reynolds number, surface finish and exact shape.

## Notes

- Air density falls with altitude and temperature (about 1.0 kg/m³ at 2,000 m), reducing drag.
- At very low Reynolds numbers (dust, fine droplets) use [Stokes' law](/physics/stokes-law/) instead.
- Terminal velocity of a falling object is where drag equals weight: v = √(2 m g ÷ (ρ C<sub>d</sub> A)).

## How speed changes force, power and energy

For the default car (C<sub>d</sub> 0.30, 2.2 m², sea level air), drag force rises with v² and drag power with v³. The last column is the energy spent on air drag alone per 100 km driven, which is force × distance:

| Speed | Drag force | Drag power | Drag energy per 100 km |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 km/h | 78.0 N | 1.08 kW | 2.2 kWh |
| 80 km/h | 199.6 N | 4.44 kW | 5.5 kWh |
| 100 km/h | 311.9 N | 8.66 kW | 8.7 kWh |
| 120 km/h | 449.2 N | 14.97 kW | 12.5 kWh |
| 130 km/h | 527.1 N | 19.04 kW | 14.6 kWh |
| 150 km/h | 701.8 N | 29.24 kW | 19.5 kWh |

At 100 km/h the car spends 8.7 kWh per 100 km on air; at 130 km/h that rises to 14.6 kWh, because energy per distance follows the force, not the power.

## Wind, altitude and other real conditions

- **Headwind.** Drag uses speed relative to the air. Driving at 100 km/h into a 20 km/h headwind, the air speed is 120 km/h, so the force is 449.2 N and the power at the wheels is 449.2 × 27.78 = 12.48 kW, against 8.66 kW in still air.
- **Altitude.** At about 2,000 m, air density is near 1.007 kg/m³ and the same car at 100 km/h meets only 256.4 N.
- **Cyclists.** An upright rider (C<sub>d</sub> 0.9, 0.36 m²) at 36 km/h needs 198 W against air; a time trial position (C<sub>d</sub> 0.7, 0.30 m²) cuts that to 129 W.
- **Terminal velocity.** An 80 kg skydiver in a belly position (C<sub>d</sub> ≈ 1.0, A ≈ 0.7 m²) stops accelerating at √(2 × 80 × 9.807 ÷ (1.225 × 1.0 × 0.7)) = 42.8 m/s, about 154 km/h.

## Finding C<sub>d</sub> from a measurement

C<sub>d</sub> values come from wind tunnel balances, coast down tests (the car rolls in neutral and the deceleration is logged) or CFD. If a coast down test shows 400 N of aerodynamic force at 120 km/h on a 2.2 m² car, then C<sub>d</sub> = 400 ÷ (0.5 × 1.225 × 33.33² × 2.2) = **0.267**. In the calculator, enter the force and clear C<sub>d</sub> to get the same result.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering km/h as if it were m/s.** The formula needs m/s; 100 km/h is 27.78 m/s. The calculator converts for you, but hand calculations often skip it and come out 13 times too high.
- **Using the wrong reference area.** A C<sub>d</sub> quoted for a wing uses plan area, a car uses frontal area; mixing them can be off by a factor of 5 or more.
- **Ignoring rolling resistance.** Drag is only part of the road load. Below about 60 km/h, tyre rolling resistance is often larger than air drag for a car.
- **Applying one C<sub>d</sub> at every Reynolds number.** A sphere drops from about 0.47 to roughly 0.1 past the drag crisis, which is why dimpled golf balls fly further.
- **Forgetting water is 800 times denser than air.** The same shape and speed in water feels about 816 times the force, so marine sensors and pipe probes see large loads at low speeds.
