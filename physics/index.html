    For homework, exams and real problems

    The calculators cover the core of school and university physics — mechanics, thermodynamics, electricity, waves and astrophysics. Units switch between SI and imperial, and each page has worked examples and reference tables.

    Mechanics: from SUVAT to orbits

    Most school and first-year physics problems are mechanics. The SUVAT calculator solves any of the five constant-acceleration equations once you know three values, and the projectile motion calculator splits a launch into horizontal and vertical parts to give range, height and flight time. For forces, start with Newton's second law, then add friction, an inclined plane or drag. Energy problems are often quicker: equate kinetic and potential energy and the path the object took no longer matters. For planets and satellites, use gravitational force, orbital period and escape velocity.

    Heat, waves and light

    Thermal tools cover specific heat and final temperature, latent heat, conduction through a wall and Newton's law of cooling, which is how forensic scientists and food engineers estimate cooling times. For waves, the wavelength and frequency calculator links v = fλ, the Doppler effect calculator gives the pitch shift of a moving source, and the decibel calculator handles sound and signal levels. Optics is covered by Snell's law and the thin lens equation.

    Electricity, magnetism and modern physics

    Start circuits with Ohm's law and resistors in series and parallel, then move on to the RC time constant and parallel plate capacitor. Fields are covered by Coulomb's law, electric field, magnetic field, the Lorentz force and Faraday's law. For twentieth-century physics there are photon energy, de Broglie wavelength, E = mc², time dilation and radioactive activity.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do the physics calculators show the working?

    Yes. Press “Show steps” to see the formula and how the answer was found.

    Can I use different units?

    Yes. Each field has a unit selector, and values convert automatically.

    Which physics calculator should I use for a falling object?

    Without air resistance, use the free fall calculator (g = 9.81 m/s²). If the object is light or fast, such as a raindrop or a skydiver, air drag matters, so combine the drag force calculator with Newton's second law.

    What value of g is used?

    Standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s², unless the calculator lets you enter your own value, for example for the Moon (1.62 m/s²) or Mars (3.71 m/s²).
