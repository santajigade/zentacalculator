  - q: Why is power 10 log but voltage 20 log?
    a: Power is proportional to voltage squared, so 10 log(V²) = 20 log(V). A tenfold voltage increase is a hundredfold power increase — both 20 dB.

    a: A doubling of power (or halving for −3 dB). For voltage, doubling is about 6 dB.
  - q: How do I add gains in a signal chain?
    a: Add the dB values. An amplifier of +20 dB followed by a cable loss of −3 dB gives +17 dB overall.
  - q: What is dBm?
    a: Power relative to 1 mW. 0 dBm = 1 mW, 30 dBm = 1 W.

## How to use the decibel calculator

Use the top section for **power** ratios and the bottom for **voltage** (or current, or sound pressure) ratios. Enter the reference and the measured value to get decibels, or enter dB and a reference to find the other value.

## Decibel formulas

<p class="formula">dB = 10 log₁₀(P₂ ÷ P₁) &nbsp;·&nbsp; dB = 20 log₁₀(V₂ ÷ V₁)</p>

| Sound | Level |

| Threshold of hearing | 0 dB |

| Busy traffic | 80 dB |
| Hearing damage risk with long exposure | 85 dB and above |
| Rock concert | 110 dB |
| Pain threshold | 120–130 dB |

Every 10 dB increase sounds roughly twice as loud and carries ten times the sound power.

## Worked example with the default values

- Power: P₂ ÷ P₁ = 100 ÷ 1 = 100, and 10 × log₁₀(100) = 10 × 2 = **20.00 dB**
- Voltage: V₂ ÷ V₁ = 10 ÷ 1 = 10, and 20 × log₁₀(10) = 20 × 1 = **20.00 dB**

Both give 20 dB because a tenfold voltage across the same resistance delivers a hundredfold power. The two results only agree like this when both voltages are measured across equal impedances.

## Adding gains along a signal chain

| Stage | Gain |
|---|---|
| Preamplifier | +26.0 dB |
| Cable | -2.5 dB |
| Splitter | -3.5 dB |
| Power amplifier | +14.0 dB |
| **Total** | **+34.0 dB** |

The overall power gain is 10<sup>3.4</sup> = 2,512 times. Working the same chain with plain ratios needs four multiplications (398.1 × 0.562 × 0.447 × 25.12); in decibels it is one sum.

## From watts to dBm, dBV and dBu

An amplifier delivering 0.5 W into an 8 Ω speaker produces V = √(P R) = 2.00 V RMS. That is 10 log(500 mW ÷ 1 mW) = **27.0 dBm** (or -3.0 dBW), 20 log(2.00 ÷ 1 V) = **6.02 dBV** and 20 log(2.00 ÷ 0.7746 V) = **8.24 dBu**.

## Adding sound levels

Decibel levels of separate sources do not add arithmetically. Two machines at 80 dB each give 10 log(10<sup>8</sup> + 10<sup>8</sup>) = **83.0 dB**, not 160 dB. A 90 dB source plus an 85 dB source gives 91.2 dB, so the quieter one barely changes the total.

## How decibels are measured

RF engineers read dBm directly on spectrum analysers and power meters, audio engineers read dBu or dBFS on mixing desk meters, and sound level meters show dB SPL with A weighting (dBA) to match human hearing. In each case the instrument does the 10 log or 20 log against its own fixed reference.

## Common mistakes

- **Using 20 log for power.** Power and energy ratios use 10 log; only field quantities such as voltage, current and sound pressure use 20 log.
- **Comparing voltages across different impedances.** 1 V across 600 Ω and 1 V across 50 Ω are very different powers, so a 20 log voltage ratio is not a power ratio there.
- **Dropping the reference.** "+10 dB" is only a ratio; dBm, dBV and dBu each name a reference, and 0 dBu is 0.7746 V, not 1 V.
- **Adding dB SPL of separate sources** as plain numbers instead of adding their powers.
- **Treating 3 dB as "a bit".** It is double or half the power, which is a big change in a link budget.
