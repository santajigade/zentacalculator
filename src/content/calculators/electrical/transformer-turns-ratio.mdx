## How to use the transformer turns ratio calculator

Enter the **primary voltage**, **primary turns** and **secondary turns**, plus the **secondary load current**. You get the turns ratio, secondary voltage and primary current. Fill any consistent set: for example, both voltages and primary turns to find secondary turns.

For more background, read [Isolation Transformer Working and Uses](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/isolation-transformer-working-uses/).

## The ideal transformer equations

<p class="formula">V<sub>p</sub> ÷ V<sub>s</sub> = N<sub>p</sub> ÷ N<sub>s</sub> = I<sub>s</sub> ÷ I<sub>p</sub></p>

## Example

230 V primary, 1,000 turns; 52 secondary turns:

- Ratio = 1,000 ÷ 52 = 19.23
- Secondary voltage = 230 ÷ 19.23 = **11.96 V**
- With 5 A secondary load, the primary draws 5 ÷ 19.23 = **0.26 A**

## Turns per volt

Small transformers are often designed by turns per volt, which depends on core area and flux density: turns/V ≈ 1 ÷ (4.44 × f × B × A). For a 50 Hz core with 10 cm² area at 1.2 T, that is about 3.75 turns per volt.

## Three-phase ratios

For three-phase transformers, the voltage ratio uses line voltages, and the vector group affects the turns ratio:

| Connection | Line voltage ratio |
|---|---|
| Yy or Dd | Np ÷ Ns |
| Dy (delta primary, star secondary) | Np ÷ (√3 × Ns) |
| Yd (star primary, delta secondary) | √3 × Np ÷ Ns |

A Dyn11 11 kV/433 V distribution transformer has a phase-winding turns ratio of 11,000 ÷ 250 = 44.

## Real transformers

On load, the secondary voltage drops slightly because of winding resistance and leakage reactance (regulation, typically 2% to 5%). Transformers are often wound with a few percent extra turns to compensate.

## Worked example with the default values

The defaults are a **230 V primary with 1,000 turns**, a **52 turn secondary** and a **5 A load**:

1. Turns ratio a = 1,000 ÷ 52 = **19.231**
2. Secondary voltage Vs = 230 ÷ 19.231 = **11.96 V**
3. Primary current Ip = 5 ÷ 19.231 = **0.260 A**
4. Power check: 11.96 V × 5 A = 59.8 VA on the secondary, and 230 V × 0.260 A = 59.8 VA on the primary, equal as they must be for an ideal transformer.

A real 12 V winding is usually given about 3% extra turns so that it still delivers 12 V on full load. Here that means 53.6, rounded to 54 turns, and the no-load voltage then rises to around 12.3 V with the original 52 turns.

## Taps on a distribution transformer

An 11 kV/433 V Dyn11 unit has a secondary phase voltage of 433 ÷ √3 = 250.0 V, so the phase turns ratio is 44.00. Off-circuit taps on the HV winding change that ratio. With the supply held at exactly 11 kV, each tap gives:

| HV tap | Phase turns ratio | LV line voltage |
|---|---|---|
| +5.0% | 46.20 | 412.4 V |
| +2.5% | 45.10 | 422.4 V |
| +0.0% | 44.00 | 433.0 V |
| -2.5% | 42.90 | 444.1 V |
| -5.0% | 41.80 | 455.8 V |

Raising the HV tap adds primary turns and so lowers the secondary voltage. When the incoming supply is high, the operator moves to a plus tap to bring the LV back to nominal.

## How the turns ratio is measured

A transformer turns ratio (TTR) tester applies a low AC voltage, typically 8 V to 100 V, to the HV winding and measures the voltage induced on the LV winding with a bridge or precision voltmeter. It reports the ratio, the phase angle deviation and the excitation current for each tap and each phase. IEC 60076-1 allows a ratio deviation of 0.5% of the declared ratio (or a tenth of the impedance voltage, whichever is smaller) at the principal tap. A wrong ratio on one phase points to shorted turns or a tap changer contact fault.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing line and phase voltages.** On a Dy or Yd transformer the winding ratio differs from the line voltage ratio by √3, as the table above shows.
- **Measuring the ratio on load.** Regulation lowers the secondary voltage under load, so a ratio taken from loaded voltages reads a few percent high.
- **Forgetting the current goes the other way.** A 19:1 step-down transformer draws about one nineteenth of the secondary current from the primary, not 19 times more.
- **Using the ratio for a DC supply.** A transformer only works on AC; DC through the primary just heats the winding and saturates the core.
- **Ignoring magnetising current.** Even with no load the primary draws a small current to magnetise the core, so a very light load gives a primary current above Is ÷ a.
