## How to use the margin of error calculator

Choose the **confidence level** and enter the **sample size** and **observed proportion**. The margin of error appears as ± percentage points.

## Margin of error formula

<p class="formula">E = z × √( p(1 − p) ÷ n )</p>

## Worked example

A poll of 1,000 people finds 50% support, at 95% confidence:

E = 1.96 × √(0.25 ÷ 1000) = **±3.1%**

## Margin of error at different proportions

n = 1,000, 95% confidence:

| Observed proportion | Margin of error |
|---|---|
| 5% | ±1.35% |
| 10% | ±1.86% |
| 20% | ±2.48% |
| 30% | ±2.84% |
| 50% | ±3.10% |
| 70% | ±2.84% |
| 90% | ±1.86% |

The margin is largest at 50%, which is why planners use 50% when they do not know the result in advance.

## Finite population correction

When the sample is a large share of the population (more than about 5%), multiply the margin by:

<p class="formula">√((N − n) ÷ (N − 1))</p>

A sample of 500 from a population of 2,000 has its margin reduced by about 13%.

## What the margin of error does not cover

- **Biased samples**: surveying only people online or only volunteers.
- **Non-response**: people who refuse may differ from those who answer.
- **Question wording** and order effects.
- **Small subgroups**: a poll of 1,000 has a much larger margin for a subgroup of 150.

## Comparing two results

If two candidates poll 48% and 45% with a ±3.1% margin each, the margin on the **difference** is larger — roughly 1.7 to 2 times the single margin. A 3-point lead is within the margin of error.

## Planning a survey

To find the sample size for a target margin, use the [sample size calculator](/statistics/sample-size/).

## Margin by sample size (p = 50%, 95%)

| Sample size | Margin of error |
|---|---|
| 100 | ±9.8% |
| 400 | ±4.9% |
| 1,000 | ±3.1% |
| 2,500 | ±2.0% |

## How the default result is built

The calculator starts at 95% confidence (z = 1.959964), n = 1,000 and p = 50%:

- Standard error = √(0.5 × 0.5 ÷ 1,000) = 0.01581
- Margin = 1.959964 × 0.01581 = **±3.10 percentage points**

So a reported 50% means roughly 46.9% to 53.1%.

## Worked example: the lead and a subgroup

Back to the poll with 48% for A and 45% for B among the same 1,000 people. The margin on the 3 point lead is not ±3.1 but z × √((p₁ + p₂ − (p₁ − p₂)²) ÷ n) = 1.96 × √((0.48 + 0.45 − 0.03²) ÷ 1,000) = **±6.0 points**. The lead could be anything from about -3 to +9 points.

If the same poll breaks out the 150 respondents aged 18 to 24, their margin is 1.96 × √(0.25 ÷ 150) = **±8.0 points**, so subgroup findings need far more caution than the headline.

## Confidence level and sample size together

Margin at p = 50%:

| Confidence | z | n = 400 | n = 1,000 | n = 2,500 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 90% | 1.645 | ±4.1% | ±2.6% | ±1.6% |
| 95% | 1.960 | ±4.9% | ±3.1% | ±2.0% |
| 99% | 2.576 | ±6.4% | ±4.1% | ±2.6% |

Moving from 95% to 99% confidence widens the margin by about a third at any sample size.

## Weighting and the design effect

Real polls are weighted so the sample matches the population by age, sex, region and so on. Weighting makes the estimate less precise, measured by the design effect. With a design effect of 1.5, the 1,000 interviews behave like 667 simple random ones and the margin grows to **±3.8 points**. Reputable pollsters publish this adjusted margin; you can reproduce it here by entering n ÷ design effect as the sample size.

## Rare outcomes and small samples

The formula is a normal approximation and breaks down when n × p is below about 10. For 3% in a sample of 100, it gives ±3.3 points, implying a lower limit of -0.3%, which is impossible. The Wilson score interval gives 1.0% to 8.5% instead, lopsided because the true value cannot go below zero. Use the [confidence interval calculator](/statistics/confidence-interval/) or an exact method for such cases.

## Common mistakes

- **Applying the single margin to a lead.** The gap between two candidates in one poll has a margin close to double the headline figure.
- **Quoting the full sample margin for a subgroup.** Margins depend on the subgroup's own n.
- **Mixing percent and percentage points.** ±3% on a 40% result means 37% to 43%, not 38.8% to 41.2%.
- **Ignoring weighting.** An unweighted margin overstates the precision of a weighted poll.
- **Using it on opt-in online panels.** Without random selection there is no sampling distribution, so a margin of error is not strictly meaningful.
