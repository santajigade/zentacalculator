## How to use the debt-to-Income ratio calculator

Add up **all monthly debt payments** — loan EMIs, minimum credit-card payments and other fixed obligations — and enter your **gross monthly income** (before tax). The calculator shows your debt-to-income ratio and how much more you could borrow each month while staying at 36%.

## Debt-to-Income ratio formula

<p class="formula">DTI = total monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income × 100</p>

## How lenders read DTI

| DTI | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Below 20% | Excellent — plenty of room |
| 20–36% | Healthy — most lenders are comfortable |
| 36–43% | Acceptable for many mortgages, with fewer options |
| 43–50% | High — approval harder, higher rates likely |
| Above 50% | Very high — little room for new credit |

## Front-end and back-end ratios

US mortgage lenders often look at two ratios:

- **Front-end (housing) ratio**: housing payment ÷ income, often 28% or less.
- **Back-end ratio**: all debts including housing ÷ income — this calculator.

The well-known **28/36 rule** combines both.

## FOIR in India

Indian banks use FOIR on **net** (take-home) income:

| Net monthly income | Typical maximum FOIR |
|---|---|
| Lower incomes | 40–50% |
| Higher incomes | 50–65% |

So with a take-home of ₹1,00,000 and a 50% FOIR, total EMIs can be up to ₹50,000 a month. Use the [EMI calculator](/finance/emi/) to see what loan that supports.

## Example

Monthly EMIs of 1,500 and a gross income of 6,000:

- DTI = 1,500 ÷ 6,000 = **25%**
- Room at 36% = 2,160 − 1,500 = **660 a month** for new payments

## Worked example: front-end ratio and a new car loan

The default 1,500 of monthly debt is made up of a 1,100 mortgage payment, a 280 car loan and a 120 minimum card payment, against 6,000 of gross income:

- Back-end DTI = 1,500 ÷ 6,000 = **25%**
- Front-end (housing) ratio = 1,100 ÷ 6,000 = **18.3%**, inside the 28% guideline
- Adding a second car loan of 450 a month raises DTI to 1,950 ÷ 6,000 = **32.5%**, still under 36%
- The most a lender capping at 43% would allow in new payments is 0.43 × 6,000 − 1,500 = **1,080 a month**

## Maximum total debt payment by income

Monthly payment ceilings for common lender limits (any currency):

| Gross monthly income | 28% | 36% | 43% | 50% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3,000 | 840 | 1,080 | 1,290 | 1,500 |
| 5,000 | 1,400 | 1,800 | 2,150 | 2,500 |
| 8,000 | 2,240 | 2,880 | 3,440 | 4,000 |
| 12,000 | 3,360 | 4,320 | 5,160 | 6,000 |

Subtract what you already pay to see the room for a new loan.

## FOIR on take-home pay: an Indian home loan

Indian lenders apply FOIR to net salary after tax and PF. Take a gross salary of ₹1,00,000 with a take-home of ₹82,000 and existing EMIs of ₹15,000:

- Current FOIR = 15,000 ÷ 82,000 = **18.3%**
- At a 50% FOIR cap the total EMI allowed is ₹41,000, leaving **₹26,000** for a new EMI
- At 8.5% for 20 years that EMI supports a home loan of about **₹29,96,002**

Closing the ₹15,000 of existing EMIs before applying would add roughly ₹17,28,463 to the eligible loan.

## What lenders actually count

Lenders work from your credit report and payslips or tax returns. Every EMI and credit card on the report counts, including loans you have guaranteed or co-signed. For self-employed applicants and bonus-heavy salaries they average income over two years, so a single strong year rarely raises eligibility on its own.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing gross and net income.** US style DTI uses gross pay, while Indian FOIR uses take-home pay; the same debts give a higher FOIR than DTI.
- **Leaving out small or shared debts.** Minimum card payments, buy now pay later plans and co-signed loans all appear on the credit report.
- **Adding living costs.** Groceries, utilities and insurance premiums are not debt payments and do not belong in the ratio.
- **Forgetting the new loan.** The ratio a lender checks includes the EMI of the loan you are applying for.
- **Using one month of variable income.** Commission or freelance income is usually averaged, so use a 12 to 24 month average.
