    Two resistors set the closed-loop gain of an operational amplifier, in either the inverting or the non-inverting form. This op-amp gain calculator returns the gain as a ratio and in dB plus the ideal output, and finds Rf or Rin for a target gain.

    Circuit designers, students and instrument technicians use it to scale sensor signals for ADCs and check stages. Start with op-amp basics, the voltage follower and how an instrumentation amplifier differs from an op-amp.

    How to use the op-amp gain calculator

    Choose non-inverting or inverting, and enter the feedback resistor, the input resistor and an input voltage. You get the gain (as a ratio and in dB) and the ideal output. To pick a resistor for a target gain, enter the gain and press × on Rf or Rin.

    Op-amp gain formulas

    Configuration Gain
    Non-inverting G = 1 + Rf ÷ Rin
    Inverting G = −Rf ÷ Rin
    Voltage follower G = 1

    Input impedance is very high for the non-inverting amplifier and equal to Rin for the inverting amplifier.

    Worked example

    Non-inverting amplifier with Rf = 100 kΩ, Rin = 10 kΩ and a 100 mV input:

    • Gain = 1 + 100 ÷ 10 = 11, or 20 log₁₀(11) = 20.83 dB
    • Output = 11 × 100 mV = 1.1 V

    Switch to inverting with the same resistors: gain = −100 ÷ 10 = -10 (20.00 dB) and the output is -1 V.

    Resistor pairs for common gains

    Target gain Non-inverting Rf / Rin → actual gain Inverting Rf / Rin → actual gain
    2 10 k / 10 k → 2 20 k / 10 k → −2
    5 40.2 k / 10 k → 5.02 49.9 k / 10 k → −4.99
    10 90.9 k / 10 k → 10.09 100 k / 10 k → −10
    11 100 k / 10 k → 11 110 k / 10 k → −11
    101 100 k / 1 k → 101 100 k / 990 Ω → −101

    Standard E96 (1%) values get within about 1% of most targets; use 0.1% resistors when the gain must be precise.

    Bandwidth

    Every op-amp has a gain-bandwidth product (GBW): the closed-loop bandwidth is roughly GBW ÷ noise gain, where the noise gain is 1 + Rf/Rin for both configurations. An inverting amplifier with a gain of −1 therefore has the bandwidth of a gain-of-2 stage.

    Noise gain Bandwidth, GBW = 1 MHz Bandwidth, GBW = 10 MHz
    1 1,000.0 kHz 10,000.0 kHz
    2 500.0 kHz 5,000.0 kHz
    10 100.0 kHz 1,000.0 kHz
    11 90.9 kHz 909.1 kHz
    100 10.0 kHz 100.0 kHz
    1000 1.0 kHz 10.0 kHz

    Instrumentation amplifiers

    For bridge sensors and thermocouples, use an instrumentation amplifier, which rejects common-mode voltage and sets gain with a single resistor.

    Practical notes

    • Keep resistors roughly 1 kΩ–1 MΩ: low values load the op-amp output and the source, high values add noise and offset from input bias current.
    • The inverting amplifier’s input impedance is only Rin — buffer high-impedance sensors or use the non-inverting form.
    • Check the output swing against the rails and the slew rate (a 10 V peak at 20 kHz needs about 1.3 V/µs).

    How accurate is the default gain of 11 in a real circuit?

    The ideal formula gives 11 and 1.1 V out for the default 100 kΩ, 10 kΩ and 100 mV. Four real effects pull it away from that:

    1. Resistor tolerance. With 1% parts the worst case is 1 + 101 ÷ 9.9 = 11.202 and 1 + 99 ÷ 10.1 = 10.802, about ±1.8% of 11.
    2. Bandwidth. On a 1 MHz GBW op-amp the closed-loop bandwidth is 1 MHz ÷ 11 = 90.9 kHz. At 20 kHz the gain has already fallen to 11 ÷ √(1 + (20 ÷ 90.9)²) = 10.743, 2.3% low, even though 20 kHz is well inside the −3 dB point.
    3. Offset voltage. A 1 mV input offset is multiplied by the noise gain, so the output sits 11 mV away from zero with no signal, 1% of the 1.1 V output.
    4. Bias current. 100 nA of bipolar input bias current flowing through Rf adds 10 mV. A 9.09 kΩ resistor (Rf ∥ Rin) in series with the non-inverting input balances the two inputs so only the much smaller offset current matters.

    A 0.5 V/µs slew rate limits a full 1.1 V peak sine to about 72 kHz before the output turns triangular.

    Worst-case gain error from resistor tolerance

    Gain Non-inverting, 5% Non-inverting, 1% Non-inverting, 0.1% Inverting, 1%
    2 5.3% 1.01% 0.100% 2.02%
    11 9.6% 1.84% 0.182% 2.02%
    101 10.4% 2.00% 0.198% 2.02%

    The inverting stage always carries the full error of both resistors, while in the non-inverting stage the fixed “1” dilutes it at low gain.

    Measuring the gain on the bench

    Feed a sine from a signal generator at a frequency at least ten times below the expected bandwidth, with an amplitude that keeps the output well inside the rails, and read input and output on the same scope or true RMS meter. To measure offset, short the input to ground, read the output and divide by the noise gain.

    Common mistakes

    • Confusing the two forms. With the same pair, the inverting stage gives −10, not −11; the “1 +” belongs only to the non-inverting formula.
    • Expecting full gain right up to GBW ÷ G. The gain is already 3 dB down at that frequency and a few percent low a decade below it at high gain.
    • Ignoring the output swing. An input of 1 V at gain 11 asks for 11 V, which a 5 V rail-to-rail part clips at about 4.9 V.
    • Leaving Rin out of the source impedance. In the inverting stage the source resistance adds to Rin, so a 1 kΩ source cuts the gain from −10 to −9.09.
    • Using megohm resistors to save current. Bias current, noise and stray capacitance across Rf all grow, and a few pF across 1 MΩ forms a pole at tens of kHz.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the gain of a non-inverting amplifier?

    G = 1 + Rf ÷ Rin, where Rin is the resistor from the inverting input to ground. 100 kΩ and 10 kΩ give a gain of 11.

    What is the gain of an inverting amplifier?

    G = −Rf ÷ Rin. The minus sign means the output is inverted. 100 kΩ and 10 kΩ give −10.

    Why is my output lower than calculated?

    The output cannot swing beyond the supply rails (minus a headroom of 0.1–2 V depending on the op-amp), and gain falls at high frequency due to the gain-bandwidth product.

    What is a voltage follower?

    A non-inverting amplifier with Rf = 0 and no Rin — a gain of exactly 1. It buffers a high-impedance source.

    Why is my op-amp output not zero when the input is zero?

    Input offset voltage and bias current are amplified. A 1 mV offset in a gain of 11 stage gives about 11 mV at the output, and 100 nA of bias current through a 100 kΩ feedback resistor adds about 10 mV unless the inputs see matched resistances.

    How much do 1% resistors change the op-amp gain?

    In the worst case for a non-inverting gain of 11, one resistor high and the other low, the gain ranges from 10.802 to 11.202, about ±1.8%. Use 0.1% resistors or a matched network when gain accuracy matters.
