## How to use the retirement corpus calculator

Enter your **current annual expenses**, expected **inflation**, **years until retirement** and a **withdrawal rate**. The calculator projects your expenses at retirement and the corpus needed to fund them.

## Retirement corpus formulas

<p class="formula">Expenses at retirement = E × (1 + inflation)<sup>t</sup><br/>Corpus = Expenses at retirement ÷ withdrawal rate</p>

## Worked example

Annual expenses of 600,000 today, 6% inflation, retiring in 25 years, 4% withdrawal rate:

- Expenses at retirement = 600,000 × 1.06²⁵ = **2,575,122 per year**
- Corpus needed = 2,575,122 ÷ 0.04 = **64,378,061**

That large figure shows how strongly inflation compounds over decades.

## Withdrawal rate and corpus multiple

| Withdrawal rate | Corpus = expenses × |
|---|---|
| 5% | 20 |
| 4% | 25 |
| 3.5% | 28.6 |
| 3% | 33.3 |

## How to reach the corpus

1. Note the corpus from the calculator.
2. Subtract what your existing savings, EPF, PPF or pension will be worth by then.
3. Use the [SIP calculator](/finance/sip/) to find the monthly investment for the gap, or the [step-up SIP calculator](/finance/step-up-sip/) if you will raise it every year.

## Expenses that change in retirement

- **Lower**: commuting, work clothes, home loan EMI (if paid off), children's costs.
- **Higher**: healthcare and insurance, travel in the early years, help at home later.

A common shortcut is 70–80% of current expenses, but listing actual costs gives a better estimate.

## Sources of retirement income

| Source | Inflation-linked? |
|---|---|
| Withdrawals from investments (SWP) | Only if you increase them |
| Annuity / pension plan | Usually not |
| Government pension | Often partly |
| Rental income | Usually rises over time |

Combining several sources reduces the risk of any one falling short. See the [SWP calculator](/finance/swp/) to plan withdrawals.

## Important assumptions

- Investments keep growing during retirement, roughly matching inflation plus the withdrawal rate.
- Healthcare costs often rise faster than general inflation.
- Pensions or rental income reduce the corpus needed — subtract them from expenses.

This is a planning estimate, not financial advice. Consider speaking to a qualified financial planner.

## What the default answer means

With the defaults (600,000 a year of expenses, 6% inflation, 25 years, 4% withdrawal) the calculator shows expenses at retirement of **2,575,122** a year and a corpus of **64,378,061**. That looks enormous, but in today's money it is 64,378,061 ÷ 1.06^25 = **15,000,000**, exactly 25 times today's expenses. The rest of the number is inflation, not extra spending.

## From corpus to monthly saving

A target is only useful once it becomes a monthly figure. Using the same formula as the [SIP calculator](/finance/sip/), building 64,378,061 over 25 years at an assumed 12% a year needs about **33,925 a month**. If you already hold 2,000,000 that grows at 10% a year, it reaches 21,669,412 by retirement, the gap falls to 42,708,649, and the monthly saving drops to about **22,506**. These returns are assumptions for illustration, not forecasts.

## How sensitive the corpus is

Corpus needed for 600,000 of expenses today, 25 years to retirement:

| Inflation | Expenses at retirement | Corpus at 4% | Corpus at 3.5% | Corpus at 3% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 2,031,813 | 50,795,324 | 58,051,799 | 67,727,099 |
| 6% | 2,575,122 | 64,378,061 | 73,574,927 | 85,837,414 |
| 7% | 3,256,460 | 81,411,490 | 93,041,702 | 108,548,653 |

One point of inflation either way moves the 4% corpus from 50,795,324 to 81,411,490, a bigger swing than most people expect. Choosing the withdrawal rate matters just as much: going from 4% to 3% raises the target by a third.

## Building a realistic expense figure

Planners usually start from 12 months of bank and card statements rather than a guess. Split spending into essentials (food, utilities, insurance, rent or maintenance), lifestyle (travel, dining) and one-off items such as a car or a wedding. Remove costs that end at retirement, add expected medical cover, and treat big one-off goals as separate targets rather than folding them into the yearly figure.

## Common mistakes

- **Using today's expenses as the final figure.** The corpus must be built on expenses in the retirement year, which is what the inflation step does.
- **Mixing real and nominal returns.** If you plan with inflated expenses, compare them with nominal investment returns, not returns after inflation.
- **Counting EPF or pension twice.** Either subtract the pension from yearly expenses or subtract its value from the corpus, not both.
- **Treating 4% as guaranteed.** It is a historical guideline from US markets; a poor run of returns early in retirement can shorten the life of the corpus.
- **Leaving out healthcare.** Medical costs and insurance premiums often rise faster than general inflation and grow with age.
