## How to use the Boyle's law calculator

Enter **any three** of the initial and final pressure and volume. The fourth is calculated. Temperature and the amount of gas must stay constant.

## Boyle's law formula

<p class="formula">P₁ V₁ = P₂ V₂</p>

## Worked example

10 L of gas at 100 kPa is compressed to 250 kPa:

V₂ = 100 × 10 ÷ 250 = **4 L**

The product P V stays at 1,000 kPa·L before and after, which is also 1,000 J, because 1 kPa·L = 1 J. If the 100 kPa and 250 kPa were gauge readings rather than absolute pressures, the correct answer would be (100 + 101.325) × 10 ÷ (250 + 101.325) = **5.73 L**, not 4 L.

## Pressure–volume table

Starting at 100 kPa and 10 L, at constant temperature:

| Pressure | Volume |
|---|---|
| 50 kPa | 20.00 L |
| 100 kPa | 10.00 L |
| 150 kPa | 6.67 L |
| 200 kPa | 5.00 L |
| 300 kPa | 3.33 L |
| 500 kPa | 2.00 L |
| 1000 kPa | 1.00 L |

Double the pressure and the volume halves; the product P × V stays at 1,000 kPa·L.

## Second example: free air in a compressed air receiver

A 50 L receiver is charged to 7 bar gauge (801.3 kPa absolute). Expanded to atmospheric pressure at the same temperature, the air would occupy (801.3 × 50) ÷ 101.325 = **395 L** of free air. Only the part above atmospheric can leave the tank, so emptying it to 0 bar gauge releases 700 × 50 ÷ 101.325 = 345 L. Compressor capacities are quoted in free air delivery for exactly this reason.

## Everyday examples

- **Syringes**: pulling the plunger increases volume, lowering pressure, so liquid is drawn in.
- **Breathing**: your diaphragm enlarges the chest, lowering lung pressure so air flows in.
- **Bicycle pumps**: pushing the handle compresses air until its pressure exceeds the tyre's.
- **Aerosol cans**: gas expands as it leaves the higher-pressure can.

## Why temperature must be constant

Compressing a gas quickly heats it (think of a bicycle pump getting warm). Boyle's law applies to slow changes where heat can escape, keeping the temperature steady. If temperature changes too, use the [combined gas law](/chemistry/combined-gas-law/).

## Gauge vs absolute pressure

Always use **absolute** pressure. A tyre gauge reading of 200 kPa is about 301 kPa absolute at sea level. Using gauge pressure gives wrong answers.

## Diving example

In seawater (1,025 kg/m³) the absolute pressure at depth d is 101.325 kPa + ρ g d. A 6 L lungful of air taken at the surface shrinks as the diver descends:

| Depth | Absolute pressure | Atmospheres | Volume of 6 L of surface air |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 m | 101.3 kPa | 1.00 | 6.00 L |
| 5 m | 151.6 kPa | 1.50 | 4.01 L |
| 10 m | 201.8 kPa | 1.99 | 3.01 L |
| 20 m | 302.4 kPa | 2.98 | 2.01 L |
| 30 m | 402.9 kPa | 3.98 | 1.51 L |
| 40 m | 503.4 kPa | 4.97 | 1.21 L |

At 10 m the pressure is 201.8 kPa and the air occupies 3.01 L. The reverse is the danger: air breathed at depth expands on the way up, which is why divers must never hold their breath while ascending.

## Measuring Boyle's law in the lab

The classic school method traps air in a sealed syringe or a glass tube connected to a pressure gauge or electronic sensor. The plunger is moved in small steps, waiting each time for the gas to return to room temperature, and pressure and volume are recorded. A graph of P against V is a curve, but P against 1/V is a straight line through the origin whose slope is the constant P V. Readings from the syringe scale should include the dead volume of the tubing and sensor, or the line will not pass through zero.

## Common mistakes

- **Using gauge pressure.** With the default numbers read as gauge values the true answer is 5.73 L, not 4 L, an error of 30%.
- **Compressing quickly.** A fast, adiabatic squeeze from 100 to 250 kPa leaves 5.20 L, not 4 L, and heats air from 20 °C to about 108 °C; Boyle's law only holds once the gas has cooled back.
- **Letting the amount of gas change.** Leaks, gas dissolving in liquid or water vapour condensing all change n, so P V is no longer constant.
- **Applying it to liquids.** Liquids are almost incompressible; Boyle's law describes gases only.
- **Forgetting dead volume.** Tubing, valves and gauges add trapped volume that must be counted in V₁ and V₂.
