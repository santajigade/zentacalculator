## How to use the radioactive activity calculator

Enter the **mass** of the radioactive isotope itself (not the whole source capsule), its **molar mass**, its **half-life** and, optionally, an **elapsed time**. The calculator returns the decay constant, the number of atoms and the activity now and after that time, in becquerels and curies. To find the mass of isotope in a source of known activity, enter the activity and clear the mass field.

## Activity formula

<p class="formula">λ = ln 2 ÷ t<sub>½</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; N = (m ÷ M) × N<sub>A</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; A = λN</p>

<p class="formula">A(t) = A<sub>0</sub> × 2<sup>−t/t½</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; 1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq</p>

## Example

1 mg of cobalt-60 (M = 59.9338 g/mol, t<sub>½</sub> = 5.2714 years):

- λ = 0.693147 ÷ (5.2714 × 31,557,600 s) = **4.1667 × 10⁻⁹ s⁻¹**
- N = 0.001 ÷ 59.9338 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.0048 × 10¹⁹ atoms**
- A = λN = **4.1867 × 10¹⁰ Bq** = **1.132 Ci**
- After 10 years: A = 4.1867 × 10¹⁰ × 2<sup>−10/5.2714</sup> = **1.1241 × 10¹⁰ Bq** (0.304 Ci)

## Notes

- The half-life field accepts years, days, hours, minutes or seconds.
- Activity alone does not give dose — that also depends on the radiation type, its energy, distance and shielding.
- For half-life arithmetic without atoms or masses, use the [half-life calculator](/chemistry/half-life/).

## Decay of the default 1 mg Co-60 source

Starting from A<sub>0</sub> = 4.1867 × 10¹⁰ Bq (1.132 Ci), the activity after each period is A<sub>0</sub> × 2<sup>−t/5.2714</sup>:

| Elapsed time | Activity | Curies | Remaining |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 yr | 4.1867 × 10¹⁰ Bq | 1.132 Ci | 100.0% |
| 1 yr | 3.6709 × 10¹⁰ Bq | 0.992 Ci | 87.7% |
| 2 yr | 3.2186 × 10¹⁰ Bq | 0.870 Ci | 76.9% |
| 5 yr | 2.1694 × 10¹⁰ Bq | 0.586 Ci | 51.8% |
| 10 yr | 1.1241 × 10¹⁰ Bq | 0.304 Ci | 26.8% |
| 15 yr | 0.5825 × 10¹⁰ Bq | 0.157 Ci | 13.9% |
| 20 yr | 0.3018 × 10¹⁰ Bq | 0.082 Ci | 7.2% |
| 30 yr | 0.0810 × 10¹⁰ Bq | 0.022 Ci | 1.9% |

A Co-60 source loses **12.3% of its activity every year**, so it falls to 10% after 17.5 years. A Cs-137 source loses only 2.28% per year and takes 100 years to reach 10%, which is why Cs-137 is the usual choice for level and density gauges that must stay calibrated for decades.

## How activity is measured in practice

A detector never sees every decay. A Geiger-Müller tube or scintillation probe records counts per second, and the activity is the net count rate divided by the counting efficiency, after subtracting background. Efficiency depends on the radiation type, energy, geometry and window, and is set by counting a certified reference source. Counting is statistical: a 1 kBq check source read at 5% efficiency for 600 s gives about 30,000 counts, with a one-sigma uncertainty of √N, or **0.6%**. Hospitals measure patient doses in a dose calibrator, an ionisation chamber with nuclide-specific settings that reads activity directly in MBq or mCi.

The calculator also works in reverse for licensing paperwork. A 100 mCi (3.7 GBq) Cs-137 gauge source contains only about **1.15 mg** of the isotope itself; the rest of the capsule is carrier and stainless steel.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering the capsule or compound mass.** Only the mass of the radioactive isotope counts. In CsCl, caesium is about 79% of the mass, and the Cs-137 is only a fraction of that.
- **Treating activity as dose.** Becquerels count decays; dose in grays or sieverts also depends on the emitted energy, distance and shielding.
- **Ignoring short-lived daughters.** Cs-137 gamma rays actually come from Ba-137m, which is in equilibrium with its parent; gamma yield is about 85 photons per 100 Cs-137 decays.
- **Mixing up mCi and MBq.** 1 mCi is 37 MBq, so a factor of 37 error is easy when reading certificates from different countries.
- **Using the activity on the certificate as today's value.** Always decay it from the certificate reference date before calibrating an instrument.
