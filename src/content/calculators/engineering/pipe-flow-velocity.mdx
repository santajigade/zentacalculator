## How to use the pipe flow velocity calculator

Enter the **flow rate** and the pipe's **inner diameter** to get the velocity. You can also solve for the flow rate at a given velocity, or the diameter needed to keep velocity within limits — press × on the field you want calculated.

Units mix freely: flow in m³/h, L/min or US gpm; diameter in mm or inches; velocity in m/s or ft/s.

## Pipe flow velocity formula

<p class="formula">A = π D² ÷ 4   v = Q ÷ A</p>

## Worked example

50 m³/h of water in a pipe with 100 mm inner diameter:

- Q = 50 ÷ 3600 = 0.01389 m³/s
- A = π × 0.1² ÷ 4 = 0.007854 m²
- v = 0.01389 ÷ 0.007854 = **1.77 m/s**

| Compressed air | 6 – 10 m/s |

## Choosing a pipe size

Rearrange the formula to find the diameter for a target velocity:

<p class="formula">D = √(4Q ÷ (π × v))</p>

For 50 m³/h at 2 m/s: D = √(4 × 0.01389 ÷ 6.283) = **94 mm**, so DN100 is a suitable size.

## Water hammer

Fast valve closure in a pipe with high velocity creates a pressure surge. The Joukowsky equation gives the surge: ΔP = ρ × a × Δv, with a about 1,200–1,400 m/s in steel water pipes. Stopping 2 m/s instantly can create a surge of over 25 bar. Keep velocities moderate and close valves slowly.

## Why velocity matters for flow meters

Most flow meters have a velocity range. Electromagnetic meters typically need about 0.3–10 m/s, and many work best above 1 m/s. If the velocity is too low, a smaller meter (with reducers) often improves accuracy. Check the [Reynolds number](/engineering/reynolds-number/) too, since many meters need fully turbulent flow.

## Sizing a pump suction line from the default flow

The default case, 50 m³/h through a 100 mm bore, gives **1.77 m/s** and a cross-section of 78.54 cm², which is fine for a discharge line but too fast for a pump suction. Targeting 1.5 m/s:

- D = √(4 × 0.01389 ÷ (π × 1.5)) = **108.6 mm** minimum bore
- DN100 schedule 40 (102.26 mm bore) would run at 1.69 m/s, so the next size up is used
- DN125 schedule 40 (128.2 mm bore) gives **1.08 m/s**, comfortably inside the suction range

The same flow squeezed through a DN50 reducer (52.5 mm bore) speeds up to 6.42 m/s, since velocity scales with the inverse square of the bore. The velocity head v²/2g at the default 1.77 m/s is 0.159 m, which is the head lost at a pipe exit into a tank.

## How velocity is measured in practice

The calculator gives the mean velocity, flow divided by area. A clamp on ultrasonic meter measures it directly from transit time across the pipe, but you must type the real outside diameter and wall thickness into it, or the flow it reports is wrong by the same area error. A pitot tube or insertion probe reads a point velocity; in fully developed turbulent flow the centreline velocity is roughly 1.2 times the mean, so a single centre reading overstates flow unless a profile factor is applied. To find the true wall thickness on an old line, use an ultrasonic thickness gauge rather than the schedule tables, because corrosion and scale change the bore.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the nominal size as the bore.** DN100 schedule 80 has a 97.2 mm bore, and a cement lined ductile iron pipe of the same DN is smaller still.
- **Mixing m³/h with m³/s.** A factor of 3,600 error usually shows up as an impossible velocity of thousands of metres per second.
- **Applying the full bore area to a partly full drain.** Gravity drains and sewers seldom run full, so the wetted area is smaller and the real velocity differs.
- **Using normal m³/h for a gas line.** Compressors and gas meters often quote flow at reference conditions; at 7 bar gauge the actual volume is about one eighth, and so is the velocity.
- **Sizing on average flow only.** Check the peak flow too, because noise and erosion follow the highest velocity the line will see.
