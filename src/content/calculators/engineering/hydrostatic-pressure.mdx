## How to use the hydrostatic pressure & tank level calculator

Enter the **liquid height** and **density** to get the hydrostatic pressure at the bottom. To set up a level transmitter, enter the maximum level to get its upper range value. To read level from a pressure gauge, press × on the level and type the pressure.

## Hydrostatic pressure & tank level formula

<p class="formula">P = ρ × g × h</p>

- **ρ** — liquid density (kg/m³)
- **g** — standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s²
- **h** — height of liquid above the measuring point (m)

## Worked examples

**Water tank, 5 m level**

P = 1000 × 9.80665 × 5 = 49,033 Pa = **0.490 bar** (7.11 psi)

**Diesel tank (ρ ≈ 832 kg/m³), 3 m level**

P = 832 × 9.80665 × 3 = 24,477 Pa = **0.245 bar**

The same pressure would show only 2.5 m on a transmitter calibrated for water — always range for the real density.

## Pressure at the bottom of a tank

Total pressure at the bottom = liquid pressure + pressure above the liquid. In a **closed** tank, the vapour or blanket pressure adds to the reading, which is why level in pressurised tanks is measured with a **differential pressure** transmitter connected to both the bottom and the top.

## Density changes with temperature

Water at 80 °C has a density of about 972 kg/m³, nearly 3% less than at 20 °C. A level transmitter calibrated for cold water will read about 3% low on hot water. Compensate in the DCS or recalibrate for the operating temperature.

## Interface level

When two immiscible liquids are present (oil on water), a DP transmitter measures the combined effect. If the total level is known, the interface position can be calculated from the two densities.

## Ranging a level transmitter

1. Calculate the pressure at minimum level (often 0) and maximum level.
2. If the transmitter is mounted below the tank nozzle, add the liquid head between the nozzle and the transmitter (zero elevation).
3. Enter these as the 4 mA and 20 mA values — then use the [4–20 mA scaling calculator](/engineering/4-20ma-scaling/) to check readings.

A useful rule: **1 m of water ≈ 0.098 bar ≈ 1.42 psi ≈ 9.81 kPa**.

## Default result in other units

The default 5 m of water at 1,000 kg/m³ gives 49,033 Pa = **0.4903 bar** = 49.03 kPa = 7.11 psi gauge. On an absolute scale, adding standard atmospheric pressure of 1.01325 bar gives 1.504 bar(a), which is the figure needed for a sealed absolute transmitter or for gas solubility work.

## Worked example: transmitter below the tank nozzle

A gauge transmitter sits 1.2 m below the bottom nozzle of an open water tank, with the impulse line full of water. At empty tank it already sees 1.2 m of head: 1,000 × 9.80665 × 1.2 = 11,768 Pa = **0.1177 bar**. At the 5 m maximum it sees 6.2 m, **0.6080 bar**. So the range is set to 0.1177 to 0.6080 bar, an elevated zero. Ranging it 0 to 0.4903 bar instead would make an empty tank read 24% full.

## Wrong density, wrong level

If the same 0.4903 bar is produced by a brine of 1,200 kg/m³, the true level is 49,033 ÷ (1,200 × 9.80665) = **4.17 m**, not 5 m. A transmitter ranged for water over-reads by 20%. Whenever the product changes, recalculate the range with the new density.

## Does local gravity matter?

Gravity varies from about 9.78 m/s² at the equator to 9.832 m/s² at the poles. The 5 m water column gives 48,900 Pa at the equator and 49,160 Pa at the poles, a 0.5% spread. That is below the accuracy of most level loops, so standard gravity is used, but custody transfer tank gauging applies local g.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring from the tank bottom instead of the tapping point.** Level below the transmitter tapping is invisible to it; h is the height above the measuring point.
- **Ignoring the gas pressure in a closed tank.** A gauge transmitter at the bottom of a pressurised vessel reads blanket pressure plus liquid head; use a DP transmitter referenced to the top.
- **Treating mH2O and metres of liquid as the same.** 1 mH2O is defined with water at 1,000 kg/m³; 1 m of diesel produces only about 0.84 mH2O.
- **Mixing gauge and absolute.** Adding or forgetting 1.013 bar shifts the reading by about 10.3 m of apparent water level, double the whole 5 m range.
- **Leaving gas in a liquid filled impulse line.** A bubble in the leg changes the head between tank and transmitter and gives an offset that drifts with temperature.
