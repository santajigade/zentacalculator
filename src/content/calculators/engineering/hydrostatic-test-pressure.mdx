faq:
  - q: How is hydrostatic test pressure calculated?
    a: For a pressure vessel to ASME VIII Div 1 (UG-99), test pressure = 1.3 × MAWP × (stress at test temperature ÷ stress at design temperature), using the lowest ratio of the materials in the vessel. For process piping to ASME B31.3, P_T = 1.5 × P × S_T ÷ S, with the ratio not taken above 6.5.
  - q: Why is the test pressure 1.5 times the design pressure?
    a: The margin proves the strength and leak-tightness of the welds and joints with a safety allowance over normal operation. ASME VIII-1 used 1.5 until the 1999 addenda lowered it to 1.3 when the design margin on tensile strength was reduced; B31.3 piping still uses 1.5.
  - q: Where is the test pressure measured?
    a: ASME VIII-1 specifies the test pressure at the top of the vessel in its test position. The bottom sees the test pressure plus the water head, about 0.098 bar per metre, which must not over-stress any part.
  - q: What range should the test pressure gauge have?
    a: ASME VIII-1 UG-102 says the dial range should preferably be about double the test pressure, and never less than 1.5 times or more than 4 times it. Gauges must be calibrated, and a recorder or second gauge is common practice.
  - q: "Why is a pneumatic test more dangerous than a hydrostatic test?"
    a: "Gas stores energy as it compresses and releases it all if the vessel fails. A 2 m³ vessel at 14.4 bar g holds about 4.1 MJ as air but only about 1 kJ as water."
  - q: "Why does the pressure rise during a hydrotest when nothing is added?"
    a: "Water expands as it warms, and a full, rigid system has no room for it. Sunshine on a pipeline or vessel can raise the gauge reading by several bar per degree, so monitor temperature and keep a bleed valve ready."

## How to use the hydrostatic test pressure calculator

Enter the **design pressure or MAWP**, choose the **code and test type**, and enter the **allowable stress** of the material at the test temperature and at the design temperature (the same value for ambient services). Add the **height of the lowest point** below the test gauge and the **test fluid density** to see the pressure at the bottom of the system. The calculator also gives the test gauge ranges allowed by UG-102.

## Hydrostatic test pressure formulas

<p class="formula">ASME VIII-1 UG-99: P<sub>T</sub> = 1.3 × MAWP × (S<sub>T</sub> ÷ S) &nbsp;·&nbsp; ASME B31.3: P<sub>T</sub> = 1.5 × P × (S<sub>T</sub> ÷ S)</p>

<p class="formula">Pneumatic: P<sub>T</sub> = 1.1 × P × (S<sub>T</sub> ÷ S) &nbsp;·&nbsp; P<sub>bottom</sub> = P<sub>T</sub> + ρ g h</p>

In B31.3 the stress ratio is capped at 6.5, and the test pressure is reduced if it would make the hoop stress exceed the yield strength at test temperature. Under the European PED (2014/68/EU) the vessel test pressure is at least the greater of 1.25 × the maximum allowable pressure (corrected for temperature) and 1.43 × PS.

## Worked example

A vessel with MAWP 10 bar g at a design temperature where S = 125 MPa, tested with water at ambient (S<sub>T</sub> = 138 MPa) to ASME VIII-1; the bottom is 6 m below the gauge:

- Stress ratio = 138 ÷ 125 = **1.1040**
- Test pressure = 1.3 × 10 × 1.1040 = **14.35 bar g** at the top
- At the bottom: 14.35 + 1000 × 9.807 × 6 ÷ 10⁵ = **14.94 bar g**
- Test gauge: at least **21.5 bar**, preferably about **28.7 bar** (a 0–25 or 0–40 bar gauge), and not more than **57.4 bar**

## Practical notes

- **Hold time:** B31.3 requires at least 10 minutes at test pressure; ASME VIII-1 then lowers the pressure to at least test pressure ÷ 1.3 for the visual inspection of all joints.
- **Metal temperature:** keep it at least 17 °C (30 °F) above the minimum design metal temperature during a VIII-1 test, but not over 48 °C (120 °F) for close inspection, to avoid brittle fracture.
- **Vent the high points** before pressurising — trapped air makes a hydrotest store energy like a pneumatic test.
- **Isolate instruments**, relief valves and anything with a lower rating, following a written [hydrotest procedure for piping](ib:hydrotest-procedure-for-piping-system), and [select a calibrated test gauge](ib:pressure-gauge-selection-7-factors) with the right range.
- A pneumatic test stores far more energy than a hydrotest; use it only when water cannot be used, with an exclusion zone and a staged pressure increase.

## Second example: a B31.3 piping test with a tall riser

A process line designed for 20 bar g has an allowable stress of 118 MPa at design temperature and 138 MPa at the ambient test temperature. The test gauge is at grade and the lowest point is a drain 15 m below it on a pipe rack riser:

- Stress ratio = 138 ÷ 118 = **1.1695**
- Test pressure = 1.5 × 20 × 1.1695 = **35.08 bar g** at the gauge
- At the low point: 35.08 + 1000 × 9.807 × 15 ÷ 10⁵ = **36.56 bar g**
- Gauge range between 52.6 and 140.3 bar, preferably about 70.2 bar, so a 0 to 60 bar gauge suits

The 1.47 bar of head at the drain is added to the pressure the low point sees. Check that the flanges and valves down there are rated for 36.6 bar g, and that the hoop stress there stays below yield, as B31.3 requires.

## Why a pneumatic test needs extra care

A pneumatic test of the default vessel needs only 12.14 bar g, but compressed air stores far more energy than water. For a 2 m³ vessel at the hydrotest pressure of 14.35 bar g, the water stores about **0.9 kJ** (from its compressibility), while air at the same pressure would release about **4.1 MJ** if the vessel failed, over 4,432 times as much. That is why pneumatic tests use a lower factor, staged pressurisation, a hold at half the test pressure and an exclusion zone.

## How the test is run

Fill from the low point while venting at every high point until water runs clear of air. Raise the pressure slowly with a test pump, with a calibrated gauge or recorder at the top and a second gauge on the pump. Hold the full test pressure for the specified time, then on an ASME VIII-1 vessel lower it to at least 11.04 bar g (test pressure ÷ 1.3) for the close visual inspection of every weld and joint. Keep watch on the water temperature: in a rigid, completely full system, warming by the sun can raise the pressure by several bar for each degree.

## Common mistakes

- **Setting the test pressure at the low point gauge.** ASME VIII-1 defines the test pressure at the top; a gauge at the bottom reads high by the water head.
- **Leaving out the stress ratio.** For hot services S<sub>T</sub> ÷ S is above 1, and a test at plain 1.3 or 1.5 times design pressure is too low.
- **Testing against closed valves or relief valves.** Valve seats and relief valves are not test boundaries; use blinds and remove or isolate the relief devices.
- **Choosing a gauge far over range.** A 0 to 100 bar gauge on a 14 bar test reads in the bottom seventh of the scale, where small errors and pointer friction dominate.
- **Draining without venting.** Opening the drain with the high point vent shut can pull a vacuum and collapse thin tanks or large thin wall pipe.
