## How to use the MOSFET power dissipation calculator

Enter the **RMS drain current**, the **Rds(on)** at operating temperature, the **off-state voltage**, the **switching frequency**, the total **rise + fall time**, the **thermal resistance** to ambient (or to the heat sink path) and the **ambient temperature**. You get conduction loss, switching loss, total loss and the estimated junction temperature.

## MOSFET loss formulas

<p class="formula">P<sub>cond</sub> = I<sub>RMS</sub>² × R<sub>DS(on)</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; P<sub>sw</sub> ≈ ½ × V × I × (t<sub>r</sub> + t<sub>f</sub>) × f</p>

<p class="formula">T<sub>j</sub> = T<sub>a</sub> + P<sub>total</sub> × R<sub>θJA</sub></p>

## Example

10 A, 20 mΩ, 24 V, 50 kHz, 100 ns total transition time:

- Conduction loss = 100 × 0.02 = **2.0 W**
- Switching loss = 0.5 × 24 × 10 × 100 ns × 50 kHz = **0.6 W**
- Total = **2.6 W**; with 40 °C/W and 40 °C ambient, T<sub>j</sub> ≈ **144 °C** → add a heat sink

## Reducing losses

- Choose lower Rds(on) — but larger MOSFETs have more gate charge and slower switching.
- Use a proper gate driver to switch in tens of nanoseconds.
- Lower the switching frequency where ripple allows.
- Improve cooling: copper area on the PCB, a heat sink or airflow. See the [heat sink calculator](/electronics/heat-sink/).

## Not included

Gate-drive loss (Q<sub>g</sub> × V<sub>gs</sub> × f), body-diode conduction and reverse-recovery losses add more in synchronous converters.

## Second example: sizing the heat sink

The default case reaches 144 °C, too hot for a reliable design. To hold the junction at 100 °C with 2.6 W of loss and 40 °C ambient:

- Total thermal resistance allowed = (100 − 40) ÷ 2.6 = **23.08 °C/W**
- Subtract junction to case (1.0 °C/W) and the thermal pad (0.5 °C/W)
- The heat sink must be **21.58 °C/W** or better

Enter 23.08 in the thermal resistance field to confirm 100 °C. Because R<sub>DS(on)</sub> falls as the device cools, the real loss is slightly lower, so this is a safe answer.

## How frequency shifts the balance

Same 10 A, 20 mΩ, 24 V and 100 ns, with no heat sink (40 °C/W):

| Frequency | Conduction | Switching | Total | T<sub>j</sub> |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 kHz | 2.00 W | 0.24 W | 2.24 W | 130 °C |
| 50 kHz | 2.00 W | 0.60 W | 2.60 W | 144 °C |
| 100 kHz | 2.00 W | 1.20 W | 3.20 W | 168 °C |
| 200 kHz | 2.00 W | 2.40 W | 4.40 W | 216 °C |
| 500 kHz | 2.00 W | 6.00 W | 8.00 W | 360 °C |

Conduction dominates up to about 167 kHz, where switching loss equals it. Above that, a faster gate driver is worth more than a lower R<sub>DS(on)</sub>.

## Measuring the real loss

Fit a thermocouple to the tab, run the converter at full load until the temperature settles, then estimate T<sub>j</sub> = T<sub>case</sub> + P × R<sub>θJC</sub>. For the switching loss itself, capture V<sub>DS</sub> and I<sub>D</sub> on an oscilloscope with a current probe, deskew the channels, and integrate their product over each transition.

## Common mistakes

- **Using average current instead of RMS.** A 50% duty PWM drawing 14.14 A peak has an RMS of 10 A but an average of only 7.07 A; squaring the average halves the conduction loss.
- **Using R<sub>θJA</sub> with a heat sink fitted.** The datasheet R<sub>θJA</sub> assumes a small copper pad; with a sink, add R<sub>θJC</sub>, interface and sink values.
- **Forgetting output capacitance loss.** ½C<sub>oss</sub>V²f with 500 pF is only 7.2 mW at 24 V but 2.0 W at 400 V and 50 kHz.
- **Counting gate charge as MOSFET heat.** Q<sub>g</sub> × V<sub>GS</sub> × f, here 15 mW for 30 nC at 10 V, is dissipated mostly in the driver and gate resistor.
- **Expecting parallel devices to halve every loss.** Two MOSFETs sharing 10 A dissipate 0.5 W conduction each, half the total, but each still switches the full voltage at its share of current.
