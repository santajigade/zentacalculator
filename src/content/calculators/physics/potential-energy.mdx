## How to use the potential energy calculator

Enter the **mass** and **height** to get the potential energy, or enter the energy to find the height. Choose J, kJ, kWh or other energy units.

## Potential energy formula

<p class="formula">PE = m × g × h   (g = 9.80665 m/s²)</p>

## Worked example

1,000 kg (1 m³) of water held 50 m above a turbine:

PE = 1,000 × 9.80665 × 50 = 490,332 J = **490.3 kJ** = 0.1362 kWh

Each metre of height adds m g = 9,807 J for this tonne of water. Released through a turbine and generator with 88% overall efficiency, the 0.1362 kWh becomes about 0.120 kWh of electricity. Dropped freely, the same water would hit the bottom at √(2 g h) = 31.32 m/s, because all of the 490.3 kJ has turned into kinetic energy.

### How high for one kilowatt hour?

Turning the formula round, h = PE ÷ (m g). To store 1 kWh (3.6 MJ) in one tonne you must lift it 3,600,000 ÷ (1,000 × 9.80665) = **367.1 m**. That is why gravity storage needs very large masses or very large height differences.

## Energy and power for a lift

The calculator gives energy; dividing by time gives power. Raising the default 1,000 kg through 50 m in 40 s needs 490.3 kJ ÷ 40 s = 12.26 kW of mechanical power. With a 70% efficient motor and gearbox the electrical demand is about 17.5 kW. Counterweights in real lifts balance the car and part of the load, so the motor only lifts the difference.

| Situation | Mass | Height | Energy |

| Person climbing a floor | 70 kg | 3 m | 2,059 J (0.0006 kWh) |
| Water in a rooftop tank | 1,000 kg | 15 m | 147,100 J (0.0409 kWh) |
| Lift car with passengers | 1,500 kg | 30 m | 441,299 J (0.1226 kWh) |
| Water behind a 100 m dam (1 m³) | 1,000 kg | 100 m | 980,665 J (0.2724 kWh) |

## Hydroelectric power

Hydro plants turn water's potential energy into electricity:

<p class="formula">P = ρ × g × Q × h × η</p>

with Q the flow in m³/s, h the head in metres and η the efficiency (often 0.85–0.9). A flow of 10 m³/s through a 50 m head at 88% efficiency gives about **4.3 MW**.

## Pumped storage

Pumped-storage hydro stores energy by pumping water uphill when electricity is cheap and releasing it through turbines when demand is high. 1 m³ of water raised 100 m stores only about 0.27 kWh, so these schemes move very large volumes.

## Elastic potential energy

Springs store energy too: E = ½ × k × x², where k is the spring constant and x the stretch or compression. A spring with k = 2,000 N/m compressed by 5 cm stores 2.5 J.

## Energy conservation

If dropped, the object's potential energy turns into kinetic energy. Ignoring air resistance, its speed at the bottom is v = √(2gh) — for 50 m that is 31.3 m/s. Check it with the [free fall calculator](/physics/free-fall/).

## Height is measured from a reference

Potential energy is always relative to a chosen zero level, such as the floor or the turbine.

## How g changes the answer

The calculator uses standard gravity, 9.80665 m/s². The same 1,000 kg raised 50 m stores:

| Location | g | Potential energy |
|---|---|---|
| Earth (standard) | 9.80665 m/s² | 490.3 kJ |
| Earth at the equator | 9.78 m/s² | 489.0 kJ |
| Earth at the poles | 9.832 m/s² | 491.6 kJ |
| Moon | 1.62 m/s² | 81.0 kJ |
| Mars | 3.71 m/s² | 185.5 kJ |
| Jupiter | 24.79 m/s² | 1,239.5 kJ |

Across Earth's surface the spread is only about half a percent, which is smaller than most height measurement errors. On the Moon the same lift takes about a sixth of the energy.

## When m g h stops being accurate

The formula assumes g is constant over the height range, which is excellent for buildings, dams and mountains. For large heights use the exact change G M m (1 ÷ R<sub>1</sub> − 1 ÷ R<sub>2</sub>). Lifting a mass to 400 km altitude, the exact value is only R ÷ (R + h) = 0.941 of m g h (taking g = G M ÷ R² at the surface), so the simple formula overstates the energy by about 6.3%.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring height to the wrong point.** Use the vertical rise of the centre of mass. Counting a 2 m tall, 1 tonne crate from its top instead of its middle adds 9.8 kJ that is not really there.
- **Using the length of a slope.** Only vertical height counts; a 100 m ramp at 30° raises a load 50 m.
- **Entering grams or pounds as kilograms.** The SI formula needs kilograms, metres and m/s² to give joules.
- **Mixing kJ and kWh.** 1 kWh is 3,600 kJ, so 490.3 kJ is only 0.136 kWh, not 490 kWh.
- **Treating potential energy as absolute.** Only differences matter, so pick one reference level and use it for every height in the problem.
