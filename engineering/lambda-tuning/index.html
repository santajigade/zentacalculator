    Lambda tuning sets a PI controller so the closed loop responds like a first order lag with a time constant λ that you choose, fast or gentle, with no overshoot. This lambda tuning calculator uses process gain, time constant and dead time from a step test and compares the result with Cohen-Coon.

    Control engineers use it to tune flow, pressure and temperature loops predictably, as covered in PID controller tuning parameters and how to tune a PID controller.

    How to use the lambda tuning calculator

    Enter the process gain K, time constant τ and dead time θ of a first-order-plus-dead-time (FOPDT) model, plus the desired closed-loop time constant λ. The calculator gives the lambda (IMC) PI settings and the Cohen–Coon PI settings for comparison. Gains are dimensionless (% output per % PV); integral time is in seconds per repeat.

    Lambda (IMC) PI tuning

    Kc = τ ÷ [K × (λ + θ)]  ·  Ti = τ

    Cohen–Coon PI tuning

    Kc = (1/K)(τ/θ)(0.9 + θ/12τ)  ·  Ti = θ(30 + 3θ/τ) ÷ (9 + 20θ/τ)

    Example

    K = 2, τ = 60 s, θ = 10 s, λ = 30 s:

    • Lambda PI: Kc = 60 ÷ (2 × 40) = 0.750, Ti = 60 s
    • Cohen–Coon PI: Kc = 2.742, Ti = 24.7 s

    The Cohen–Coon gain is more than three times higher — expect overshoot. Lambda tuning gives a smooth, non-oscillating response.

    Effect of λ on controller gain (same process)

    λ Kc Ti
    10 s (1 θ) 1.500 60 s
    20 s (2 θ) 1.000 60 s
    30 s (3 θ) 0.750 60 s
    60 s (6 θ) 0.429 60 s
    120 s (12 θ) 0.231 60 s

    Notes

    • These rules are for self-regulating processes (flow, temperature, pressure). Integrating processes such as tank level need the integrating-process lambda rules.
    • Flow loops are often tuned with λ of 1–3 × τ; temperature loops with λ = τ or slower.
    • Coordinate λ across cascaded loops: the inner (slave) loop should be at least 3–5 times faster than the outer loop.

    Worked example: entering the settings in a controller

    For the default process (K = 2, τ = 60 s, θ = 10 s) and λ = 30 s the calculator gives Kc = 0.750 and Ti = 60 s. Controllers use different units, so convert before typing:

    • Proportional band = 100 ÷ 0.750 = 133%
    • Integral time = 60 s = 1 min per repeat, or 1 repeat per minute
    • Expected response: after a set point step, the PV starts to move after the 10 s dead time and reaches 63% of the change about λ + θ = 40 s after the step, with no overshoot.

    Cohen-Coon for the same process gives Kc = 2.742 and Ti = 24.7 s, a far more aggressive loop.

    Integrating processes such as tank level

    A level loop has no steady state gain; instead the PV ramps at a rate Ki (% per second for each % of output). The integrating lambda rule, with λ as the arrest time, is:

    Kc = (2λ + θ) ÷ [Ki (λ + θ)²]  ·  Ti = 2λ + θ

    For a tank with Ki = 0.01 %/s per % and θ = 20 s:

    Arrest time λ Kc PB Ti
    100 s 1.53 65% 220 s (3.7 min)
    200 s 0.87 115% 420 s (7.0 min)
    300 s 0.61 165% 620 s (10.3 min)
    600 s 0.32 315% 1220 s (20.3 min)
    900 s 0.22 465% 1820 s (30.3 min)

    A surge tank is tuned slowly (large λ) so the outlet flow stays smooth; a reactor level that must stay tight uses a short λ. With λ = 300 s the settings are Kc = 0.61 and Ti = 620 s.

    Second example: heat exchanger outlet temperature

    A steam heated exchanger is step tested: the outlet temperature moves 1.5 % of span per % of valve, with τ = 180 s and θ = 30 s. Temperature loops are usually tuned for robustness, so take λ = τ = 180 s:

    • Kc = 180 ÷ (1.5 × (180 + 30)) = 0.571, a proportional band of 175%
    • Ti = τ = 180 s (3.0 min per repeat)
    • After a set point step the outlet should reach 63% of the change about 210 s later, without overshoot
    • Cohen-Coon would give Kc = 3.656 and Ti = 74.2 s, a loop that would fight the slow steam valve

    How dead time changes the comparison

    Same process gain (2) and time constant (60 s), λ set to 3θ in each case:

    Dead time θ θ ÷ τ λ = 3θ Lambda Kc Cohen-Coon Kc Cohen-Coon Ti Ratio
    5 s 0.08 15 s 1.500 5.442 14.2 s 3.6 ×
    10 s 0.17 30 s 0.750 2.742 24.7 s 3.7 ×
    30 s 0.50 90 s 0.250 0.942 49.7 s 3.8 ×
    60 s 1.00 180 s 0.125 0.492 68.3 s 3.9 ×
    120 s 2.00 360 s 0.062 0.267 88.2 s 4.3 ×

    The lambda gain falls with dead time while Ti stays at τ; Cohen-Coon stays nearly four times more aggressive across the range.

    Robustness to a wrong process gain

    If the real process gain is twice the step test value, the loop with Kc = 0.750 behaves as if λ had been set to 10 s instead of 30 s: faster, with less margin. Choosing λ of 3θ or more leaves room for that kind of error, which is common when valve gain varies across the stroke.

    Common mistakes

    • Using K in engineering units. K must be % of PV span per % of output. A gain of 4 °C per % output on a 0 to 200 °C span is 2% per %.
    • Mixing integral time units. Typing 60 into a controller that expects minutes per repeat sets Ti to an hour.
    • Choosing λ shorter than the dead time. The loop then loses robustness quickly; λ of at least 3θ is a safer start.
    • Applying the self-regulating rule to a level loop. Integrating processes need the separate rule above.
    • Testing once at one operating point. Valve characteristics change K across the range, so step test at low and high loads and tune for the worst case.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the lambda tuning formula?

    For a self-regulating process, Kc = τ ÷ (K × (λ + θ)) and Ti = τ. λ is the closed-loop time constant you want.

    How do I choose lambda?

    Start with λ equal to the process time constant (or three times the dead time, whichever is larger). Smaller λ gives a faster but less robust loop; larger λ is slower and more tolerant of model error.

    Why does Cohen–Coon give a much higher gain?

    Cohen–Coon aims for quarter-amplitude decay, so it is aggressive and sensitive to model error. It is mainly useful for loops with dead time that is large compared with the time constant.

    Where do K, τ and θ come from?

    From an open-loop step test — use the [FOPDT step test calculator](/engineering/fopdt-step-test/) to extract them from the 28.3% and 63.2% response times.

    How do I tune a level loop with lambda tuning?

    Use the integrating rule: Kc = (2λ + θ) ÷ (Ki (λ + θ)²) and Ti = 2λ + θ, where Ki is the ramp rate of the level in % per second per % of output and λ is the arrest time you want after a disturbance.

    How do I check that the loop responds as designed?

    Make a small set point step in automatic. The PV should start to move after the dead time, reach about 63% of the step at λ + θ (here 40 s) and settle without overshoot. A faster or oscillating response means the process gain or dead time was underestimated.

    Does lambda tuning work when dead time is longer than the time constant?

    Yes. The gain falls automatically because λ + θ grows. For K = 2, τ = 60 s and θ = 120 s with λ = 360 s, Kc = 60 ÷ (2 × 480) = 0.0625. Keep λ at least equal to θ; for strongly dead time dominant loops some engineers also set Ti a little above τ.
