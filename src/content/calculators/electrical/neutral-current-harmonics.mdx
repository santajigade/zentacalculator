  - q: Why does the neutral carry third-harmonic current?
    a: The 50 Hz currents of the three phases are 120° apart and cancel in the neutral. Third-harmonic currents of the three phases are all in phase (3 × 120° = 360°), so they add, and the neutral carries three times the third-harmonic current of one phase.
  - q: Can the neutral current be higher than the phase current?
    a: Yes. With pure third harmonics the neutral current exceeds the phase current once the third harmonic is about 33% of the phase RMS current (about 35% of the fundamental). Circuits feeding many single-phase rectifier loads commonly reach 1.4–1.7 × phase current.
  - q: What are the IEC 60364-5-52 reduction factors for harmonics?
    a: For four- and five-core cables, Annex E gives 1.0 (size on phase current) up to 15% third harmonic, 0.86 (phase current) for 15–33%, 0.86 (neutral current) for 33–45%, and 1.0 (neutral current) above 45%, where the harmonic is a percentage of the phase current.
  - q: Do other harmonics add in the neutral?
    a: Only triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th, 15th …) add. The 5th, 7th, 11th and 13th cancel in a balanced system, like the fundamental. Load unbalance adds a further fundamental-frequency neutral current.

## How to use the neutral current calculator

Enter the **phase RMS current** and the **third-harmonic current as a percentage of the fundamental** (the value a power-quality analyser shows in its harmonic spectrum). The calculator splits the phase current into its fundamental and third-harmonic parts, gives the neutral current, re-expresses the harmonic as a percentage of the phase RMS current (the basis IEC 60364-5-52 uses) and returns the design current for choosing a four-core or five-core cable.

## Neutral current formulas

<p class="formula">I₁ = I ÷ √(1 + h₃²) &nbsp;·&nbsp; I₃ = h₃ × I₁ &nbsp;·&nbsp; I<sub>N</sub> = 3 × I₃</p>

This assumes balanced phases and that the third harmonic is the only significant triplen harmonic. Other odd harmonics raise the phase RMS slightly but cancel in the neutral.

## Worked example

Phase current 100 A with a third harmonic of 30% of the fundamental:

- Fundamental I₁ = 100 ÷ √(1 + 0.3²) = **95.78 A**, third harmonic I₃ = **28.73 A**
- Neutral current I<sub>N</sub> = 3 × 28.73 = **86.2 A** (0.862 × phase current)
- As a share of the phase RMS current the harmonic is **28.7%**, which falls in the 15–33% band
- Size the cable on the phase current with a factor of 0.86: design current = 100 ÷ 0.86 = **116.3 A**

## Practical notes

- **Do not use a reduced neutral** on circuits feeding LED drivers, IT equipment, UPS or single-phase VFDs; many designers use a full-size or double-size neutral for such loads.
- The IEC factors apply to four- and five-core cables where the neutral is inside the same cable and adds heat; for single-core cables, check the neutral on its own current.
- Measure the harmonic spectrum at full load — percentages measured at light load often look high while the actual amps are small.
- Harmonic filters or 3rd-harmonic blocking transformers (zig-zag, delta windings) reduce the neutral current at the source.

## When the neutral overtakes the phase

The neutral current equals the phase current when 3h ÷ √(1 + h²) = 1, which solves to h = 1 ÷ √8 = **35.4%** of the fundamental. Above that the neutral is the most heavily loaded conductor in the cable. In the default case (30%) the neutral carries **86.2 A** against 100 A on each phase, and the total heat in the four-core cable is about 1.25 times that of the three phases alone, which is why the IEC factor of 0.86 raises the design current to **116.3 A**.

## Neutral heating relative to a phase conductor

For a neutral of the same cross-section as the phases, its I²R heating compared with one phase conductor:

| 3rd harmonic (% of fundamental) | I<sub>N</sub> ÷ I | Neutral heat ÷ phase heat |
|---|---|---|
| 10.0% | 0.30 | 0.09 |
| 20.0% | 0.59 | 0.35 |
| 30.0% | 0.86 | 0.74 |
| 35.4% | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 40.0% | 1.11 | 1.24 |
| 50.0% | 1.34 | 1.80 |
| 70.0% | 1.72 | 2.96 |

Heating rises with the square of current, so a neutral carrying 1.34 times phase current runs about 1.8 times as hot in watts.

## Unbalance and harmonics together

Real panels are never perfectly balanced. Suppose the fundamental currents are 100, 90 and 80 A, each with 30% third harmonic. The 50 Hz parts no longer cancel and leave **17.3 A** in the neutral, while the third harmonics add arithmetically to 0.3 × 270 = **81 A**. Because the two are at different frequencies they combine as a root sum of squares: √(17.3² + 81²) = **82.8 A**. The unbalance adds less than you might expect, so the harmonic content dominates the neutral sizing.

## Measuring neutral current

Use a true RMS clamp meter directly on the neutral; an average responding meter can read 30% or more low on distorted current. A power quality analyser logging over a working day shows both the RMS neutral current and the 150 Hz (or 180 Hz) component, and the peak period is the one that matters for cable temperature. A neutral reading higher than any phase is the clearest sign that triplen harmonics are present.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering the harmonic as a share of RMS instead of fundamental.** Analysers normally show % of fundamental; the calculator converts it to the IEC basis for you.
- **Using a half-size neutral on LED or IT circuits.** Reduced neutrals are only acceptable when the third harmonic is low and the load is balanced.
- **Relying on phase overcurrent protection.** Breakers on the phases do not see neutral overload; IEC 60364 may require neutral current detection when it can exceed the phase current.
- **Assuming 5th and 7th harmonics add.** Only triplen orders add in the neutral; the others raise phase RMS but cancel.
- **Measuring at light load.** Percentages look high when the fundamental is small, so size on full load amps.
