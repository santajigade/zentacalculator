## How to use the half-life calculator

Enter the **initial amount**, **half-life** and **time elapsed** to get the amount remaining. Or enter the remaining amount to find the age (time elapsed) or the half-life. Time units can be years, days, hours, minutes or seconds.

## Half-life formula

<p class="formula">N = N₀ × (½)<sup>t / T½</sup></p>

## Worked example — carbon-14

Half-life 5,730 years. After 10,000 years, of 100 units:

N = 100 × 0.5<sup>10,000/5,730</sup> = **29.8** units remaining (29.8%)

## Fraction remaining after each half-life

| Half-lives passed | Fraction remaining |
|---|---|
| 1 | 50% |
| 2 | 25% |
| 3 | 12.5% |
| 4 | 6.25% |
| 5 | 3.12% |
| 7 | 0.781% |
| 10 | 0.0977% |

After about 10 half-lives, less than 0.1% remains.

## Decay constant

The half-life and the decay constant λ are related:

<p class="formula">λ = ln 2 ÷ T<sub>½</sub>, &nbsp; N = N₀ × e<sup>−λt</sup></p>

## Decay constants and lifetimes of common isotopes

From λ = ln 2 ÷ T<sub>½</sub>, the mean lifetime τ = T<sub>½</sub> ÷ ln 2 and the time to fall to 1%, T<sub>½</sub> × log₂ 100:

| Isotope | Half-life | Decay constant λ | Mean lifetime τ | Time to 1% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Technetium-99m | 6.01 h | 0.1153 per h | 8.7 h | 39.9 h |
| Iodine-131 | 8.02 d | 0.0864 per d | 11.6 d | 53.3 d |
| Cobalt-60 | 5.27 yr | 0.1315 per yr | 7.6 yr | 35.0 yr |
| Caesium-137 | 30.1 yr | 0.0230 per yr | 43.4 yr | 200.0 yr |
| Carbon-14 | 5,730 yr | 1.21 × 10<sup>−4</sup> per yr | 8,267 yr | 38,069 yr |

For the default carbon-14 case, λ = 1.21 × 10<sup>−4</sup> per year and τ = 8,267 years.

## Second example: activity of a medical source

A patient receives 3.7 GBq of iodine-131 (half-life 8.02 days). After 30 days the activity is 3.7 × 0.5<sup>30/8.02</sup> = **0.277 GBq**, about 7.5% of the original. Activity in becquerels falls by the same fraction as the number of atoms, because activity = λN, so the calculator works with either.

## Finding the half-life from two measurements

If a count rate falls from 100 to 80 in 3 days, the half-life is T<sub>½</sub> = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N₀ ÷ N) = 3 × 0.6931 ÷ ln 1.25 = **9.32 days**. Enter 100, 80 and 3 days and solve for the half-life to get the same figure.

## How half-lives are measured

Short half-lives are measured by counting decays with a Geiger tube or scintillation detector at regular intervals, subtracting the background count, and fitting a straight line to ln(count rate) against time; the slope is −λ. Very long half-lives such as uranium-238 cannot be watched directly, so they come from the specific activity: count the decays per second from a weighed sample and use λ = A ÷ N. Radiocarbon laboratories now count carbon-14 atoms directly by accelerator mass spectrometry, which needs only milligrams of carbon.

## Radiocarbon dating

To find age from the fraction remaining:

<p class="formula">t = T<sub>½</sub> × log₂(N₀ ÷ N)</p>

A sample with 25% of its original carbon-14 is 2 half-lives old: about **11,460 years**. Carbon dating works up to roughly 50,000 years.

## Half-life in medicine

Drug half-lives determine dosing intervals. A medicine with a 6-hour half-life falls to about 6% of its peak after 24 hours (4 half-lives). It takes about 4–5 half-lives to reach a steady state with regular doses.

## Other uses

- **First-order chemical reactions** have a constant half-life, independent of concentration.
- **Biological half-life**: how long the body takes to eliminate half of a substance.
- **Radioactive waste**: long half-lives mean safe storage for very long periods.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing time units.** The half-life and elapsed time must be in the same unit; each field here has its own unit selector. Entering the elapsed time as 10,000 days while the half-life stays at 5,730 years shows 99.7 units remaining instead of 29.8.
- **Subtracting linearly.** Two half-lives do not remove everything; they leave 25%. Decay is exponential, so some always remains.
- **Reporting the decayed amount as the remainder.** After 10,000 years 29.8 units remain and 70.2 have decayed; check which one a question asks for.
- **Using the wrong half-life convention for radiocarbon.** Conventional radiocarbon ages use the Libby value of 5,568 years, then a calibration curve; 40% remaining gives 7,360 years with 5,568 and 7,575 years with 5,730.
- **Applying one half-life to every drug situation.** Elimination half-life describes the falling phase; during absorption or infusion, or when the liver is saturated, levels do not follow a single exponential.
