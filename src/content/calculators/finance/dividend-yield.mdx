## How to use the dividend yield calculator

Enter the **annual dividend per share** and the **share price** to get the yield. Add the **amount invested** to see your yearly dividend income. You can also find the price at which a stock would reach a target yield.

## Dividend yield formulas

<p class="formula">Yield = Annual dividend ÷ Price<br/>Income = Amount invested × Yield</p>

## Worked example

A share pays 0.60 per quarter (2.40 a year) and trades at 60:

- Yield = 2.40 ÷ 60 = **4%**
- 10,000 invested earns about **400 a year** in dividends

## Yield on cost

If you bought at 40 and the dividend is now 2.40, your **yield on cost** is 6% — even though new buyers at 60 get 4%. Enter your purchase price to see it.

## Typical yields by sector

| Sector | Usual yield range |
|---|---|
| Technology, growth stocks | 0–1.5% |
| Consumer goods | 1–3% |
| Banks and financials | 2–5% |
| Utilities, energy, REITs | 3–7% |

Ranges change with markets. A yield far above its sector often means the share price has fallen because the market expects a dividend cut.

## Payout ratio

The payout ratio shows how much of the profit is paid out:

<p class="formula">Payout ratio = Dividend per share ÷ Earnings per share</p>

A ratio of 30–60% is usually sustainable. Above 80–100% the company is paying more than it earns, which cannot last.

## Dividend growth

A lower yield that grows every year can beat a high flat yield over time. A 2% yield growing 10% a year pays more than a flat 4% yield after about 8 years, and the share price usually rises with the dividend.

## Tax on dividends

Dividends are usually taxable as income in the year you receive them. Rates and allowances differ by country, so check local rules before comparing yields with tax-free options.

## Things to check

- **Payout ratio** — the share of profits paid as dividends. Very high ratios may not be sustainable.
- **Dividend history** — consistent growth is a good sign.
- **Taxes** — dividends are often taxed, and foreign shares may have withholding tax.
