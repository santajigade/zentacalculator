## How to use the water hardness calculator

Enter the **calcium** and **magnesium** concentrations in mg/L from your water test. The calculator returns total hardness as mg/L CaCO₃ (the same as ppm in dilute water) with a soft-to-very-hard rating, plus German degrees, French degrees, grains per gallon and mmol/L. If you only have a total hardness result, clear Ca and Mg and enter it directly to convert between units.

## Water hardness formula

<p class="formula">Hardness (mg/L CaCO₃) = 2.497 × Ca + 4.118 × Mg</p>

<p class="formula">°dH = ppm ÷ 17.848 &nbsp;·&nbsp; °fH = ppm ÷ 10 &nbsp;·&nbsp; gpg = ppm ÷ 17.118 &nbsp;·&nbsp; mmol/L = ppm ÷ 100.09</p>

## Example

A well water with 60 mg/L calcium and 15 mg/L magnesium:

- From calcium: 2.497 × 60 = 149.8 mg/L
- From magnesium: 4.118 × 15 = 61.8 mg/L
- Total hardness = **211.6 mg/L as CaCO₃** — very hard
- = 11.86 °dH = 21.16 °fH = 12.36 gpg

## Notes

- Lab titrations with EDTA report total hardness directly as mg/L CaCO₃.
- To convert between mg/L and other concentration units, use the [ppm calculator](/chemistry/ppm/).

## Measuring hardness by EDTA titration

Most labs measure total hardness by titrating the sample with EDTA at pH 10 using Eriochrome Black T, which turns from wine red to blue when all the Ca and Mg have been complexed. One mole of EDTA binds one mole of Ca or Mg, so:

<p class="formula">Hardness (mg/L CaCO₃) = V<sub>EDTA</sub> (mL) × M<sub>EDTA</sub> × 100,087 ÷ V<sub>sample</sub> (mL)</p>

For the default water (211.6 mg/L) and a 50 mL sample titrated with 0.01 M EDTA, the endpoint should come at about **10571.18 mL**. Doing the titration again at pH 12 to 13 with murexide or calcon indicator gives calcium alone; magnesium is the difference.

## Carbonate and non-carbonate hardness

The alkalinity result on the same report splits hardness into two parts. With an alkalinity of 150 mg/L as CaCO₃ and total hardness of 211.6 mg/L:

- Carbonate (temporary) hardness = the smaller of the two = **150.0 mg/L**
- Non-carbonate (permanent) hardness = 211.6 − 150.0 = **61.6 mg/L**

The carbonate part forms scale in kettles, heaters and cooling towers. Lime softening removes it, but the non-carbonate part needs soda ash, ion exchange or membranes.

## Sizing a water softener

A softener is rated by how many grains of hardness it removes between regenerations. At 211.6 mg/L the water carries 12.36 grains per US gallon. A household using 600 L a day treats 159 gallons, which is **1,959 grains per day**. A 32,000 grain softener would therefore regenerate about every **16.3 days**. Iron in the water adds to the load, so add about 4 gpg of extra hardness for each mg/L of iron.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding Ca and Mg directly.** 60 mg/L Ca plus 15 mg/L Mg is not 75 mg/L hardness. Each must be converted to CaCO₃ with its own factor first.
- **Mixing up Ca as the ion and Ca as CaCO₃.** Some reports give "calcium hardness as CaCO₃"; multiplying that by 2.497 again counts it twice.
- **Reading ppm on a TDS meter as hardness.** TDS includes sodium, chloride and sulfate, so it is always higher than hardness and cannot replace a titration.
- **Confusing UK and US grains.** A Clark degree (UK) is 14.25 mg/L CaCO₃, while a US grain per gallon is 17.12 mg/L.
- **Ignoring magnesium.** Gram for gram, magnesium adds 65% more hardness than calcium, so a calcium-only test underestimates scaling.
