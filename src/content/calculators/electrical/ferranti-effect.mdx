## How to use the Ferranti effect calculator

Enter the **sending-end voltage**, the **line length**, the line's **inductance and capacitance per km** and the **frequency**. The calculator returns the line's electrical length, the open-circuit receiving-end voltage and the percentage rise. Typical overhead-line values are about 1.0 mH/km and 11–12 nF/km; cables have much higher capacitance.

## Ferranti effect formula

<p class="formula">β = 2πf √(LC) &nbsp;·&nbsp; V<sub>r</sub> = V<sub>s</sub> ÷ cos(βl)</p>

## Worked example

A 400 kV, 300 km, 50 Hz overhead line with L = 1.0 mH/km and C = 11.5 nF/km, open at the far end:

- β = 2π × 50 × √(1.0×10⁻⁶ × 11.5×10⁻¹²) = 1.0654e-03 rad/km
- βl = 0.3196 rad (18.31°)
- V<sub>r</sub> = 400 ÷ cos(0.3196) = **421.3 kV** — a **5.33%** rise

## Notes

- The lossless model slightly overstates the rise; line resistance and any load reduce it.
- Underground cables show the effect over much shorter distances because their capacitance is 20–50 times higher.

## Charging power and shunt reactors

The default line rises by **5.33%** to **421.3 kV** when open at the far end. The reactive power it generates is roughly Q<sub>c</sub> = V² × ω × C × l = (400 kV)² × 314.2 × 11.5 nF/km × 300 km ≈ **173 Mvar**. Utilities usually compensate 60 to 80% of this with shunt reactors at one or both ends, for example two 61 Mvar reactors, which pulls the open end voltage back close to the sending end value.

The same constants give a surge impedance of √(L ÷ C) = **295 Ω** and a surge impedance loading of V² ÷ Z<sub>c</sub> = **543 MW**. Below SIL the line generates more reactive power than it absorbs and the voltage profile rises along the line; above SIL it falls. The Ferranti effect is the extreme case of zero load.

## Why the rise grows so fast with length

The small angle form V<sub>r</sub> ÷ V<sub>s</sub> ≈ 1 + (βl)² ÷ 2 gives 5.11% for the default line, close to the exact 5.33%. The rise goes with the square of length and of frequency, so doubling the line from 300 to 600 km roughly quadruples it, and the same line on a 60 Hz system rises by **7.83%**. The wave travels at 1 ÷ √(LC) ≈ 294,884 km/s, a wavelength of 5,898 km at 50 Hz; a line a quarter wavelength long (1,474 km) would in theory give an infinite open end voltage, which is why very long AC lines are split into compensated sections or replaced by HVDC.

The voltage rise on a cable stays modest, but the charging current is the real limit: a long cable can use up most of its own thermal rating carrying reactive current, which is why long AC cable routes have reactors at intermediate points.

## How it shows up in operation

Operators see the effect when a line is energised from one end: the voltage transformer at the open end reads above the sending end bus. Switching studies check this before the event, adding the rise caused by the source impedance, because charging current flowing through a weak source raises the sending end voltage too. The usual practice is to energise long lines from the stronger end and close the far end breaker within seconds.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering capacitance in µF/km as nF/km.** Overhead lines are about 0.011 µF/km, which is 11 nF/km; a factor of 1,000 error gives a nonsense electrical length.
- **Using cable constants for an overhead line or the reverse.** Cable capacitance is 20 to 50 times higher and inductance about a third.
- **Treating the rise as linear in length.** It grows roughly with the square of length, so halving a section cuts the rise by about four.
- **Ignoring the source.** The calculator assumes a stiff sending end; a weak source adds its own rise on top.
- **Forgetting that load removes the effect.** At loads near SIL the receiving end voltage is close to the sending end, so the open line case is the one to design for.
