## How to use the food cost & menu price calculator

Enter the **total ingredient cost**, how many **servings** it makes and your **target food cost %**. The cost per serving, menu price and gross profit are calculated.

## Worked example with the default values

A tray of lasagne costs 36.00 in ingredients and is cut into 12 portions. The target food cost is 30%:

1. Cost per serving = 36.00 ÷ 12 = **3.00**
2. Menu price = 3.00 ÷ 0.30 = **10.00**
3. Gross profit = 10.00 - 3.00 = **7.00** per plate

Dividing by a 30% food cost is the same as multiplying the plate cost by 3.33. Expressed as a markup on cost, that is 233%.

## Beyond food cost

Food cost does not include:

- **Labour** — often 25–35% of sales.
- **Rent, utilities, packaging and marketing**.
- **Waste and spoilage**, which raise the real food cost.

A common rule is that food plus labour (prime cost) should stay under about 60–65% of sales.

## Plate costing tips

- Cost **every** ingredient, including oil, garnish and sauces.
- Use the **yield** after trimming and cooking, not the raw weight.
- Update costs when supplier prices change.
- Round menu prices to psychologically comfortable figures.

## Checking an existing price

The formula also works backwards. If the local market will only pay 9.00 for the dish, the food cost becomes 3.00 ÷ 9.00 = 33.3%. Decide whether that is acceptable, cut the plate cost, or change the portion.

## Practical notes

- **Allow for waste.** If one portion out of 12 is lost to trimming, tasting or a dropped plate, the real cost per serving is 36.00 ÷ 11 = 3.27, and the 30% price rises to 10.91.
- **Use edible portion cost.** Price meat, fish and vegetables at their yield after trimming and cooking, not their bought weight.
- **Recost regularly.** Re-enter the recipe cost whenever a main ingredient changes price; a 10% rise in a key item can push a dish several points over target.
- **Price sides and sauces too.** A dish that includes bread, dips or a side salad must carry their cost as well.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing food cost with markup.** Adding 30% to the plate cost gives 3.90, not 10.00; a 30% food cost means dividing by 0.30.
- **Using the recipe cost as the serving cost.** Always divide by the number of portions first.
- **Including tax in the menu price.** Compare food cost with the price before sales tax or VAT.
- **Forgetting labour and overheads.** A healthy food cost does not guarantee profit if prep is slow or rent is high.
- **Setting one target for every item.** Drinks and desserts usually run lower food costs than steaks, so judge the menu as a whole.
