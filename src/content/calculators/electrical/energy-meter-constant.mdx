## How to use the energy meter constant calculator

Enter the **meter constant** from the nameplate, the number of **pulses** (or disc revolutions) you counted and the **time** you counted for. The calculator returns the load power the meter is registering and the energy recorded. Add a **reference load** from a calibrated instrument to see the meter's percentage error and the expected time between pulses.

## Meter constant formulas

<p class="formula">P (kW) = 3600 × n ÷ (C × t) &nbsp;·&nbsp; E (kWh) = n ÷ C</p>

<p class="formula">Error = (P<sub>meter</sub> − P<sub>actual</sub>) ÷ P<sub>actual</sub> × 100%</p>

## Example

A 3,200 imp/kWh meter flashes 32 times in 30 s while a reference wattmeter reads 1.19 kW:

- P = 3600 × 32 ÷ (3200 × 30) = **1.20 kW**
- Energy recorded = 32 ÷ 3200 kWh = **10 Wh**
- Error = (1.20 − 1.19) ÷ 1.19 = **+0.84%** (the meter reads high)
- At 1.19 kW the LED should flash every **0.945 s**

## Testing tips

- Count for at least 60 s or 20 pulses to keep timing error below about 1%.
- Use a purely resistive, steady load; motor or switching loads fluctuate.
- For three-phase meters, apply a balanced load or test each element separately.
- To turn monthly kWh into load factor and demand figures, use the [load factor calculator](/electrical/load-factor/).

## How long to count

A stopwatch count is only as good as its resolution. Starting the watch on a flash and stopping on a flash removes the half pulse at each end, but a miscount of one pulse in the default 32 still shifts the result by 3.1%, much more than the 0.84% error being measured. At 1.19 kW on a 3,200 imp/kWh meter, each pulse takes 0.945 s:

| Pulses counted | Time needed | Effect of a one pulse miscount |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 4.7 s | ±20.0% |
| 10 | 9.5 s | ±10.0% |
| 20 | 18.9 s | ±5.0% |
| 32 | 30.3 s | ±3.1% |
| 50 | 47.3 s | ±2.0% |
| 100 | 94.5 s | ±1.0% |

To separate a class 1 meter from a faulty one, count at least 100 pulses, or use a photo head and a meter test set that times the pulses electronically.

## Disc meters and CT operated meters

On an electromechanical meter, count the black mark on the disc instead of LED flashes. A 600 rev/kWh meter turning 10 times in 60 s is carrying 10 × 3,600 ÷ (600 × 60) = **1.00 kW**.

For a meter fed from a 200/5 A CT and an 11,000/110 V PT, the pulses measure secondary power. Multiply by 40 × 100 = **4,000**, so a secondary reading of 1.20 kW is 4.8 MW on the primary side.

## What the default error means on a bill

The default test shows the meter reading 0.84% high. On a household using 300 kWh a month, that is about 2.5 kWh of extra recorded energy each month. It is still inside the ±1% limit of a class 1 meter, so it would pass an accuracy test, although the single 30 s count is too short to prove it.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting the first flash.** Start timing on a flash and call it zero; counting it as one adds a pulse and inflates the power.
- **Using the kvarh LED.** Many static meters have separate kWh and kvarh pulse outputs. Check the label next to the LED you are watching.
- **Testing on a varying load.** Fridges, inverters and motors change power during the count; use a resistive heater or kettle.
- **Forgetting other loads.** Anything else on the circuit adds pulses. Switch off the rest of the installation, or measure the reference at the meter itself.
- **Leaving out the CT and PT ratios.** On HT and large LT services the raw pulse result is secondary power only.
