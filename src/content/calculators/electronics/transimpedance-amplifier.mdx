## How to use the transimpedance amplifier calculator

Enter the **photodiode current**, the **feedback resistor**, the **total input capacitance** (photodiode junction capacitance at its bias plus the op-amp's common-mode and differential input capacitance) and the op-amp's **gain-bandwidth product**. The calculator returns the output voltage, the achievable bandwidth and two values of feedback capacitor: the larger gives a flat Butterworth response, the smaller is the minimum for a 45° phase margin (faster, with some peaking). To find Rf for a target output, press × on Rf.

## Transimpedance amplifier formulas

<p class="formula">V<sub>out</sub> = I<sub>p</sub> × R<sub>f</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; f<sub>−3dB</sub> ≈ √(GBW ÷ (2π R<sub>f</sub> C<sub>in</sub>))</p>

<p class="formula">C<sub>f</sub> (Butterworth) ≈ √(C<sub>in</sub> ÷ (π R<sub>f</sub> GBW)) &nbsp;·&nbsp; C<sub>f</sub> (45°) ≈ √(C<sub>in</sub> ÷ (2π R<sub>f</sub> GBW))</p>

These approximations hold when C<sub>f</sub> is much smaller than C<sub>in</sub>, which is the usual case.

## Worked example

A photodiode with 20 pF total capacitance producing 10 µA, R<sub>f</sub> = 100 kΩ and a 10 MHz op-amp:

- V<sub>out</sub> = 10 µA × 100 kΩ = **1 V**
- Bandwidth = √(10 MHz ÷ (2π × 100 kΩ × 20 pF)) = **892 kHz**
- Feedback capacitor: **2.52 pF** for a flat response (minimum **1.78 pF** for 45° phase margin) — use the nearest standard value, e.g. 2.7 pF

## Practical notes

- Values of C<sub>f</sub> below about 0.5 pF are swamped by the resistor's own parasitic capacitance (≈ 0.1–0.2 pF for an 0805 part); use a T-network or a lower R<sub>f</sub>.
- Choose an op-amp with **FET or CMOS inputs** — bias current flows through R<sub>f</sub> and appears directly as an output offset.
- Photovoltaic mode (zero bias) gives the lowest dark current and noise; photoconductive mode (reverse bias) gives lower capacitance and higher speed.
- Thermal noise of R<sub>f</sub> is √(4kT/R<sub>f</sub>) in current terms, so a larger R<sub>f</sub> improves signal-to-noise as well as gain.

## Noise and signal to noise ratio with the default values

The worked example above stops at bandwidth, but a TIA is usually chosen for its noise. Take the same 10 µA, 100 kΩ, 20 pF and 10 MHz design at 300 K:

1. Thermal noise current of R<sub>f</sub> = √(4kT ÷ R<sub>f</sub>) = **0.407 pA/√Hz**, which appears at the output as 40.7 nV/√Hz.
2. Shot noise of the 10 µA photocurrent = √(2qI) = **1.79 pA/√Hz**, about 4.4 times larger.
3. A second-order Butterworth response has a noise bandwidth of about 1.11 × f<sub>−3dB</sub> = 990 kHz, so √ENBW = 995.
4. Integrated: R<sub>f</sub> noise 405 pA rms (40.5 µV rms at the output), shot noise 1.78 nA rms, total 1.83 nA rms.
5. SNR = 20 log₁₀(10 µA ÷ 1.83 nA) = **74.8 dB**. Without shot noise it would be 87.9 dB.

At this signal level the light itself sets the noise floor, so a bigger R<sub>f</sub> buys little. At nanoamp currents the balance reverses and R<sub>f</sub> noise dominates, which is why weak-signal receivers use 1 to 10 MΩ.

The op-amp's own voltage noise matters too. Above the feedback zero at 1 ÷ (2π R<sub>f</sub> C<sub>f</sub>) = 631 kHz the noise gain rises to 1 + C<sub>in</sub> ÷ C<sub>f</sub> = **8.93**, so an op-amp with 10 nV/√Hz contributes about 89 nV/√Hz there, more than R<sub>f</sub> does.

With the default 100 kΩ the output reaches 4.5 V at 45 µA, leaving 4.5 times headroom over the 10 µA signal.

## Testing a TIA

Drive a fast LED or laser diode with a square wave and watch the output step. A Butterworth design rises in roughly 0.35 ÷ f<sub>−3dB</sub> = 392 ns with about 4% overshoot; ringing means C<sub>f</sub> is too small or C<sub>in</sub> is larger than you assumed. Cover the photodiode to read the dark offset: a FET-input op-amp with 1 pA bias adds only 0.1 µV, while 100 nA of bipolar bias current would add 10 mV.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the photodiode's zero-bias capacitance in a biased circuit.** Reverse bias can cut junction capacitance by half or more, and the op-amp's input capacitance must be added.
- **Calling f<sub>−3dB</sub> the noise bandwidth.** The noise integrates over about 1.11 times the −3 dB frequency for a Butterworth response, more with peaking.
- **Fitting a Cf below 0.5 pF.** Board and resistor parasitics already provide about that much, so the real response is slower than calculated.
- **Ignoring shot noise at high light levels.** Above a few µA the photocurrent's own shot noise usually exceeds R<sub>f</sub> noise.
- **Picking Rf without checking the rails.** A 10 MΩ resistor saturates a 5 V output at only 0.45 µA.
