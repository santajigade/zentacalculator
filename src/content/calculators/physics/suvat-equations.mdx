## How to use the SUVAT equations calculator

Enter **any three** of u, v, a, t and s. The other two are solved automatically — press × on a field to choose which values are calculated.

## The equations

<p class="formula">v = u + at<br/>s = ½(u + v)t<br/>s = ut + ½at²<br/>v² = u² + 2as<br/>s = vt − ½at²</p>

## Worked example

u = 5 m/s, a = 2 m/s², t = 10 s:

- v = 5 + 2 × 10 = **25 m/s**
- s = ½ × (5 + 25) × 10 = **150 m** (the same as 5 × 10 + ½ × 2 × 10²)

Two quick checks confirm the pair: v² = u² + 2as gives 25 + 2 × 2 × 150 = 625 = 25², and s = vt − ½at² gives 250 − 100 = 150 m. The average velocity is 15 m/s, midway between u and v. In the tenth second alone the object covers s(10) − s(9) = 150 − 126 = **24 m**, which also follows from u + a(n − ½) = 5 + 2 × 9.5.

### Solving for time from displacement

With the same u = 5 m/s and a = 2 m/s², when has the object gone 100 m? Rearranging s = ut + ½at² gives t² + 5t − 100 = 0, and the positive root is t = (−5 + √425) ÷ 2 = **7.808 s**, at which point v = 20.62 m/s.

### Motion that reverses direction

A ball thrown up at 15 m/s from the top of a 20 m cliff lands on the beach below when s = −20 m, with up positive and a = −9.80665 m/s². The quadratic −4.903t² + 15t + 20 = 0 has roots -1.004 s and **4.063 s**. The negative root is the time before the throw at which a ball on the same path would have passed the beach level, so it is discarded. The ball lands at -24.84 m/s, the minus sign showing it is moving downwards.

## Braking distance example

A car at 20 m/s (72 km/h) brakes at −8 m/s². Set u = 20, v = 0, a = −8: it stops in **2.5 s** over **25 m**. Doubling the speed to 40 m/s quadruples the distance to 100 m.

## Which equation to use

Each SUVAT equation leaves out one of the five quantities:

| Equation | Missing |
|---|---|
| v = u + at | s |
| s = ½(u + v)t | a |
| s = ut + ½at² | v |
| s = vt − ½at² | u |
| v² = u² + 2as | t |

Pick the one that omits the quantity you neither know nor need. The calculator handles this automatically.

## Vertical motion

For objects thrown upward, use a = −9.81 m/s² (taking up as positive). A ball thrown up at 15 m/s reaches a maximum height of v² ÷ 2g = **11.5 m** after 1.53 s.

## Limits

SUVAT applies only when acceleration is **constant**. For changing acceleration, such as a car with varying throttle or an object with significant air resistance, use calculus or a numerical method.

## Sign convention

Pick one direction as positive. Velocities and accelerations in the opposite direction are negative — for example, a = −9.81 m/s² for an object thrown upwards.

## Using SUVAT with real measurements

Car magazines quote 0 to 100 km/h times. A car that takes 8 s has an average acceleration of 27.78 ÷ 8 = 3.47 m/s² and covers ½ × 27.78 × 8 = 111 m during the run. Real acceleration falls as the car changes gear and gains speed, so this is the constant acceleration that would give the same time. In school labs, two light gates measure u and v and a timer gives t, so a = (v − u) ÷ t can be checked against a = (v² − u²) ÷ 2s from the gate spacing.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up displacement and distance.** With u = 10 m/s, a = −2 m/s² and t = 8 s, s = 16 m, but the object went 25 m forward before turning back at t = 5 s, so the distance travelled is 34 m.
- **Using SUVAT across a change in acceleration.** Split the motion into phases, for example thinking time at constant speed and braking at constant deceleration, and use the final v of one phase as the u of the next.
- **Keeping the wrong root.** A quadratic in t gives two answers; keep the one that is positive and physically follows the start of the motion.
- **Leaving speeds in km/h.** Convert to m/s (divide by 3.6) before using a in m/s².
- **Inconsistent signs.** Once up is positive, a thrown ball has a = −9.80665 m/s² on the way up and on the way down.
