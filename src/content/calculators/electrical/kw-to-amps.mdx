## How to use the kW to amps calculator

1. Enter the **power** in kW (or switch to W or hp).
2. Choose **three-phase**, **single-phase** or **DC**.
3. Enter the **voltage** and **power factor** (use 1 for DC or purely resistive loads).

The calculator gives the current. It works in reverse too — enter amps and press × on Power to find kW.

## KW to amps formulas

| System | Current |
|---|---|
| DC | I = P ÷ V |
| Single-phase AC | I = P ÷ (V × PF) |
| Three-phase AC | I = P ÷ (√3 × V<sub>L</sub> × PF) |

P is in watts. For a motor, use the **electrical input** power (rated output ÷ efficiency).

## Choosing a breaker and cable

The calculated current is the **running** current. When selecting protection and cable:

- Choose a breaker rated above the running current (often the next standard size, such as 16 A, 20 A, 25 A, 32 A).
- Check the cable's current-carrying capacity for its installation method.
- Allow for motor starting current and check the [voltage drop](/electrical/cable-voltage-drop/).
- Follow local wiring regulations.

## Single-phase vs three-phase

For the same power, a three-phase circuit carries much less current per conductor than a single-phase one, which is why large motors and buildings use three-phase supplies. 10 kW is 43.5 A at 230 V single-phase but only 16.4 A per line at 415 V three-phase (PF 0.85).

## Worked example

With the default inputs, a 10 kW three-phase load at 415 V and a power factor of 0.85:

- I = P ÷ (√3 × V × PF) = 10,000 ÷ (1.732 × 415 × 0.85) = **16.37 A** per line

## Converting a motor rating to amps

A motor nameplate kW is the **shaft output**, so the electrical input is higher by the efficiency. For a 22 kW motor at 415 V with η = 92% and PF = 0.86:

- Electrical input = 22 ÷ 0.92 = 23.91 kW
- Current = 23,913 ÷ (1.732 × 415 × 0.86) = **38.7 A**

Entering 22 kW directly would give 35.6 A, about 8% low. Enter the input kW, or divide the result by the efficiency.

## North American and DC supplies

The same 10 kW at PF 0.85 draws **14.2 A** on a 480 V three-phase system and **32.7 A** on 208 V. On a 48 V DC telecom or battery system, a 3 kW load draws 3,000 ÷ 48 = **62.5 A**, which is why DC busbars are so heavy.

## Constant power loads at low voltage

Drives, SMPS and UPS inputs hold their power constant, so current rises when the voltage falls. If the 415 V supply sags to 380 V, the 10 kW load draws 17.9 A instead of 16.4 A, a 9% increase. Size cables and breakers for the lowest expected voltage on these loads.

## Common mistakes

- **Taking the motor output kW as input power.** Divide by efficiency first, as shown above.
- **Choosing DC with a power factor below 1.** DC has no power factor; set PF to 1 or the current comes out too high.
- **Using 230 V with the three-phase option on a 400 V system.** The √3 formula needs the line-to-line voltage; 230 V would overstate the current by about 74%.
- **Sizing the breaker exactly at the calculated current.** The breaker must sit above the running current, and the cable must be rated for the breaker, after derating for grouping and temperature.
- **Assuming the result covers starting.** Direct-on-line motors draw 6 to 7 times this current for a few seconds; check the trip curve and voltage dip.
