## How to use the lumen method calculator

Enter the **target illuminance**, the **room length and width**, the **lumens per fixture** and the **utilisation and maintenance factors**. The calculator returns the exact number of fixtures, the number rounded up and the illuminance you will actually get.

## Lumen method formula

<p class="formula">N = E × A ÷ (Φ × UF × MF) &nbsp;·&nbsp; E<sub>achieved</sub> = N × Φ × UF × MF ÷ A</p>

## Worked example

An office 10 m × 8 m (80 m²) needs 500 lux, using 4,000 lm LED panels with UF = 0.6 and MF = 0.8:

- N = 500 × 80 ÷ (4,000 × 0.6 × 0.8) = 40,000 ÷ 1,920 = **20.83** → **21 fixtures**
- Achieved = 21 × 1,920 ÷ 80 = **504 lux**

## Notes

- In practice round to a number that fits a neat grid (for example 21 → 4 × 5 = 20 or 4 × 6 = 24) and recheck the lux.
- The lumen method gives the average illuminance; use lighting software to check uniformity and glare.

## From the result to a real grid

The default room needs **20.83** fixtures, so the calculator rounds up to **21** and reports **504 lux**. Twenty one panels rarely fit a ceiling grid, so check the neighbouring layouts with the same formula:

- 4 × 5 = 20 fixtures: 20 × 4,000 × 0.6 × 0.8 ÷ 80 = **480 lux**, just under target
- 4 × 6 = 24 fixtures: **576 lux**, comfortably above target and easy to dim back

With 6 rows along the 10 m length and 4 across the 8 m width, the spacing is 1.67 m by 2.00 m. For panels mounted on a 2.8 m ceiling above a 0.8 m desk height, the mounting height over the work plane is 2.0 m, giving a spacing to height ratio of 1.00, inside the 1.0 to 1.5 that most panel datasheets allow. Keep the outer row about half a spacing from the walls.

## Where the utilisation factor comes from

Manufacturers publish UF tables against the room index K = L × W ÷ [H<sub>m</sub> × (L + W)] and the ceiling, wall and floor reflectances. For this room K = 80 ÷ (2.0 × 18) = **2.22**. A large, low room (K above 2) wastes little light on the walls and typically reaches UF 0.6 to 0.7 with light finishes; a narrow corridor or a tall plant room (K below 1) may fall to 0.35 to 0.45, which can almost double the fixture count for the same lux.

## Checking the installation with a lux meter

Measure at the work plane height on a regular grid of points, with daylight excluded (at night or with blinds closed) and the LEDs run for at least 20 minutes so their output is stable. The average of the grid is the maintained illuminance once the room is old and dirty only if you divide by the maintenance factor: a new installation should read about E ÷ MF, here around 625 lux, so a reading of 500 lux on day one means the room will fall short later. Uniformity is the minimum reading divided by the average; EN 12464-1 asks for at least 0.6 on office task areas.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering lamp lumens for a fitting with a diffuser.** Use the luminaire output from the datasheet, which already includes optic losses, or the count comes out too low.
- **Using the floor to ceiling height for the room index.** The relevant height is from the fitting to the work plane, not to the floor.
- **Taking MF as 1 for LED.** Even long life LEDs lose 10 to 20% of their output by L80 or L90, and dust on the diffuser adds to it.
- **Rounding 20.8 down to 20 to save a fitting.** The average then falls below the target before any ageing.
- **Applying an office UF to a dark warehouse.** Low reflectance walls and racking absorb far more light, so take the UF from the table at the real reflectances.
