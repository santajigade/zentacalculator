## How to use the electric field calculator

Enter the **source charge** in microcoulombs (negative for a negative charge), the **distance** from it, and optionally a **test charge**. The calculator returns the electric field strength, the electric potential at that point (taking zero at infinity) and the force on the test charge. Clear the distance and enter a field value to find how far away a given field strength occurs.

## Electric field formula

<p class="formula">E = k q ÷ r² &nbsp;·&nbsp; V = k q ÷ r &nbsp;·&nbsp; F = E q<sub>0</sub></p>

k = 1 ÷ (4πε<sub>0</sub>) = 8.9875517923 × 10⁹ N·m²/C².

## Example

A 5 µC charge, measured 0.5 m away:

- E = 8.988 × 10⁹ × 5 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.5² = **179,751 V/m**
- V = 8.988 × 10⁹ × 5 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.5 = **89,876 V**
- Force on a 1 µC test charge = 179,751 × 10⁻⁶ = **0.1798 N**

## Notes

- Outside a uniformly charged sphere the field is the same as for a point charge at its centre.
- Between two parallel plates the field is uniform, E = V ÷ d — see the [parallel plate capacitor calculator](/physics/parallel-plate-capacitor/).
- The force between two charges follows directly from [Coulomb's law](/physics/coulombs-law/).

## Work done on the test charge

The potential tells you the energy per coulomb. Bringing the default 1 µC test charge in from far away to 0.5 m from the 5 µC charge takes W = q<sub>0</sub> V = 1 × 10⁻⁶ × 89,876 = **0.0899 J**. Both charges are positive, so this work is stored as potential energy and released if the test charge is let go. With a negative test charge the sign flips and the charges attract.

Doubling the distance to 1 m cuts the field to a quarter, 44,938 V/m, but the potential only to half, 44,938 V.

## How large can the source be?

The field at the surface of a charged sphere of radius a is kq ÷ a². Air breaks down near 3 × 10⁶ V/m, so a sphere holding 5 µC must have a radius of at least √(kq ÷ E) = √(44,938 ÷ 3 × 10⁶) = **12.2 cm**. A smaller ball would leak charge by corona discharge. This is why Van de Graaff generators use large, smooth domes.

## Typical field strengths

| Situation | Field strength |
|---|---|
| Fair-weather atmosphere near the ground | about 100 V/m |
| ICNIRP public limit, 50 Hz | 5,000 V/m |
| Default 5 µC charge at 0.5 m | 179,751 V/m |
| Breakdown of dry air | about 3 × 10⁶ V/m |
| Across a cell membrane (70 mV over 7 nm) | 1 × 10⁷ V/m |
| At the Bohr radius of hydrogen | 5.14 × 10¹¹ V/m |

In practice, static and low-frequency fields are measured with a field mill, which uses a rotating shutter to chop the field over a sensing plate, or with a small probe meter around power lines and substations. Near real conductors the field is set by their shape, so the point-charge formula is a far-field estimate.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the micro prefix.** 5 µC is 5 × 10⁻⁶ C; leaving it as 5 C gives a field a million times too large.
- **Entering distance in centimetres.** r must be in metres, and because it is squared, a cm error is a factor of 10,000.
- **Adding fields from several charges as plain numbers.** Fields are vectors; add their x and y components. Only potentials add as scalars.
- **Dropping the sign of the potential.** A negative charge gives a negative potential, which matters when computing work.
- **Using the formula inside a conductor.** The static field inside a conductor is zero; the 1/r² law applies outside the charge.
