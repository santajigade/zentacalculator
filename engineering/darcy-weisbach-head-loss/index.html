    Friction between a moving fluid and the pipe wall causes a pressure drop that pumps must overcome. This pipe pressure drop calculator uses the Darcy Weisbach equation with your friction factor, length, bore, velocity and density, as explained in how to calculate pressure drop in pipes.

    Piping and pump engineers use it to size lines and pumps, and the equation builds on Bernoulli's equation.

    How to use the pipe pressure drop calculator

    Enter the friction factor, pipe length, inner diameter, velocity and density. You get the friction head loss and pressure drop. Get the velocity from flow using the pipe flow velocity calculator.

    Pipe pressure drop formula

    hf = f × (L ÷ D) × v² ÷ 2g ΔP = ρ g hf

    Worked example

    100 m of 100 mm pipe, water at 1.77 m/s, f = 0.02:

    • hf = 0.02 × (100 ÷ 0.1) × 1.77² ÷ (2 × 9.81) = 3.19 m
    • ΔP = 998 × 9.81 × 3.19 = 31,270 Pa = 0.313 bar

    Velocity matters most

    Pressure drop rises with the square of velocity. Doubling the flow in the same pipe gives four times the loss — the chart shows the curve. Going up one pipe size usually cuts losses dramatically.

    Finding the friction factor

    The Darcy friction factor depends on Reynolds number and pipe roughness:

    • Laminar flow (Re < 2,300): f = 64 ÷ Re.
    • Turbulent flow: use the Colebrook equation or the explicit Swamee–Jain approximation:

    f = 0.25 ÷ [log₁₀(ε ÷ 3.7D + 5.74 ÷ Re0.9)]²

    Find Re with the Reynolds number calculator.

    Pipe roughness values

    Material Absolute roughness ε (mm)
    Drawn copper, PVC, PE 0.0015
    Stainless steel 0.015
    Commercial steel (new) 0.045
    Galvanised steel 0.15
    Cast iron 0.26
    Concrete 0.3–3.0

    Old pipes become rougher with scale and corrosion, which raises friction losses over time.

    Minor losses in fittings

    Valves, bends and tees add losses: h = K × v² ÷ 2g.

    Fitting Typical K
    90° long-radius elbow 0.3–0.5
    90° standard elbow 0.75–0.9
    Tee, flow through branch 1.0–1.8
    Gate valve, fully open 0.15–0.2
    Globe valve, fully open 6–10
    Pipe entrance (sharp) 0.5
    Pipe exit 1.0

    Add the K values for all fittings and include them with the straight-pipe loss to get total head loss for pump sizing.

    Typical friction factors (turbulent water)

    Pipe Typical f
    Smooth plastic / copper 0.012 – 0.018
    New commercial steel 0.015 – 0.022
    Old or corroded steel 0.025 – 0.04

    For laminar flow, f = 64 ÷ Re — check the Reynolds number first.

    Friction factor for the default case

    The default f = 0.02 is a round figure. For water at 20 °C in the same 100 mm new steel pipe at 1.77 m/s, Re = 998 × 1.77 × 0.1 ÷ 0.001002 = 176,293. With ε = 0.045 mm the Swamee Jain equation gives f = 0.0189, so the straight pipe loss is 3.02 m instead of the 3.19 m shown with the default (0.313 bar).

    Now add six standard elbows (K = 0.75 each), an open gate valve (0.15) and the pipe exit (1.0). ΣK = 5.65 and the fitting loss is 5.65 × 1.77² ÷ (2 × 9.807) = 0.90 m. The total for pump sizing is about 3.92 m, so the fittings add 30% to the straight run.

    Pressure drop per 100 m of new steel pipe

    Water at 20 °C, ε = 0.045 mm, friction factor from Swamee Jain, result in bar per 100 m:

    Pipe (bore) 1.0 m/s 1.5 m/s 2.0 m/s
    DN25 (26.6 mm) 0.530 1.128 1.941
    DN50 (52.5 mm) 0.224 0.479 0.826
    DN80 (77.9 mm) 0.137 0.294 0.507
    DN100 (102.3 mm) 0.098 0.210 0.362
    DN150 (154.1 mm) 0.059 0.127 0.220

    At the same velocity, small pipes lose much more per metre because L ÷ D is larger and their relative roughness is higher.

    Measuring friction loss on site

    Fit two pressure gauges or a DP transmitter on a straight run with no fittings between them, ideally 50 diameters or more apart. Correct the reading for any height difference between the tappings (9.81 kPa per metre of water), then divide by ρg to get hf. Back-calculating f from a measured drop and a known flow shows how much an old line has scaled up compared with its design.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the Fanning factor. A Fanning value of 0.005 is a Darcy value of 0.02; using it here underestimates the loss four times.
    • Using nominal size for the bore. DN100 schedule 40 has a 102.3 mm bore, and the loss is very sensitive to diameter.
    • Counting elevation as friction. Static head from a height change is separate from hf and must be added on its own.
    • Keeping f fixed when the flow changes. In turbulent flow f falls slightly as Re rises; a 20% increase in flow raises the loss by a factor of 1.41, not exactly 1.44.
    • Designing with new pipe roughness for an old system. Scaled or tuberculated steel can be several times rougher than new pipe.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the Darcy–Weisbach equation?

    It gives the friction loss in a full pipe — head loss = f × (L/D) × v²/2g. It works for any liquid and any flow regime, as long as the right friction factor is used.

    How do I find the friction factor?

    For laminar flow (Re below 2,300), f = 64/Re. For turbulent flow, use the Moody chart or the Colebrook equation with the pipe's roughness. 0.015–0.03 covers most commercial steel pipes in turbulent water flow.

    Does this include losses from valves and fittings?

    No — only straight pipe. Add fittings as an equivalent length of pipe, or use their K factors separately.

    What is the difference between Darcy and Fanning friction factors?

    The Darcy factor is four times the Fanning factor. Make sure your chart or correlation uses the same one as your formula.

    How does diameter affect head loss?

    For the same flow, head loss varies roughly with 1 ÷ D⁵, so a slightly larger pipe greatly reduces losses.

    Can I measure pipe friction loss with two pressure gauges?

    Yes, on a straight run without fittings. Subtract the downstream reading from the upstream one, correct for any height difference between the gauges, and divide by ρg to get the friction head.

    How much does pressure drop rise if flow increases by 20%?

    Roughly with the square of flow, so about 1.44 times. Because the friction factor falls a little as Re rises, the default 100 mm steel case gives 1.41 times.
