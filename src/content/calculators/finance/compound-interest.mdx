## How to use the compound interest calculator

Choose the compounding frequency, then fill in **any three** of principal, rate, time and maturity amount. The fourth — and the interest earned — is calculated. One tool answers four questions:

- *What will my savings grow to?* → enter principal, rate, time.
- *What rate do I need?* → enter principal, target amount, time.
- *How long will it take?* → press × on Time, then enter the target amount.
- *How much should I invest now?* → press × on Principal, then enter the target amount.

The currency symbol follows your region automatically. The maths is the same in any currency.

## Compound interest formula

<p class="formula">A = P × (1 + r/n)<sup>n×t</sup>   Interest = A − P</p>

- **P** — principal (starting amount)
- **r** — annual interest rate as a decimal (7.5% = 0.075)
- **n** — compounding periods per year
- **t** — time in years

## Worked example

10,000 invested at 7.5% compounded quarterly for 5 years:

- r/n = 0.075 ÷ 4 = 0.01875
- n × t = 4 × 5 = 20
- A = 10,000 × 1.01875²⁰ = **14,499.48**
- Interest earned = **4,499.48**

## How compounding frequency changes the result

For 10,000 at 7.5% for 5 years:

| Compounding | Maturity amount |
|---|---|
| Yearly | 14,356.29 |
| Half-yearly | 14,450.44 |
| Quarterly | 14,499.48 |
| Monthly | 14,532.94 |
| Daily | 14,549.35 |

More frequent compounding earns slightly more because interest starts earning interest sooner. The gap is small compared with the effect of rate and time — use the chart to see how time dominates.

## Rule of 72

To estimate how long money takes to double, divide 72 by the interest rate. At 8%, 72 ÷ 8 ≈ 9 years. The calculator gives the exact answer: clear the Time field and set the maturity amount to twice the principal.

## Compound vs simple interest

10,000 at 7.5% a year, compounded yearly versus simple interest:

| Years | Compound | Simple | Extra from compounding |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10,750.00 | 10,750.00 | 0.00 |
| 5 | 14,356.29 | 13,750.00 | 606.29 |
| 10 | 20,610.32 | 17,500.00 | 3,110.32 |
| 20 | 42,478.51 | 25,000.00 | 17,478.51 |
| 30 | 87,549.55 | 32,500.00 | 55,049.55 |

For the first few years the difference is small. Over decades, interest on interest becomes the larger part of the total.

## Continuous compounding

As compounding becomes more frequent, the result approaches a limit called continuous compounding:

<p class="formula">A = P × e<sup>r × t</sup></p>

10,000 at 7.5% for 5 years gives 14,549.91, only slightly more than daily compounding. In practice, monthly or daily compounding is close enough.

## Effective annual rate (EAR)

Banks may quote the same nominal rate with different compounding. Compare them with the effective annual rate:

<p class="formula">EAR = (1 + r ÷ n)<sup>n</sup> − 1</p>

7.5% compounded monthly is an EAR of 7.763%. Quarterly gives 7.714%.

## Things to keep in mind

- Interest is usually taxable, so your after-tax return is lower.
- Inflation reduces real returns; compare your rate with current inflation.
- Stock and fund returns are not fixed, so this formula gives an estimate only.

## Year by year growth of the default deposit

For 10,000 at 7.5% compounded quarterly, each year adds a little more than the last because the interest base keeps rising:

| Year | Opening balance | Interest in the year | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10,000.00 | 771.36 | 10,771.36 |
| 2 | 10,771.36 | 830.86 | 11,602.22 |
| 3 | 11,602.22 | 894.95 | 12,497.16 |
| 4 | 12,497.16 | 963.98 | 13,461.14 |
| 5 | 13,461.14 | 1,038.34 | 14,499.48 |

The fifth year earns noticeably more than the first even though the rate never changes. That widening gap is the whole effect of compounding.

## Real return after tax and inflation

Interest on deposits is normally taxed as it is credited. If 30% tax is paid from each quarterly credit, the effective rate falls to 7.5 × 0.70 = 5.25% and the 5 year balance is **12,979.58** instead of 14,499.48. With 5% inflation, the untaxed 14,499.48 buys only what **11,360.72** buys today. Comparing the post-tax rate with inflation shows whether a deposit is actually growing your purchasing power.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering the rate as a decimal.** Type 7.5 for 7.5%, not 0.075.
- **Mixing up time units.** Time is in years; 18 months is 1.5, not 18.
- **Comparing quoted rates with different compounding.** Convert both to an effective annual rate before choosing between offers.
- **Assuming payouts compound.** A deposit that pays interest out monthly does not compound at all; only reinvested interest grows.
- **Using the rule of 72 at high rates.** It drifts above about 20%; at 7.5% quarterly the exact doubling time is 9.33 years.
