  - q: What is the exponential distribution formula?
    a: The density is f(x) = λe^(−λx) for x ≥ 0, and the cumulative probability is P(X ≤ x) = 1 − e^(−λx). The mean and standard deviation are both 1/λ.
  - q: What does memoryless mean?
    a: The chance of waiting another t units does not depend on how long you have already waited. P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t). It is the only continuous distribution with this property.
  - q: How is it related to the Poisson distribution?
    a: If events occur as a Poisson process with rate λ, the number in a fixed time is Poisson and the time between events is exponential with the same λ.

## How to use the exponential distribution calculator

Enter the **rate λ** — the average number of events per unit of time — and the value **x** in the same time unit. The calculator returns the probability that the waiting time is at most x and more than x, the density at x, and the mean and median. To find the time by which a given share of events has happened, enter P(X ≤ x) and clear x.

## Exponential distribution formulas

<p class="formula">P(X ≤ x) = 1 − e<sup>−λx</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; P(X &gt; x) = e<sup>−λx</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; f(x) = λ e<sup>−λx</sup></p>

<p class="formula">Mean = 1 ÷ λ &nbsp;·&nbsp; Median = ln 2 ÷ λ</p>

## Example

Customers arrive at an average rate of 0.5 per minute. The chance that the next one arrives within 3 minutes:

- P(X ≤ 3) = 1 − e<sup>−0.5 × 3</sup> = **77.69%**
- P(X > 3) = **22.31%**
- Density f(3) = 0.5 × e<sup>−1.5</sup> = **0.11157**
- Mean wait = **2 min**; median = ln 2 ÷ 0.5 = **1.3863 min**

## Notes

- The exponential model fits the flat "useful life" part of the reliability bathtub curve, not early failures or wear-out; use the Weibull distribution for those.
- Radioactive decay follows the same law, with λ the decay constant — see the [radioactive activity calculator](/physics/radioactive-activity/).

## Reading the default result

With λ = 0.5 per minute and x = 3 minutes, the calculator gives P(X ≤ 3) = **77.69%**, P(X > 3) = **22.31%**, f(3) = **0.11157**, a mean of **2 min** and a median of **1.3863 min**. The median is shorter than the mean because a few very long gaps pull the mean up: half of all waits are under 1.39 minutes even though the average is 2.

The density value 0.11157 is not a probability. It is a rate per minute, and multiplying it by a short interval gives an approximate probability, for example about 1.12% for an arrival in the 0.1 minute window just after 3 minutes.

## Percentile waiting times at λ = 0.5

Solving 1 − e<sup>−λx</sup> = p gives x = −ln(1 − p) ÷ λ. This is the time by which a given share of waits have ended:

| Share of waits finished | Time x | In multiples of the mean |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 0.211 min | 0.105 × mean |
| 25% | 0.575 min | 0.288 × mean |
| 50% | 1.386 min | 0.693 × mean |
| 75% | 2.773 min | 1.386 × mean |
| 90% | 4.605 min | 2.303 × mean |
| 95% | 5.991 min | 2.996 × mean |
| 99% | 9.210 min | 4.605 × mean |

Planners use the 90% or 95% row to size staffing or buffer time, because designing to the mean leaves 36.8% of waits longer than planned.

## Using it with real data

- **Interval probabilities.** The chance that the next arrival comes between 1 and 3 minutes is e<sup>−0.5</sup> − e<sup>−1.5</sup> = **38.34%**.
- **Memoryless waits.** If nobody has arrived for 2 minutes, the chance of waiting at least 3 more is still e<sup>−1.5</sup> = 22.31%, the same as from the start.
- **Reliability.** A unit with an MTBF of 50,000 h running for one year (8,760 h) has R = e<sup>−8,760/50,000</sup> = **83.9%** chance of no failure.
- **Estimating λ.** From test data, λ = failures ÷ total operating time. Twelve failures in 240,000 unit hours gives λ = 5 × 10⁻⁵ per hour, an MTBF of 20,000 h.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering the mean instead of the rate.** A mean wait of 2 minutes is λ = 0.5, not λ = 2. Swapping them turns 77.69% into 99.75%.
- **Mixing time units.** λ per hour with x in minutes gives a result 60 times off; convert both to the same unit first.
- **Treating f(x) as P(X = x).** For a continuous variable the chance of any exact value is zero.
- **Applying it to wear-out.** Bearings, belts and batteries fail more often as they age, so a constant λ understates late-life risk.
- **Assuming independence that is not there.** Arrivals that come in bursts, such as customers off the same bus, are not exponential.
