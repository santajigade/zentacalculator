  - q: What is the formula for thermal expansion of a liquid?
    a: ΔV = β × V₀ × ΔT, where β is the volumetric expansion coefficient (per K), V₀ the initial volume and ΔT the temperature rise. The final volume is V₀ (1 + β ΔT).
  - q: How much does petrol expand when it warms up?
    a: About 0.095% per °C. 1,000 L delivered at 15 °C becomes about 1,019 L at 35 °C, which is why fuel is traded at a reference temperature of 15 °C.
  - q: Why does water expand differently?
    a: Water's β changes strongly with temperature — it is zero at 4 °C (maximum density), about 207 × 10⁻⁶/K at 20 °C and about 750 × 10⁻⁶/K near 100 °C. For large temperature changes use densities from a steam table instead of a single β.
  - q: Does the container expansion matter?
    a: Yes. A steel tank's volume grows by about 3α ≈ 36 × 10⁻⁶ per K, so the apparent expansion of the liquid is β − 3α. For most liquids this is a small correction.
  - q: "How much pressure builds up in a full, closed pipe when the liquid warms?"
    a: "A lot. For water near 20 °C, β divided by the compressibility of about 4.6 × 10⁻¹⁰ per Pa gives roughly 4.5 bar for every 1 °C rise in a rigid pipe. Pipe stretch reduces it somewhat, but a few degrees can exceed the rating of the pipe, which is why thermal relief valves are fitted."
  - q: "How full can I fill a tank to allow for expansion?"
    a: "Divide 1 by (1 + β ΔT). For petrol and a 20 K rise that is 98.1% of the tank volume, and most rules then add a margin so tanks are filled to about 95%."

## How to use the liquid thermal expansion calculator

Enter the liquid's **volumetric expansion coefficient β** in units of 10⁻⁶ per kelvin, the **initial volume** and the **initial and final temperatures**. The calculator returns the volume increase, the final volume and the percentage change. A negative result means the liquid contracts on cooling.

## Volumetric expansion formula

<p class="formula">ΔV = β × V<sub>0</sub> × (T<sub>2</sub> − T<sub>1</sub>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; V<sub>1</sub> = V<sub>0</sub> + ΔV</p>

## Example

A tank is filled with 1,000 L of petrol at 15 °C, which then warms to 35 °C in the sun (β = 950 × 10⁻⁶/K):

- ΔT = 35 − 15 = 20 K
- ΔV = 950 × 10⁻⁶ × 1000 × 20 = **19.0 L**
- Final volume = **1,019 L**, an increase of **1.9%**

## Expansion coefficients and volume change per 1,000 L

| Liquid | β (×10⁻⁶/K, near 20 °C) | ΔT = 10 K | ΔT = 20 K | ΔT = 40 K |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Water (at 20 °C) | 207 | 2.07 L | 4.14 L | 8.28 L |
| Mercury | 181 | 1.81 L | 3.62 L | 7.24 L |
| Glycerine | 505 | 5.05 L | 10.10 L | 20.20 L |
| Petrol (gasoline) | 950 | 9.50 L | 19.00 L | 38.00 L |
| Kerosene / jet fuel | 990 | 9.90 L | 19.80 L | 39.60 L |
| Ethanol | 1090 | 10.90 L | 21.80 L | 43.60 L |
| Acetone | 1430 | 14.30 L | 28.60 L | 57.20 L |

Coefficients are typical values near room temperature; they vary with temperature and composition.

## Why it matters

- **Blocked-in liquid**: a full, closed pipe that warms up builds pressure very quickly because liquids are nearly incompressible — fit a thermal relief valve.
- **Tank ullage**: leave enough free space for the expansion over the full storage temperature range.
- **Custody transfer**: fuel volumes are corrected to 15 °C (or 60 °F) using expansion tables.
- For solids (pipes, rails), use the linear [thermal expansion calculator](/engineering/thermal-expansion/).

## Beyond the basic result: the default petrol tank

The default case is 1,000 L of petrol (β = 950 × 10⁻⁶/K) warming from 15 °C to 35 °C, giving **19.0 L** of extra volume and **1,019 L** in total. Three follow on figures matter in practice:

- **Apparent expansion in a steel tank.** The tank grows too, by about 3 × 12 × 10⁻⁶ per K, so the level rises by the equivalent of (950 − 36) × 10⁻⁶ × 1,000 × 20 = **18.28 L**, not 19 L.
- **Density falls, mass stays.** 745 kg of petrol at 745 kg/m³ is still 745 kg at 35 °C, but the density drops to 745 ÷ 1.019 = **731.1 kg/m³**. That is why fuel is sold at a 15 °C reference volume.
- **Maximum fill.** To leave room for the 20 K rise, the tank may be filled to at most 1 ÷ 1.019 = **98.14%** of its capacity at 15 °C, before any safety margin.

## Maximum fill level for a temperature rise

Fill fraction that just reaches 100% after the rise, ignoring tank expansion:

| Liquid | +10 K | +20 K | +30 K | +40 K |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Water (20 °C) | 99.79% | 99.59% | 99.38% | 99.18% |
| Diesel | 99.18% | 98.37% | 97.57% | 96.79% |
| Petrol | 99.06% | 98.14% | 97.23% | 96.34% |
| Ethanol | 98.92% | 97.87% | 96.83% | 95.82% |

Regulations for road tankers and storage tanks usually add a margin on top, often filling to 95% or 97% at most.

## Where it is used

Hot water systems are fitted with expansion vessels sized from the system volume and the temperature rise. Liquid pipelines that can be isolated between two valves get thermal relief valves, because a trapped liquid that warms in the sun cannot expand. Custody transfer meters apply volume correction factors from API or ISO tables, which are a more exact form of the same β calculation.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the linear coefficient α.** For a liquid only the volumetric β exists; for a solid β ≈ 3α.
- **Dropping the 10⁻⁶.** Enter β as 950, not 0.00095, because the field is already in units of 10⁻⁶ per kelvin.
- **Using water's 20 °C value over a large range.** Water heated from 20 °C to 80 °C expands about 2.7%, roughly 6 times what β = 207 × 10⁻⁶ predicts; use densities instead.
- **Taking a β quoted per °F as per K.** A coefficient per °F must be multiplied by 1.8 before use here.
- **Ignoring pressure in a closed space.** A trapped liquid does not expand, it builds pressure, see the FAQ below.
