## How to use the baseball batting average & OBP calculator

Enter **at-bats**, **hits**, **walks**, **hit by pitch** and **sacrifice flies**. Batting average and on-base percentage are calculated.

## Baseball batting average & OBP formulas

<p class="formula">AVG = H ÷ AB   OBP = (H + BB + HBP) ÷ (AB + BB + HBP + SF)</p>

## Worked example with the default values

A hitter has 150 hits in 500 at-bats, with 60 walks, 5 hit by pitches and 5 sacrifice flies:

1. AVG = 150 ÷ 500 = **.300**
2. OBP numerator = 150 + 60 + 5 = 215
3. OBP denominator = 500 + 60 + 5 + 5 = 570
4. OBP = 215 ÷ 570 = 0.37719, shown as **.377**

So he reached base in about 37.7% of the 570 plate appearances that count for OBP, even though he hit safely in exactly 30% of his at-bats. The calculator shows OBP with a leading zero; box scores drop it and write .377.

## What's a good average and OBP?

| Rating | Batting average | On-base percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | .300+ | .390+ |
| Above average | .270–.299 | .340–.389 |
| Average | .250–.269 | .310–.339 |
| Below average | .230–.249 | .290–.309 |
| Poor | below .230 | below .290 |

Ranges are rough guides for modern Major League Baseball; league averages change from season to season.

## Slugging and OPS

Slugging percentage (SLG) weights extra-base hits:

<p class="formula">SLG = (1B + 2 × 2B + 3 × 3B + 4 × HR) ÷ AB</p>

**OPS** = OBP + SLG. An OPS of .800 is good; .900 or more is excellent.

## What counts as an at-bat

At-bats exclude walks, hit-by-pitch, sacrifice bunts, sacrifice flies and catcher's interference. That is why on-base percentage uses a different denominator from batting average.

## How hits change the line (500 AB, 60 BB, 5 HBP, 5 SF)

| Hits | AVG | OBP |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | .200 | .289 |
| 115 | .230 | .316 |
| 125 | .250 | .333 |
| 135 | .270 | .351 |
| 150 | .300 | .377 |
| 165 | .330 | .404 |

Each 5 hits in 500 at-bats moves the average by 10 points (.010), which is why a .290 and a .300 season differ by only one hit every two weeks.

## How walks change OBP (150 H in 500 AB)

| Walks | AVG | OBP |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | .300 | .304 |
| 30 | .300 | .343 |
| 60 | .300 | .377 |
| 90 | .300 | .408 |
| 120 | .300 | .437 |

Walks leave batting average untouched but lift OBP, which is why patient hitters are often more valuable than their average suggests.

## Chasing .300 late in the season

A hitter with 140 hits in 480 at-bats is at .292. To finish at .300 in 500 at-bats he needs 150 hits, so 10 hits in his last 20 at-bats. Working backwards like this is easier than adding hits by trial: target hits = target average × final at-bats.

## Practical notes

- **Take totals from the official line.** Box scores list AB, H, BB, HBP and SF separately; plate appearances also include sacrifice bunts and catcher's interference, which neither formula uses.
- **Sacrifice flies count only in OBP.** They are left out of at-bats, so they do not hurt batting average, but they are in the OBP denominator.
- **Small samples swing wildly.** In 20 at-bats one hit moves the average by 50 points; wait for a few hundred at-bats before judging a hitter.
- **Qualifying for titles.** Major League Baseball batting titles need 3.1 plate appearances per team game, about 502 in a 162 game season.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting walks as at-bats.** That lowers the average; walks, HBP and sacrifices are not at-bats.
- **Forgetting HBP in OBP.** Being hit by a pitch is a time on base and belongs in both the top and bottom of the formula.
- **Writing OBP as a percentage.** .377 is a rate of 0.377; it is spoken as "three seventy seven", not 37.7 points.
- **Comparing across eras without context.** A .270 average in a low scoring season can be better than .290 in a high scoring one.
- **Rounding before dividing.** Divide the raw totals and round only the final result to three decimals.
