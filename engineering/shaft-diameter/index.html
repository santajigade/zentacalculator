    A rotating shaft must carry its torque without the torsional shear stress at its surface exceeding a safe limit. This shaft diameter calculator converts power and speed into torque and sizes a solid round shaft from τ = 16T ÷ (πd³), using an allowable shear stress that already includes your margin.

    Mechanical and maintenance engineers use it to size pump, fan and gearbox shafts, check couplings and confirm that a replacement shaft is strong enough.

    How to use the shaft diameter calculator

    Enter the power to be transmitted, the shaft speed in rpm and the allowable shear stress for the shaft material. The calculator returns the torque and the minimum solid-shaft diameter; round up to the next standard size. To check an existing shaft, enter its diameter and clear the allowable stress to see the actual stress.

    Shaft diameter formula (torsion)

    T = P ÷ (2πN ÷ 60)  ·  τ = 16 T ÷ (π d³)  ·  d = ∛(16 T ÷ (π τ))

    Example

    A 15 kW motor running at 1,450 rpm, steel shaft with τallow = 40 MPa:

    • T = 15,000 ÷ (2π × 1450 ÷ 60) = 98.79 N·m
    • d = ∛(16 × 98.79 ÷ (π × 40 × 10⁶)) = 23.3 mm → choose a 25 mm shaft

    Notes

    • Keyways, shoulders and grooves concentrate stress; fatigue design for these features needs stress-concentration factors.
    • Long shafts may be governed by twist rather than strength — a common limit is 0.25° per metre for machinery shafts.
    • Check that a motor’s shaft size matches its output torque using the torque, power & speed calculator.

    Worked example: adding bending from a belt pulley

    The 15 kW, 1,450 rpm drive above gives T = 98.79 N·m and a pure torsion diameter of 23.3 mm. If an overhung pulley also puts a bending moment of 60 N·m on the shaft, use the equivalent torque:

    • Te = √(M² + T²) = √(60² + 98.79²) = 115.6 N·m
    • d = ∛(16 × 115.6 ÷ (π × 40 × 10⁶)) = 24.5 mm, so a 25 mm shaft still works but with less margin

    Enter Te as the torque (or the matching power) to size a shaft for combined loading.

    Hollow shafts and twist

    A hollow shaft with bore = 0.6 × outside diameter carries the same torque with do = ∛(16T ÷ (πτ(1 − 0.6⁴))) = 24.4 mm outside and 14.6 mm bore. It is only 5% larger in diameter but uses 70% of the material of the solid shaft.

    Stiffness can govern instead of strength. A 25 mm solid steel shaft (G = 79.3 GPa) carrying 98.8 N·m twists by θ = T L ÷ (G J) = 1.86° per metre, against a common machinery limit of 0.25° per metre, so a long line shaft would need to be larger than the stress check suggests.

    Common mistakes

    • Using rpm as angular velocity. Torque is P ÷ ω with ω in rad/s; ω = 2πN ÷ 60.
    • Sizing on average power. Use the maximum torque, including motor starting torque and any service factor for shock loads.
    • Entering yield strength as the allowable. The allowable shear stress must already include the safety margin and keyway allowance.
    • Forgetting bending. Pulleys, sprockets and gears on the shaft add bending that often exceeds the torsion.
    • Not rounding up. Choose the next standard size above the result, then check that the keyway and bearing seats still leave enough section.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do you calculate shaft diameter from torque?

    For a solid round shaft, d = ∛(16T ÷ (π τ)), where T is the torque and τ the allowable shear stress. Torque comes from power and speed, T = P ÷ (2πN/60).

    What allowable shear stress should I use?

    The ASME shaft code suggests the lower of 18% of ultimate strength or 30% of yield, reduced by 25% for keyways. For commercial mild-steel shafting this gives about 40 MPa.

    Does this account for bending?

    No. Shafts carrying pulleys, gears or overhung loads also bend. Then combine them with the equivalent torque √(M² + T²) in place of T, and apply shock factors for the load type.

    How does speed affect shaft size?

    At the same power, torque is inversely proportional to speed, and diameter grows with the cube root of torque. Halving the speed increases the diameter by about 26%.

    How much lighter is a hollow shaft for the same torque?

    With a bore of 0.6 times the outside diameter, the outside diameter grows by only about 5% but the cross section, and so the weight, drops to about 70% of a solid shaft. Material near the centre carries little torsional stress.

    How do I check shaft twist?

    Use θ = T L ÷ (G J) with J = π d⁴ ÷ 32. A 25 mm steel shaft carrying 98.8 N·m twists about 1.86° per metre. Many machinery guidelines limit twist to about 0.25° per metre.
