## How to use the Gibbs free energy calculator

Enter **ΔH** (kJ/mol), **ΔS** (J/mol·K) and the **temperature**. ΔG is calculated and labelled spontaneous or not.

## Gibbs free energy formula

<p class="formula">ΔG = ΔH − T ΔS</p>

## Worked example — ammonia synthesis

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, ΔH = −92.2 kJ/mol, ΔS = −198.7 J/mol·K, at 25 °C:

ΔG = −92.2 − 298.15 × (−0.1987) = **-33.0 kJ/mol** → spontaneous

It stops being spontaneous above T = 92.2 ÷ 0.1987 ≈ 464 K (191 °C).

## Crossover temperature

When ΔH and ΔS have the same sign, the reaction switches between spontaneous and non-spontaneous at:

<p class="formula">T = ΔH ÷ ΔS</p>

For ammonia synthesis (ΔH = −92.2 kJ/mol, ΔS = −198.7 J/mol·K), T = 92,200 ÷ 198.7 = **464 K (191 °C)**. Below this temperature the forward reaction is favoured; above it, the reverse.

## Gibbs energy and equilibrium

ΔG° is linked to the equilibrium constant:

<p class="formula">ΔG° = −R × T × ln K</p>

| ΔG° (kJ/mol, 25 °C) | K |
|---|---|
| -40 | 1.02e+07 |
| -20 | 3.19e+03 |
| -10 | 56.5 |
| 0 | 1 |
| 10 | 0.0177 |
| 20 | 0.000313 |
| 40 | 9.82e-08 |

Every 5.7 kJ/mol change in ΔG° at 25 °C changes K by a factor of 10.

## Ammonia synthesis at plant temperatures

Treating ΔH and ΔS as constant, the calculator equation gives ΔG for N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ at higher temperatures, and K = e<sup>−ΔG/RT</sup> shows how the equilibrium constant falls:

| Temperature | T | ΔG | K |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 °C | 298.15 K | −33.0 kJ/mol | 5.94 × 10<sup>5</sup> |
| 100 °C | 373.15 K | −18.1 kJ/mol | 3.37 × 10<sup>2</sup> |
| 200 °C | 473.15 K | 1.8 kJ/mol | 6.30 × 10<sup>−1</sup> |
| 300 °C | 573.15 K | 21.7 kJ/mol | 1.06 × 10<sup>−2</sup> |
| 400 °C | 673.15 K | 41.6 kJ/mol | 5.96 × 10<sup>−4</sup> |
| 450 °C | 723.15 K | 51.5 kJ/mol | 1.91 × 10<sup>−4</sup> |
| 500 °C | 773.15 K | 61.4 kJ/mol | 7.08 × 10<sup>−5</sup> |

At 25 °C, K is about 5.94 × 10<sup>5</sup>, so the equilibrium lies far towards ammonia, but the reaction is too slow without a catalyst. Industrial Haber plants run near 450 °C, where ΔG is 51.5 kJ/mol and K is below 1, and they compensate with pressures of 150 to 300 bar and an iron catalyst, recycling the unreacted gas. This trade between thermodynamics and rate is the classic use of ΔG in process design.

## Second example: when does limestone decompose?

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ has ΔH = +178.3 kJ/mol and ΔS = +160.6 J/mol·K, both positive, so it becomes favourable only when hot:

- At 25 °C: ΔG = 178.3 − 298.15 × 0.1606 = **+130.4 kJ/mol**, so limestone is stable
- Crossover: T = 178,300 ÷ 160.6 = **1,110 K** (837 °C)
- At 900 °C: ΔG = 178.3 − 1,173.15 × 0.1606 = **−10.1 kJ/mol**, so lime kilns run above about 900 °C

## Where ΔH and ΔS come from

Values are usually built from tables of standard enthalpies of formation (ΔH<sub>f</sub>°) and standard molar entropies (S°): ΔH = Σ ΔH<sub>f</sub>°(products) − Σ ΔH<sub>f</sub>°(reactants), and ΔS the same way with S°, each multiplied by its stoichiometric coefficient. ΔH can also be measured directly in a calorimeter. For redox reactions ΔG is measured electrically from the cell voltage, ΔG = −nFE, and for others it comes from a measured equilibrium constant through ΔG° = −RT ln K.

## Spontaneous is not the same as fast

A negative ΔG means a reaction can proceed, not that it will be quick. Diamond turning into graphite has a negative ΔG at room conditions but takes an immeasurably long time. Speed depends on activation energy — see the [Arrhenius calculator](/chemistry/arrhenius-equation/).

## Units

ΔH is usually in kJ/mol and ΔS in J/mol·K. Divide ΔS by 1,000 before combining them, as the calculator does.

## The four cases

| ΔH | ΔS | Spontaneous? |
|---|---|---|
| − | + | Always |
| + | − | Never |
| − | − | At low temperatures |
| + | + | At high temperatures |

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving ΔS in J/mol·K when combining by hand.** −92.2 − 298.15 × (−198.7) gives 59,150, which mixes kJ and J; dividing ΔS by 1,000 gives the correct −32.96 kJ/mol.
- **Using Celsius for T.** Putting 25 instead of 298.15 gives −87.2 kJ/mol, far too negative; the formula needs kelvin.
- **Forgetting that ΔG is per mole of reaction as written.** The ammonia value is for 2 mol of NH₃; per mole of ammonia it is −16.5 kJ/mol. Doubling every coefficient doubles ΔG.
- **Confusing ΔG with ΔG°.** Tables give standard values (1 bar, 1 mol/L). At other compositions ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q; with Q = 10<sup>−4</sup> the ammonia reaction at 25 °C has ΔG = −55.8 kJ/mol.
- **Extrapolating too far.** ΔH and ΔS drift with temperature and phase changes, so values from 25 °C give estimates, not exact figures, hundreds of degrees away.
