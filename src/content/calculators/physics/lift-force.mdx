## How to use the lift force calculator

Enter the **air density**, the **airspeed**, the **wing (reference) area** and the **lift coefficient**. The calculator returns the lift force and the mass that lift can hold up against gravity. To find the speed needed to fly, enter the mass and clear the airspeed. The same equation works for hydrofoils — use water's density.

## Lift equation

<p class="formula">L = ½ ρ v² S C<sub>L</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; m = L ÷ g</p>

## Example

A light aircraft with 16 m² of wing flies at 50 m/s (97 knots) in sea-level air with C<sub>L</sub> = 0.5:

- L = 0.5 × 1.225 × 50² × 16 × 0.5 = **12,250 N**
- Mass supported = 12,250 ÷ 9.80665 = **1,249 kg**

## Notes

- C<sub>L</sub> rises roughly linearly with angle of attack (about 0.1 per degree for a typical wing) until the stall.
- Lift always comes with induced drag; estimate the parasitic part with the [drag force calculator](/physics/drag-force/).
- Use true airspeed, not indicated airspeed, with the actual air density.

## Dynamic pressure: the part the pilot controls

The product ½ρv² is the dynamic pressure q, the quantity a pitot-static system actually senses. With the default inputs q = 0.5 × 1.225 × 50² = **1,531.25 Pa**. Lift is then simply q × S × C<sub>L</sub> = 1,531.25 × 16 × 0.5 = 12,250 N. In a wind tunnel the process runs backwards: a force balance measures L, the tunnel instruments give q, and the lift coefficient is found as C<sub>L</sub> = L ÷ (q S). That is how published airfoil and wing C<sub>L</sub> curves are produced.

## Speed needed to carry 1,249 kg at different lift coefficients

Holding lift at the default 12,250 N in sea-level air, the wing must fly slower as C<sub>L</sub> (angle of attack) rises:

| C<sub>L</sub> | Airspeed for level flight | In knots |
|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | 64.5 m/s | 125 kn |
| 0.5 | 50.0 m/s | 97 kn |
| 0.8 | 39.5 m/s | 77 kn |
| 1 | 35.4 m/s | 69 kn |
| 1.2 | 32.3 m/s | 63 kn |
| 1.5 | 28.9 m/s | 56 kn |

Taking C<sub>L,max</sub> = 1.5 for the clean wing gives a stall speed of **28.9 m/s**; with flaps raising C<sub>L,max</sub> to 2.1 it drops to **24.4 m/s**. Lift-off is normally planned at about 1.1 to 1.2 times the stall speed in the take-off configuration.

## Extra lift needed in a turn

In a level turn the lift vector tilts, so the wing must produce W ÷ cos φ to keep the vertical component equal to the weight. The ratio is the load factor n, and the stall speed rises with √n:

| Bank angle | Load factor n | Lift required | Clean stall speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0° | 1.000 | 12,250 N | 28.9 m/s |
| 15° | 1.035 | 12,682 N | 29.4 m/s |
| 30° | 1.155 | 14,145 N | 31.0 m/s |
| 45° | 1.414 | 17,324 N | 34.3 m/s |
| 60° | 2.000 | 24,500 N | 40.8 m/s |

At 60° of bank the wing carries 2 g, so a turn flown near the stall speed in level flight will stall long before the bank is reached.

## Common mistakes

- **Using knots or km/h as m/s.** 50 knots is only 25.7 m/s, and because speed is squared the lift comes out almost four times too high.
- **Using the wrong reference area.** S is the planform wing area quoted by the manufacturer, not the wetted surface area of both sides of the wing.
- **Using a 2D airfoil coefficient for the whole wing.** Section data (c<sub>l</sub>) from airfoil catalogues is higher than the finite wing C<sub>L</sub>, especially for low aspect ratio wings.
- **Assuming C<sub>L</sub> keeps rising with angle of attack.** Past the stall the flow separates and C<sub>L</sub> falls, so the equation with a large C<sub>L</sub> gives lift the wing cannot make.
- **Forgetting that lift must exceed weight in manoeuvres.** Turns and pull-ups need load factors above 1, as the bank table shows.
