## How to use the buoyancy calculator

Enter the **volume** of the object, the **density of the fluid** and the object's **mass**. The calculator gives the **buoyant force** when the object is fully submerged, its **weight**, its **apparent weight** underwater, its **average density** and, if it floats, the **fraction of its volume below the surface**. A negative apparent weight means the object floats up. To find the volume of an irregular object from a weighing in water, enter the apparent weight and press × on the volume.

## Buoyancy formulas

<p class="formula">F<sub>b</sub> = ρ<sub>fluid</sub> g V &nbsp;·&nbsp; W = m g &nbsp;·&nbsp; W<sub>apparent</sub> = W − F<sub>b</sub></p>

<p class="formula">ρ<sub>object</sub> = m ÷ V &nbsp;·&nbsp; fraction submerged = ρ<sub>object</sub> ÷ ρ<sub>fluid</sub> (if less than 1)</p>

## Worked example

A 10 L object with a mass of 8 kg, held fully under fresh water (1000 kg/m³):

- Buoyant force = 1000 × 9.80665 × 0.01 = **98.07 N**
- Weight = 8 × 9.80665 = **78.45 N**
- Apparent weight = 78.45 − 98.07 = **−19.61 N** — the object is pushed up and will float
- Average density = 8 ÷ 0.01 = **800 kg/m³**, so it floats with **80%** of its volume underwater

## Weighing in water

Weighing an object in air and then fully under water gives its density without measuring the volume: ρ<sub>object</sub> = ρ<sub>water</sub> × W<sub>air</sub> ÷ (W<sub>air</sub> − W<sub>water</sub>). Archimedes is said to have used this to test whether a crown was pure gold, and it is still used to measure body fat and the density of minerals.

## Practical notes

- **Balloons and airships** float in air for the same reason: the lift is (ρ<sub>air</sub> − ρ<sub>gas</sub>) × g × V, about 10.2 N (a lift of roughly 1 kg) per cubic metre of helium at 20 °C and sea-level pressure.
- **Level measurement:** displacer level transmitters work on buoyancy — the apparent weight of a long float falls as the liquid rises around it.
- Water density changes with temperature and salinity, so a ship floats a little higher in cold seawater than in warm fresh water; load lines on the hull mark the allowed draught for each.

## Reading the default result

With the defaults the object displaces 10 L of fresh water, so the water pushes up with **98.07 N** while gravity pulls down with **78.45 N**. The apparent weight of **-19.61 N** is negative, which means a hand would have to push down with 19.61 N to hold it under. Let go and it rises until only 80% of its volume, 8 L, is below the surface. The same object could carry another **2 kg** before it sank completely.

## Finding density by weighing in water

A rock reads 2.65 kg on a hanging scale in air and 1.65 kg fully under fresh water. The loss of 1.00 kg is the mass of water displaced, so its volume is 1.00 L and its density is 2.65 ÷ 0.001 = **2,650 kg/m³**, typical of quartz and granite. Enter a volume of 1 L, a fluid density of 1,000 and a mass of 2.65 kg to check it: the apparent weight comes out at 16.18 N, which is 1.65 kg × g.

## Sizing a lift bag

A 50 kg steel anchor lies on the seabed. Its volume is 50 ÷ 7,850 = 6.37 L, so seawater supports only 64.0 N of its 490.3 N weight and it still pulls down with **426.3 N**. A lift bag must displace at least 426.3 ÷ (1,025 × 9.81) = **42.4 L** of seawater to make it neutral, so divers use a 50 L or larger bag and let air out as it rises, because the air in the bag expands as the pressure falls.

## Payload of a drum raft

Four 200 L drums (18 kg each) under a 40 kg deck can displace 800 kg of fresh water when fully submerged. Subtracting the raft's own mass leaves the load it can carry before the drums go under:

| Water | Density | Mass displaced (drums under) | Payload before sinking |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh water | 1,000 kg/m³ | 800 kg | 688 kg |
| Seawater | 1,025 kg/m³ | 820 kg | 708 kg |
| Dead Sea brine | 1,240 kg/m³ | 992 kg | 880 kg |

In practice keep the drums no more than about half submerged, which roughly halves the payload, so the raft keeps freeboard and stability in waves.

## Buoyancy in level and density instruments

A [displacer level transmitter](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/displacer-level-transmitter-working-principle/) hangs a long cylinder in the vessel; as the liquid rises the buoyant force on it grows, and a torque tube or spring measures the drop in apparent weight. Hydrometers use the opposite idea: a fixed weight floats deeper in a lighter liquid, and the stem scale reads density or specific gravity directly.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering the object's mass in place of its displaced volume.** Buoyancy depends only on the volume below the surface, not on what the object is made of.
- **Using the whole volume for a floating object.** A partly floating body displaces only its submerged part; enter that volume to get the force actually acting.
- **Forgetting the fluid changes.** Seawater gives about 2.5% more lift than fresh water, so ballast trimmed in a lake is too light at sea.
- **Mixing litres and cubic metres.** 1 L is 0.001 m³; a slip here makes the force 1,000 times wrong.
- **Treating hollow objects by their material density.** A steel tank floats if its average density, including the air inside, is below the fluid's, which is what the calculator's mass and volume give.
