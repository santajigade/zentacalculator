## How to use the buck-boost converter calculator

Enter the **input voltage**, the **magnitude** of the negative output voltage, the **output current**, the expected **efficiency**, the **switching frequency**, the inductor **ripple ratio** (20–40% of the inductor current is typical) and the allowed **output ripple**. The calculator returns the duty cycle, the average input and inductor currents, the minimum inductance, the peak current the switch and inductor must handle, the voltage stress on the switch and diode, and the minimum output capacitance.

## Buck-boost converter formulas (continuous conduction)

<p class="formula">D = |V<sub>o</sub>| ÷ (V<sub>i</sub> η + |V<sub>o</sub>|) &nbsp;·&nbsp; I<sub>L</sub> = I<sub>o</sub> ÷ (1 − D) &nbsp;·&nbsp; L = V<sub>i</sub> D ÷ (f · ΔI<sub>L</sub>)</p>

<p class="formula">I<sub>pk</sub> = I<sub>L</sub> (1 + r/2) &nbsp;·&nbsp; V<sub>sw</sub> = V<sub>i</sub> + |V<sub>o</sub>| &nbsp;·&nbsp; C ≥ I<sub>o</sub> D ÷ (f · ΔV)</p>

The output capacitor alone supplies the load while the switch is on, so — as in a [boost converter](/electronics/boost-converter/) — the output current is pulsed and the capacitor's ESR matters as much as its capacitance.

## Worked example

+12 V in, −12 V at 0.5 A out, η = 85%, f = 500 kHz, 30% ripple, 50 mV output ripple:

- D = 12 ÷ (12 × 0.85 + 12) = **54.05%**
- Average input current = **0.588 A**; average inductor current = 0.5 ÷ (1 − 0.5405) = **1.0882 A**
- L = 12 × 0.5405 ÷ (500 kHz × 0.3 × 1.0882 A) = **39.74 µH** — choose a standard 47 µH
- Peak current = **1.2515 A**; switch voltage = **24 V**
- Output capacitance ≥ **10.81 µF** (plus margin for ESR and DC-bias derating)

## Design notes

- Rate the inductor's **saturation current** above the peak current, not the average.
- Use low-ESR ceramic output capacitors; ESR ripple = I<sub>pk</sub> × ESR adds to the capacitive ripple.
- The control loop has a right-half-plane zero, as in a boost, which limits loop bandwidth — follow the controller datasheet's compensation guide.

## Second example: a battery input from 9 to 16 V

The default design takes 7.06 W from the input to deliver 6 W, so about 1.06 W is lost in the switch, diode and inductor. Now suppose the input is a vehicle or lead-acid battery that varies from 9 to 16 V while the output stays at −12 V, 0.5 A, and the standard **47 µH** inductor chosen at 12 V is kept. The ripple with a fixed inductor is ΔI = V<sub>i</sub> D ÷ (f L):

| Input | Duty cycle | Inductor current | Ripple ΔI | Peak current | Switch voltage | C (min.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9 V | 61.1% | 1.284 A | 0.234 A | 1.401 A | 21 V | 12.21 µF |
| 12 V | 54.1% | 1.088 A | 0.276 A | 1.226 A | 24 V | 10.81 µF |
| 16 V | 46.9% | 0.941 A | 0.319 A | 1.101 A | 28 V | 9.38 µF |

The **peak current is highest at the lowest input** (1.401 A at 9 V), so the inductor saturation rating and the controller current limit are set there. The **ripple and the switch voltage are highest at the highest input** (0.319 A and 28 V at 16 V), so a 40 V MOSFET and diode give sensible margin. The output capacitor is sized at the minimum input, where the duty cycle and so the on-time are longest.

## Effect of switching frequency

Default 12 V to −12 V at 0.5 A, 30% ripple and 50 mV output ripple:

| Frequency | L required | C (min.) |
|---|---|---|
| 100 kHz | 198.7 µH | 54.05 µF |
| 250 kHz | 79.5 µH | 21.62 µF |
| 500 kHz | 39.7 µH | 10.81 µF |
| 1000 kHz | 19.9 µH | 5.41 µF |
| 2000 kHz | 9.9 µH | 2.70 µF |

Higher frequency shrinks both parts in proportion, but switching loss rises with frequency, which is why 300 kHz to 1 MHz is a common compromise at this power level.

## Building it from a buck regulator

Many designs use an ordinary synchronous buck IC as an inverting buck-boost by tying its ground pin to the −12 V output. The IC then sees V<sub>i</sub> + |V<sub>o</sub>| between its input and ground pins, 24 V in the default case, so it must be rated for that sum rather than for the 12 V input. Its enable and logic pins are also referenced to the negative rail and may need level shifting.

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing the inductor at the nominal input only.** At 9 V the peak current rises to 1.401 A, about 14% above the 1.226 A at 12 V.
- **Ignoring ESR ripple.** A capacitor with 20 mΩ ESR adds 1.2515 × 0.020 = 25 mV, half of the 50 mV budget on its own.
- **Forgetting DC bias derating.** A 10 µF X7R ceramic can lose half its value at 12 V; check the bias curve.
- **Reversing polarised output capacitors.** The output is negative, so an electrolytic's positive lead goes to ground.
- **Probing with a long ground lead.** The pulsed output current makes the ripple look far worse than it is; use a short ground spring at the capacitor.
