## How to use the Sallen-Key low-pass filter calculator

Enter the two resistors and two capacitors of a **unity-gain** Sallen-Key low-pass stage: **R₁** from the input, **R₂** to the op-amp's non-inverting input, **C₁** from the R₁–R₂ junction back to the output, and **C₂** from the non-inverting input to ground. Add a **frequency** to see the gain there.

## Sallen-Key low-pass formulas

<p class="formula">f₀ = 1 / (2π √(R₁R₂C₁C₂)) &nbsp;·&nbsp; Q = √(R₁R₂C₁C₂) / [C₂(R₁ + R₂)]</p>

<p class="formula">|H(f)| = 1 / √[(1 − (f/f₀)²)² + (f / (f₀Q))²]</p>

## Worked example

R₁ = R₂ = 10 kΩ, C₁ = 22 nF, C₂ = 10 nF:

- f₀ = 1 ÷ (2π √(10k × 10k × 22n × 10n)) = **1,073.0 Hz**
- Q = ½ √(22/10) = **0.7416** — close to Butterworth
- At 5 kHz the gain is **0.0462** (-26.7 dB)

## Response types for an equal-resistor design (R₁ = R₂)

| Response | Q | C₁ / C₂ = 4Q² | Gain at f₀ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critically damped | 0.5000 | 1.000 | -6.02 dB |
| Bessel | 0.5774 | 1.333 | -4.77 dB |
| Butterworth | 0.7071 | 2.000 | -3.01 dB |
| Q = 1 | 1.0000 | 4.000 | +0.00 dB |
| Chebyshev 1 dB ripple | 0.9565 | 3.660 | -0.39 dB |

For the Chebyshev case f₀ is the natural frequency, not the −3 dB point.

## Design notes

- Use C0G/NP0 or film capacitors; X7R ceramics change value with voltage and add distortion.
- The op-amp's gain-bandwidth product should be at least 100 × f₀ × Q for an accurate response.
- High-frequency feedthrough limits the stop-band depth of Sallen-Key low-pass filters; a multiple-feedback (MFB) topology is better when deep attenuation is needed.

## Where the default filter really cuts off

With the default parts Q is 0.7416, a little above the Butterworth value of 0.7071, so f₀ is not quite the −3 dB point. Solving |H| = 0.7071 gives **1,123 Hz**, about 4.6% above f₀. The response also peaks slightly: the maximum gain is **1.0042** (+0.036 dB) at 324 Hz. That is far too small to hear or see on a scope, but it shows how Q shapes the knee.

| Frequency | Value | Gain | Gain (dB) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 f₀ | 537 Hz | 0.9916 | -0.07 dB |
| 1 f₀ | 1,073 Hz | 0.7416 | -2.60 dB |
| 2 f₀ | 2,146 Hz | 0.2479 | -12.11 dB |
| 4 f₀ | 4,292 Hz | 0.0627 | -24.05 dB |
| 10 f₀ | 10,730 Hz | 0.0100 | -39.99 dB |

Above about 2 f₀ each doubling of frequency costs close to 12 dB, the second-order slope.

## Scaling the design to a new frequency

To move the same response to about 10 kHz, keep both capacitors and divide both resistors by the same factor. Q depends only on the ratios, so it stays at 0.7416. The ideal value is 10 kΩ × 1,073 ÷ 10,000 = 1,073 Ω; the nearest E24 value, **1.1 kΩ**, gives f₀ = **9,755 Hz**. Scaling capacitors instead works the same way, but resistors come in finer steps.

## Checking a built filter

Drive the input from a signal generator at about 1 V and sweep the frequency while a two-channel scope or a Bode analyser compares input and output. The cleanest marker is phase: a second-order low-pass is exactly −90° at f₀ regardless of Q, so it confirms the natural frequency even when the −3 dB point has moved. Then read the gain at f₀, which equals Q, to check the capacitor ratio. Parts with 5% tolerance can move f₀ by several percent.

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping C₁ and C₂.** With 10 nF in the feedback position and 22 nF to ground, Q falls to 0.337 and the knee becomes soft and early.
- **Ignoring the source resistance.** The driving impedance adds to R₁, lowering f₀ and changing Q, so drive the filter from a buffer or include it in R₁.
- **Choosing a slow op-amp.** The default needs a gain-bandwidth product of at least 79.6 kHz by the 100 × f₀ × Q rule; slower parts shift the response.
- **Using the unity-gain Q formula for a gain stage.** Adding gain resistors to the op-amp changes Q, so use the equal-component gain-set equations instead.
- **Expecting endless stop-band rejection.** Output impedance rising with frequency lets signal leak through C₁, so attenuation stops improving well above f₀.
