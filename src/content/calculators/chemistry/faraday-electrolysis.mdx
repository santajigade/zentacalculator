## How to use the Faraday's law of electrolysis calculator

Enter the **current**, the **time**, the **metal and its ion** (which fixes molar mass ÷ electrons) and the **current efficiency**. The calculator returns the charge passed, the moles of electrons and the mass deposited at the cathode (or dissolved from the anode). Clear the time or current to find how long, or how much current, a target mass needs.

## Faraday's law of electrolysis formula

<p class="formula">m = (I × t × M × η) ÷ (z × F)</p>

<p class="formula">Q = I × t &nbsp;·&nbsp; n<sub>e</sub> = Q ÷ F &nbsp;·&nbsp; F = 96,485.332 C/mol</p>

M ÷ z is the mass deposited per mole of electrons; the chromium option uses Cr³⁺ (z = 3) — decorative and hard chrome plating from chromic acid is Cr(VI), z = 6, which halves the mass per coulomb.

## Example

Copper plating from an acid sulfate (Cu²⁺) bath at 2 A for 1 hour, 100% efficiency:

- Q = 2 × 3,600 = **7,200 C**
- n<sub>e</sub> = 7,200 ÷ 96,485 = **0.07462 mol** of electrons
- m = 0.07462 × 63.546 ÷ 2 = **2.3710 g** of copper

## Notes

- Divide the mass by density and plated area for thickness — copper is 8.96 g/cm³, so 2.37 g over 100 cm² is about 2.6 µm.
- Real plating thickness varies across a part; edges and corners plate faster than recesses.
- The same law gives the hydrogen produced by water electrolysis: about 0.0376 g (0.42 L at 0 °C, 1 atm) per ampere-hour.

## Plating time for a target thickness

The default case (2 A for 1 h on copper at 100%) gives Q = 7,200 C, n<sub>e</sub> = 0.07462 mol and **2.3710 g** of copper. In the shop the question usually runs the other way: how long to reach a specified thickness. Take a 10 µm nickel layer on 200 cm² at 2 A from a Watts bath running at 95% efficiency:

- Mass needed = 10 × 10⁻⁴ cm × 200 cm² × 8.908 g/cm³ = **1.7816 g**
- Current density = 2 A ÷ 2 dm² = 1 A/dm², a modest value for a Watts nickel bath
- Time = m × F ÷ (I × M/z × η) = 1.7816 × 96,485 ÷ (2 × 29.3467 × 0.95) = **3,083 s (51.4 min)**

Enter the mass, the current and the efficiency with the time field cleared and the calculator gives the same result.

## Deposition rate at 1 A/dm²

Platers think in current density rather than total current, because thickness depends on amps per unit area. At 1 A/dm² and 100% efficiency the average build-up rate is (0.01 A/cm² × 60 s × M/z) ÷ (F × density):

| Metal and ion | Density | Thickness rate | Time for 10 µm |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper, Cu²⁺ | 8.96 g/cm³ | 0.221 µm/min | 272.1 min |
| Silver, Ag⁺ | 10.49 g/cm³ | 0.639 µm/min | 93.8 min |
| Nickel, Ni²⁺ | 8.908 g/cm³ | 0.205 µm/min | 292.9 min |
| Zinc, Zn²⁺ | 7.14 g/cm³ | 0.285 µm/min | 210.7 min |
| Tin, Sn²⁺ | 7.265 g/cm³ | 0.508 µm/min | 118.1 min |
| Gold, Au⁺ | 19.32 g/cm³ | 0.634 µm/min | 94.6 min |
| Chromium, Cr³⁺ | 7.19 g/cm³ | 0.150 µm/min | 400.3 min |

Divide the time by the current density in A/dm² and by the efficiency. Hard chromium is far slower than the Cr³⁺ figure suggests, because chromic acid baths work at z = 6 and only 10 to 25% efficiency.

## How it is used in practice

Plating rectifiers carry an ampere-hour meter, and the line controller stops each load once the ampere-hours calculated from Faraday's law are reached. Bath efficiency is checked by plating a weighed test panel or by a copper coulometer in series: if the panel gains 2.181 g where 2.371 g was expected, the bath is running at 92%. Electrowinning and copper refining tanks use the same figure to report current efficiency to management, since every lost percent is wasted energy.

## Common mistakes

- **Using hours instead of seconds.** Q in coulombs needs time in seconds; 1 A·h is 3,600 C.
- **Wrong ion charge.** Copper from a cyanide bath is Cu⁺ (z = 1) and deposits twice the mass per coulomb of an acid Cu²⁺ bath.
- **Using total current for one part.** On a loaded rack the current divides between parts by area, so compute per part with its own share.
- **Ignoring hidden area.** Racks, hooks and the inside of tubes take current too, which lowers the thickness on the area you measure.
- **Assuming average thickness is the minimum.** Specifications set a minimum at significant surfaces, so plate long enough that recesses reach it.
