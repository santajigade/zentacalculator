## How to use the free fall calculator

Enter the **height**, the **fall time** or the **impact speed** — the other two are calculated. Results ignore air resistance, which is accurate for short drops of dense objects.

## Free fall formulas

<p class="formula">h = ½ g t²   v = g t   v = √(2gh)</p>

## Worked example

A stone dropped from 20 m, with g = 9.80665 m/s²:

- t = √(2 × 20 ÷ 9.80665) = **2.020 s**
- v = 9.80665 × 2.020 = **19.81 m/s** (71.3 km/h)

The average speed over the drop is 20 ÷ 2.020 = 9.90 m/s, exactly half the impact speed, as it must be when the speed rises steadily from zero. The stone covers only 4.90 m in its first second but 14.90 m in its final second. Halving the drop to 10 m does not halve the time: it still takes 1.428 s, because time grows with the square root of height.

## Air resistance and terminal velocity

Real objects do not keep accelerating. Air resistance grows with speed until it balances weight, and the object falls at **terminal velocity**:

| Object | Approximate terminal velocity |
|---|---|
| Raindrop | 9 m/s (32 km/h) |
| Skydiver, belly down | 55 m/s (200 km/h) |
| Skydiver, head down | 80–90 m/s (300 km/h) |
| Feather | under 1 m/s |

The calculator ignores air resistance, so it is accurate for short drops of dense objects, but overestimates speed for long falls.

Distance grows with the square of time: in 2 seconds an object falls four times as far as in 1 second.

## Galileo's insight

Without air resistance, all objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass. Astronaut David Scott demonstrated this on the Moon in 1971 by dropping a hammer and a feather, which landed together.

## Measuring a well's depth

Drop a stone and time the splash. If it takes 2 s, the depth is about 19.6 m (a little less once you allow for the time sound takes to travel back up).

### Correcting for the sound delay

Suppose the splash is heard 2.0 s after release. Ignoring sound, h = ½ g t² = 19.6 m. But the total time is the fall time plus the time for sound to climb back at about 343 m/s, so √(2h ÷ g) + h ÷ 343 = 2.0. Solving this as a quadratic in √h gives a depth of **18.6 m**: the stone falls for 1.946 s and the sound takes 0.054 s.

## Using free fall in practice

Packaging is tested by dropping it from set heights; a 0.76 m drop, a common test height for small parcels, means an impact at √(2 g h) = 3.86 m/s. Site safety teams use the same formula to show why tool lanyards matter: a spanner falling from a 20 m scaffold arrives at 71 km/h. In the laboratory, g itself is measured by timing a ball between two light gates, or by filming a drop against a scale and reading the position frame by frame.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the formulas for an object thrown down or up.** They assume a release from rest; with a starting velocity use the [SUVAT equations calculator](/physics/suvat-equations/).
- **Rounding g to 10 m/s².** That gives 2.000 s for the 20 m drop instead of 2.020 s, an error of about 1%.
- **Applying it to light or long falls.** A sheet of paper or a skydiver after a few seconds is dominated by drag, and the real speed is far below g t.
- **Expecting double the height to double the time.** Time scales with √h, so a 40 m drop takes only √2 times as long.
- **Forgetting that h is measured to the impact point**, not to the ground under the release point when the object lands on something higher.
