## How to use the birthday paradox calculator

Enter the **number of people** in the group. The calculator returns the probability that at least two of them share a birthday, the probability that all birthdays are different, the chance that at least one other person shares *your* birthday, and the number of pairs. Change the number of days to apply the same maths to other "collisions", such as hash values or ID numbers.

## Birthday problem formula

<p class="formula">P(all different) = d × (d − 1) × … × (d − n + 1) ÷ dⁿ = ₙP(d, n) ÷ dⁿ</p>

<p class="formula">P(shared) = 1 − P(all different) &nbsp;·&nbsp; P(shares yours) = 1 − ((d − 1) ÷ d)<sup>n−1</sup></p>

## Worked example with the default inputs

For n = 23 people and d = 365 days:

- Pairs = 23 × 22 ÷ 2 = **253**
- P(all different) = 365 × 364 × … × 343 ÷ 365²³ = **49.27%**
- P(at least one shared birthday) = **50.73%**
- P(someone shares your birthday) = 1 − (364 ÷ 365)²² = **5.86%**

Each of the 253 pairs has only a 1 in 365 chance of matching, but there are so many pairs that a match becomes more likely than not. Your own birthday is involved in just 22 of them, which is why the personal figure is so much lower.

## The quick approximation

For mental arithmetic, treat each pair as an independent 1/d chance:

<p class="formula">P(shared) ≈ 1 − e<sup>−n(n−1) ÷ 2d</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; n(50%) ≈ 1.1774 √d</p>

## Collisions in computing

The same formula applies to any set of equally likely values. A 32 bit hash has d = 2³² values, yet a 50% chance of a collision arrives after only about √(2 × 2³² × ln 2) = **77,163** items. A 64 bit hash pushes that to about 5.1 billion. Among 100 people choosing random 4 digit PINs, the chance that two pick the same one is **39.1%**. This is why identifiers and checksums are sized with the birthday bound, not the number of possible values.

## Trying it in a class

A group of 30 to 40 students is the classic demonstration: the calculator gives 71% for 30 and 89% for 40, so a match usually appears. Ask people to call out birthdays month by month; the first repeat ends the experiment.

## Common mistakes

- **Answering the personal question.** "Does anyone share my birthday?" needs 253 other people for 50%. "Do any two people share?" needs only 23.
- **Dividing n by 365.** 23 ÷ 365 = 6.3% ignores that every pair is a chance for a match.
- **Adding probabilities across pairs.** 253 ÷ 365 = 69% overcounts; the pairs overlap, so multiply the "all different" chances instead.
- **Forgetting 29 February.** Using 366 days changes the result for 23 people only to 50.63%, so it rarely matters.
- **Assuming uniform birthdays is conservative for the wrong side.** Real birth dates cluster by season, which makes a match slightly more likely, not less.
