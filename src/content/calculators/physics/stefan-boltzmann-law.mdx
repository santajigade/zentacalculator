## How to use the Stefan–Boltzmann law calculator

Enter the surface's **emissivity**, its **area**, its **temperature** and the **temperature of the surroundings**. The calculator returns the radiant flux, the total power the surface emits and the net heat it loses by radiation once the radiation it absorbs from the surroundings is subtracted. A negative net value means the surface is gaining heat.

## Stefan–Boltzmann formula

<p class="formula">P = ε σ A T⁴ &nbsp;·&nbsp; Q<sub>net</sub> = ε σ A (T⁴ − T<sub>s</sub>⁴)</p>

σ = 5.670374419 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴), with T in kelvin.

## Example

A painted steel surface (ε = 0.9) of 1 m² at 100 °C in a room at 20 °C:

- T = 373.15 K, T<sub>s</sub> = 293.15 K
- Emitted: 0.9 × 5.670 × 10⁻⁸ × 373.15⁴ = **989.4 W**
- Net loss: 0.9 × 5.670 × 10⁻⁸ × 1 × (373.15⁴ − 293.15⁴) = **612.5 W**

## Typical emissivities

| Surface | ε |
|---|---|
| Polished aluminium / silver | 0.03–0.05 |
| Galvanised steel (new) | 0.2–0.3 |
| Oxidised steel | 0.8 |
| Paint (any colour), brick, concrete | 0.9–0.95 |
| Water, human skin | 0.95–0.98 |

The wavelength at which a hot body radiates most is given by [Wien's law](/physics/wiens-law/).

## Radiation heat transfer coefficient

Engineers often fold radiation into a coefficient that can be added to the convection coefficient: h<sub>r</sub> = εσ(T² + T<sub>s</sub>²)(T + T<sub>s</sub>). With the default 373.15 K surface and 293.15 K room:

- h<sub>r</sub> = 0.9 × 5.670 × 10⁻⁸ × (373.15² + 293.15²) × (373.15 + 293.15) = **7.66 W/(m²·K)**
- Check: 612.5 W ÷ (100 − 20) K = 7.66 W/(m²·K)

Natural convection from a warm vertical surface in still air is typically 3 to 10 W/(m²·K), so at only 100 °C radiation already carries about half of the total heat loss. Over a year of continuous operation the default square metre radiates away **5,366 kWh** net.

## Effect of surface finish

Same 1 m² at 100 °C in 20 °C surroundings, with only the emissivity changed:

| Emissivity | Net radiation loss | Per year |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 34.0 W | 298 kWh |
| 0.1 | 68.1 W | 596 kWh |
| 0.3 | 204.2 W | 1,789 kWh |
| 0.6 | 408.4 W | 3,577 kWh |
| 0.9 | 612.5 W | 5,366 kWh |
| 0.95 | 646.6 W | 5,664 kWh |

This is why insulated hot pipes are clad in aluminium or stainless steel sheet. One metre of bare painted DN100 pipe (114.3 mm outside diameter, 0.359 m² of surface) at these temperatures radiates **220 W**; the same area of clean aluminium at ε = 0.05 radiates only 12.2 W.

## Using emissivity with infrared thermometers

A non-contact thermometer measures radiated power and inverts the same law, so it needs the right emissivity. If the default ε = 0.9 surface at 100 °C is read with the instrument set to 0.95, the reading is about **96.9 °C**, a little low, because the instrument assumes more of the signal is emitted and less reflected from the room. On shiny metals with ε near 0.1 the error can be tens of degrees, so technicians apply black tape or paint of known emissivity before measuring.

## Common mistakes

- **Using °C inside T⁴.** 100⁴ and 373.15⁴ differ by a factor of almost 200.
- **Forgetting the surroundings.** The emitted 989 W is not the heat loss; the surface absorbs radiation from the room, leaving a net 613 W.
- **Using the small-body formula between two large plates.** For parallel plates both emissivities matter: ε<sub>eff</sub> = 1 ÷ (1/ε<sub>1</sub> + 1/ε<sub>2</sub> − 1), which is 0.818 for two ε = 0.9 plates and gives 557 W per m² instead of 613 W.
- **Taking emissivity from the wrong condition.** Polished and oxidised versions of the same metal can differ tenfold; check the actual surface.
- **Ignoring dirt and ageing.** Aluminium jacketing that is dusty or weathered radiates far more than the 0.05 quoted for a clean sheet.
