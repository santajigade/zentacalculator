    Solutions and concentration

    Most lab calculations come back to concentration. Use the moles calculator to convert between mass and amount, the molarity calculator for mol/L, and the solution preparation calculator to find how many grams to weigh for a given volume. To make a weaker solution from a stock, use the dilution calculator (C₁V₁ = C₂V₂). Other units have their own tools: molality for colligative properties, normality for titrations, mass percent and ppm for trace levels.

    Acids, bases and equilibrium

    The pH calculator handles strong acids and bases, and the weak acid pH calculator uses Ka for acetic acid and similar compounds. For buffers, the Henderson-Hasselbalch calculator gives the pH from the acid and conjugate base ratio, and the pKa calculator converts between Ka and pKa. Titration results go in the titration calculator. For reactions at equilibrium, use the equilibrium constant and Gibbs free energy calculators to see which way a reaction runs.

    Gases, kinetics and industrial chemistry

    The ideal gas law and the combined gas law cover most gas problems, with Van der Waals for real gases at high pressure. Reaction speed is handled by the Arrhenius equation, activation energy, half-life and the integrated rate law. Plant and water chemists will find water hardness, TDS from conductivity, dissolved oxygen saturation, combustion air and GCV to NCV for fuels.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I calculate molarity?

    Molarity = moles of solute ÷ litres of solution. The molarity calculator also finds the mass to weigh out.

    Are these suitable for lab use?

    Yes — they use standard formulas. Always double-check critical preparations against your lab procedures.

    Where do the molar masses come from?

    Molar mass tools use IUPAC standard atomic weights. For hydrates and salts, enter the molar mass of the exact compound on the bottle label.

    What is the difference between molarity and molality?

    Molarity is moles per litre of solution and changes slightly with temperature. Molality is moles per kilogram of solvent and does not, which is why it is used for boiling point elevation and freezing point depression.
