## How to use the Carnot efficiency calculator

Enter the **hot** and **cold reservoir temperatures** and, optionally, the **heat input rate**. The calculator returns the Carnot efficiency, the maximum power output, and the ideal coefficients of performance for a refrigerator and a heat pump working between the same temperatures.

## Carnot efficiency formula

<p class="formula">η = 1 − T<sub>c</sub> ÷ T<sub>h</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; W = η × Q<sub>h</sub></p>

<p class="formula">COP<sub>ref</sub> = T<sub>c</sub> ÷ (T<sub>h</sub> − T<sub>c</sub>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; COP<sub>HP</sub> = T<sub>h</sub> ÷ (T<sub>h</sub> − T<sub>c</sub>)</p>

## Example

An engine takes 100 kW of heat at 500 °C (773.15 K) and rejects heat at 30 °C (303.15 K):

- η = 1 − 303.15 ÷ 773.15 = **60.79%**
- Maximum work = 0.6079 × 100 = **60.79 kW**
- Carnot COP as a refrigerator = 303.15 ÷ 470 = **0.645**; as a heat pump = 773.15 ÷ 470 = **1.645**

## Notes

- Efficiency improves by raising T<sub>h</sub> or lowering T<sub>c</sub>; that is why gas turbines run hot and power plants want cold cooling water.
- A smaller temperature lift gives a much higher heat-pump COP, which is why underfloor heating (low flow temperature) suits heat pumps.

## Where the rest of the heat goes

In the default case only 60.79 kW of the 100 kW can become work, even in an ideal engine, so at least **39.21 kW** must be rejected to the 30 °C sink. That rejected heat is why power stations need cooling towers or river water, and why the cold side matters as much as the hot side: each degree lower at the sink raises the limit.

The Carnot engine produces its maximum efficiency only at zero speed, because heat must flow across vanishing temperature differences. An engine run for maximum power with finite heat exchangers has the Curzon and Ahlborn efficiency, 1 − √(T<sub>c</sub>/T<sub>h</sub>), which for the defaults is **37.4%**. This lower figure is much closer to what real plants achieve.

## Real plants against the Carnot limit

The ratio of actual to Carnot efficiency, called the second law efficiency, shows how much room for improvement is left:

| System | Hot / cold temperature | Carnot limit | Typical actual | Fraction of Carnot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Supercritical steam plant | 600 °C / 30 °C | 65.3% | about 45% | 69% |
| Subcritical steam plant | 540 °C / 35 °C | 62.1% | about 38% | 61% |
| Geothermal binary plant | 150 °C / 25 °C | 29.5% | about 12% | 41% |
| Combined cycle gas plant | 1400 °C / 25 °C | 82.2% | about 60% | 73% |

## Heat pumps: use refrigerant temperatures

A heat pump delivering 35 °C water from 0 °C outdoor air has an ideal COP of 8.80 based on those two temperatures. The refrigerant, however, must evaporate below the air and condense above the water. With 5 K approach on each exchanger, the cycle really works between −5 °C and 40 °C, and the Carnot COP falls to **6.96**. A unit measured at COP 3.5 is therefore achieving 50% of its realistic limit, not 40%.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering Celsius into the ratio by hand.** 30 ÷ 500 gives 94% instead of the correct 60.79%; only kelvin ratios are meaningful.
- **Treating the limit as a target.** Real engines reach about half to three quarters of it, and well below at small scale.
- **Using air and water temperatures for a heat pump.** The refrigerant temperatures set the limit, as the example above shows.
- **Mixing up COP and efficiency.** A COP above 1 is normal because a heat pump moves heat rather than creating it.
- **Forgetting that COP<sub>HP</sub> = COP<sub>ref</sub> + 1.** Heating output includes the compressor work as well as the heat lifted.
