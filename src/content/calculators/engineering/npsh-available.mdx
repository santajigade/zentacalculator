  - q: How do you calculate NPSH available?
    a: NPSHa = (P_surface − P_vapour) ÷ (ρg) + h_static − h_friction, using absolute pressures. h_static is positive when the liquid level is above the pump centreline and negative for a suction lift.
  - q: What is the difference between NPSHa and NPSHr?
    a: NPSHa is a property of the suction system — the head above vapour pressure available at the pump inlet. NPSHr is a property of the pump, from the manufacturer's curve, usually defined as the NPSH at which head drops by 3% (NPSH3). NPSHa must exceed NPSHr.
  - q: How much NPSH margin is needed?
    a: Hydraulic Institute guideline ANSI/HI 9.6.1 suggests NPSHa ÷ NPSHr ratios from about 1.1 up to 2.5 depending on the pump's suction energy and service; many specifications ask for at least 1 m (3 ft) of margin or a 1.3 ratio.
  - q: Why does hot water cause cavitation more easily?
    a: Vapour pressure rises steeply with temperature — from 2.3 kPa at 20 °C to 101 kPa at 100 °C — so the vapour head term eats almost all of the atmospheric head. Boiling or near-boiling liquids need a flooded suction with enough static height.

## How to use the NPSH available calculator

Enter the **absolute pressure on the liquid surface** (atmospheric pressure for an open tank), the liquid's **vapour pressure** at the pumping temperature, its **density**, the **liquid level relative to the pump centreline** (negative for a suction lift), the **suction-line losses** at the design flow and the pump's **NPSHr** from its curve. The calculator gives each head term, the NPSH available, the margin in metres and the NPSHa ÷ NPSHr ratio.

## NPSH available formula

<p class="formula">NPSH<sub>a</sub> = (P<sub>surface</sub> − P<sub>v</sub>) ÷ (ρ g) + h<sub>z</sub> − h<sub>f</sub></p>

All pressures are absolute. Calculate NPSHa at the **maximum flow** (highest friction and NPSHr), the **lowest liquid level** and the **highest liquid temperature** you expect.

## Worked example

Water at 40 °C (P<sub>v</sub> = 7.384 kPa, ρ = 992.22 kg/m³) lifted from an open sump at sea level, liquid level 3 m below the pump, 1.2 m suction loss, pump NPSHr = 4 m:

- Pressure head = 101,325 ÷ (992.22 × 9.807) = **10.41 m**
- Vapour pressure head = 7,384 ÷ (992.22 × 9.807) = **0.759 m**
- NPSH<sub>a</sub> = 10.41 − 0.759 − 3 − 1.2 = **5.45 m**
- Margin = 5.45 − 4 = **1.45 m**, ratio **1.36** — comfortable

## Practical notes

- A suction lift of more than about 4–5 m with cold water usually leaves too little NPSHa — move the pump lower or use a flooded suction.
- **Reduce suction losses**: use a suction pipe one size larger than the pump inlet, keep it short, and avoid strainers or valves that can clog close to the pump.
- For liquids at their boiling point (condensate, deaerator outlet, refrigerants) P<sub>surface</sub> = P<sub>v</sub>, so NPSHa is just the static height minus losses.
- If you have a suction gauge reading, NPSHa = (P<sub>gauge</sub> + P<sub>atm</sub> − P<sub>v</sub>) ÷ ρg + v²/2g + gauge height above the pump centreline.

## Second example: boiler feed pump under a deaerator

A deaerator holds water at its boiling point, so the surface pressure equals the vapour pressure and the first two terms cancel. With the water level 6 m above the pump centreline and 0.8 m of suction loss, NPSHa = 6 − 0.8 = **5.2 m**, whatever the deaerator pressure. Against an NPSHr of 3.5 m the ratio is 1.49. This is why deaerators sit high on a structure: every metre of elevation is a metre of NPSH, and nothing else in the system adds any.

## How the default case reacts to changes

Starting from the default NPSHa of 5.45 m:

- Sump level 1 m lower: NPSHa falls to 4.45 m and the ratio to 1.11
- Flow 20% higher: friction rises roughly with the square of flow to 1.73 m, so NPSHa drops to 4.93 m while NPSHr also rises on the pump curve
- Water at 60 °C instead of 40 °C: the vapour head grows from 0.76 m to about 2.07 m and almost all of the margin disappears

## Checking NPSHa on a running pump

Fit a calibrated compound gauge on the suction nozzle. Suppose it reads -35 kPa gauge, it sits 0.5 m above the centreline, and the pump moves 50 m³/h of 40 °C water through a 102.26 mm suction bore (velocity 1.69 m/s, velocity head 0.146 m). Then NPSHa = (-35 + 101.325 − 7.384) kPa ÷ (ρg) + 0.146 + 0.5 = **6.70 m**. A test like this catches blocked strainers and collapsed suction hoses that a desk calculation misses. Listen as well: cavitation sounds like gravel passing through the pump and often comes with a drop in discharge pressure.

## Common mistakes

- **Using gauge pressure for the surface.** An open tank has 101.325 kPa absolute at sea level, not zero; a closed vessel needs its gauge pressure plus atmospheric.
- **Taking vapour pressure at the normal temperature.** Use the highest temperature the liquid can reach, such as summer cooling water or a hot condensate return.
- **Forgetting the strainer.** A clean suction strainer may lose 0.2 m, a half blocked one several metres.
- **Comparing NPSHa with NPSH3 and calling it safe.** NPSHr is the point where head has already dropped 3%, so cavitation and erosion begin before it.
- **Checking only the duty point.** Run out on a parallel pump trip or an open bypass moves the pump to the right of its curve, where NPSHr climbs.
