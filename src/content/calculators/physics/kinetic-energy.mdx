## How to use the kinetic energy calculator

Enter **mass** and **speed** to get the kinetic energy, or enter the energy to find the speed. Units convert automatically (km/h, mph, m/s; J, kJ, kWh).

## Kinetic energy formula

<p class="formula">KE = ½ m v²</p>

## Worked example

A 1,200 kg car at 100 km/h, the calculator defaults:

1. Convert the speed: 100 ÷ 3.6 = 27.778 m/s
2. Square it: 27.778² = 771.6 m²/s²
3. KE = 0.5 × 1,200 × 771.6 = 462,963 J = **463.0 kJ**

That is 0.129 kWh, about the same energy as dropping the car from a height of 39.3 m (v² ÷ 2g). To stop it in 40 m the brakes must apply an average force of 463.0 kJ ÷ 40 m = 11.57 kN.

At 50 km/h the same car has only 116 kJ — one quarter.

## Kinetic energy and braking distance

The brakes must remove all the kinetic energy as heat. Since energy rises with speed squared, braking distance does too: at 100 km/h a car needs about **four times** the braking distance it needs at 50 km/h.

## Work–energy theorem

The work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy:

<p class="formula">W = ½mv₂² − ½mv₁²</p>

This lets you find the average force needed to stop something over a given distance: F = KE ÷ d.

## Rotational kinetic energy

Spinning objects such as flywheels and wheels also store energy: KE = ½ × I × ω², where I is the moment of inertia and ω the angular speed in rad/s.

## Speed and safety

| Speed | Kinetic energy (1,200 kg car) |
|---|---|
| 30 km/h | 42 kJ |
| 50 km/h | 116 kJ |
| 80 km/h | 296 kJ |
| 120 km/h | 667 kJ |

This is why small speed increases make crashes much more severe.

## Equivalent drop height by speed (1,200 kg car)

| Speed | Speed in m/s | Kinetic energy | Same as falling from |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 km/h | 8.33 m/s | 42 kJ | 3.5 m |
| 50 km/h | 13.89 m/s | 116 kJ | 9.8 m |
| 80 km/h | 22.22 m/s | 296 kJ | 25.2 m |
| 100 km/h | 27.78 m/s | 463 kJ | 39.3 m |
| 120 km/h | 33.33 m/s | 667 kJ | 56.7 m |
| 150 km/h | 41.67 m/s | 1,042 kJ | 88.5 m |

The drop height does not depend on mass, which makes it a useful way to picture a crash: hitting a wall at 50 km/h is like driving off a 9.8 m high roof.

## Where the energy goes in a stop

In a normal stop almost all of the 463 kJ ends up as heat in the brakes. If four steel discs of 6 kg each absorb it (specific heat about 460 J/kg·K), they warm by roughly 42 °C in one stop from 100 km/h, which is why repeated hard braking on a long descent causes brake fade. Regenerative braking in electric cars returns part of that energy to the battery instead.

## Kinetic energy and momentum

The same car has a momentum of 33,333 kg·m/s. Momentum grows with speed, energy with speed squared, so KE = p² ÷ 2m. Collisions conserve momentum, but kinetic energy is usually lost to deformation and heat; see the [momentum calculator](/physics/momentum/).

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving speed in km/h.** Using 100 instead of 27.78 m/s gives 6,000 kJ, about 13 times too much.
- **Forgetting to square the speed.** ½ × 1,200 × 27.78 = 16,667, which is half the momentum, not the energy.
- **Dropping the ½.** m v² alone is twice the kinetic energy.
- **Mixing weight and mass.** Use the mass in kg; a weight in newtons must first be divided by 9.81.
- **Adding energies of objects moving in different directions as vectors.** Kinetic energy is a scalar; just add the values.
