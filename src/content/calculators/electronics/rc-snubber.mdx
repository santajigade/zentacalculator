    a: Measure the ringing frequency f₀, add a known capacitor C_add across the switch and measure the new frequency f₁. The parasitic capacitance is C_p = C_add ÷ ((f₀ ÷ f₁)² − 1), the inductance L_p = 1 ÷ ((2πf₀)² C_p) and the snubber resistor R = √(L_p ÷ C_p).   - q: How much power does a snubber resistor dissipate?
    a: P = C_s × V² × f_sw — the capacitor's stored energy ½C V² is dissipated in the resistor once when it charges and once when it discharges, every switching cycle. A 293 pF snubber switching 48 V at 200 kHz dissipates about 0.13 W, so a 0.25 W or larger pulse-rated resistor is used.
  - q: Why add a capacitor until the frequency halves?
    a: When f₁ = f₀ ÷ 2 the added capacitor is exactly three times the parasitic capacitance, so the measurement is well above the scope probe and fixture capacitance and the result is not sensitive to small reading errors. Any ratio works with the formula, but ratios close to 1 give poor accuracy.

## How to use the RC snubber calculator

Measure the **ringing frequency** at the switch node with the circuit running and a short ground lead on the scope probe. Solder a **test capacitor** across the device (start with a few hundred pF) and measure the **new ringing frequency** — aim for about half the original. Enter both frequencies and the test capacitor, the **capacitor multiplier**, the **voltage switched** and the **switching frequency**. The calculator gives the parasitic capacitance and inductance, the characteristic impedance, the snubber resistor and capacitor and the resistor's power dissipation.

## RC snubber formulas

<p class="formula">C<sub>p</sub> = C<sub>add</sub> ÷ [(f<sub>0</sub> ÷ f<sub>1</sub>)² − 1] &nbsp;·&nbsp; L<sub>p</sub> = 1 ÷ [(2π f<sub>0</sub>)² C<sub>p</sub>]</p>

<p class="formula">R<sub>s</sub> = Z<sub>0</sub> = √(L<sub>p</sub> ÷ C<sub>p</sub>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; C<sub>s</sub> = k × C<sub>p</sub> (k ≈ 3–4) &nbsp;·&nbsp; P<sub>R</sub> = C<sub>s</sub> V² f<sub>sw</sub></p>

The ringing is the resonance of the loop inductance with the parasitic capacitance, f = 1 ÷ (2π√(LC)). A resistor equal to the characteristic impedance gives close to critical damping; the series capacitor blocks DC so the resistor only conducts during the edges.

## Worked example

A 48 V synchronous buck switching at 200 kHz rings at 80 MHz on the switch node. Adding 220 pF across the low-side MOSFET drops the ringing to 40 MHz:

- Frequency ratio squared (f<sub>0</sub> ÷ f<sub>1</sub>)² = (80 ÷ 40)² = 4
- Parasitic capacitance C<sub>p</sub> = 220 ÷ (4 − 1) = **73.33 pF**
- Parasitic inductance L<sub>p</sub> = 1 ÷ ((2π × 80 MHz)² × 73.33 pF) = **53.97 nH**
- Snubber resistor R<sub>s</sub> = √(53.97 nH ÷ 73.33 pF) = **27.13 Ω** — use 27 Ω
- Snubber capacitor C<sub>s</sub> = 4 × 73.33 = **293.3 pF** — use 330 pF (or 270 pF)
- Resistor power P = 293.3 pF × 48² × 200 kHz = **0.135 W**; time constant R<sub>s</sub>C<sub>s</sub> = **7.96 ns**

## Practical notes

- **Fix the layout first:** a shorter hot loop and a closer input capacitor reduce L<sub>p</sub>, raise the ringing frequency and reduce the energy the snubber has to absorb.
- **Check the result:** fit the calculated R and C, then confirm on the scope that the overshoot is damped within one or two cycles. Adjust R ±50% to fine-tune; if the loss is too high, reduce k towards 2.
- **Rating:** the resistor sees pulses of up to V ÷ R at every edge — choose a pulse-rated part and allow at least twice the calculated average power.
- **Snubber loss counts as switching loss** — include it in the converter efficiency with the [MOSFET power loss calculator](/electronics/mosfet-power-loss/). To check the ringing frequency from L and C, use the [resonant frequency calculator](/electronics/resonant-frequency/).

## Choosing the capacitor multiplier k

The multiplier trades damping against loss. With the default measurement (C<sub>p</sub> = 73.33 pF, R<sub>s</sub> = 27.13 Ω, 48 V at 200 kHz):

| k | C<sub>s</sub> | Resistor loss | R<sub>s</sub>C<sub>s</sub> |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 147 pF | 68 mW | 3.98 ns |
| 3 | 220 pF | 101 mW | 5.97 ns |
| 4 | 293 pF | 135 mW | 7.96 ns |
| 6 | 440 pF | 203 mW | 11.94 ns |

Below about k = 2 the capacitor starts to limit the damping and the ringing comes back as a smaller, slower oscillation. Above 4 the extra loss buys very little. A useful rule is that R<sub>s</sub>C<sub>s</sub> should be several times shorter than the shortest on or off time, so the capacitor fully charges and discharges every cycle.

## Measuring the ringing properly

- Use the probe tip and ground spring, not the 15 cm ground clip: the clip adds its own loop of roughly 100 nH, which rings with the probe capacitance near the frequency you are trying to measure.
- Read the frequency from several cycles of the first ringing burst after the edge, for example the time across five peaks divided by five.
- Take both readings at the same load current and input voltage, because MOSFET output capacitance falls as the drain voltage rises.
- Solder the test capacitor directly across the drain and source pins; leads of a few centimetres add inductance and spoil the ratio.

## Why the 2:1 frequency drop matters

With a 2:1 drop, reading f<sub>1</sub> as 41 MHz instead of 40 MHz changes C<sub>p</sub> to 78.4 pF, an error of 6.9%. If the test capacitor were so small that the frequency only fell from 80 MHz to 76 MHz, the same 1 MHz reading error (77 MHz) would move C<sub>p</sub> from 2036 pF to 2769 pF. A big frequency shift makes the method robust.

## Peak current and energy

When the switch turns on, the charged snubber capacitor discharges through R<sub>s</sub>, so with a 27 Ω part the peak current is about 48 ÷ 27 = **1.78 A** for a few nanoseconds. That pulse flows through the MOSFET too and adds slightly to its turn on loss. For comparison, a 10 A current through the 53.97 nH loop stores ½ L I² = **2699 nJ** at turn off, which is the energy the snubber has to absorb to stop the overshoot.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring with a long ground lead**, which reads the probe resonance instead of the circuit resonance.
- **Using a wirewound resistor.** Its inductance can be larger than the loop inductance being damped.
- **Placing the snubber far from the device**, so the snubber loop has its own inductance and rings at a new frequency.
- **Sizing the resistor for average power only.** The 0.135 W average comes as 1.8 A pulses; pick a pulse rated part and at least twice the average rating.
- **Forgetting the capacitor voltage rating.** It sees the full switched voltage plus the overshoot, so a 100 V part is the minimum for a 48 V rail.
