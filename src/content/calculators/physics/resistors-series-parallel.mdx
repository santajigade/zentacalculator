## How to use the resistors in series & parallel calculator

Enter up to **three resistor values** (Ω, kΩ or MΩ). You get the total for both a series and a parallel connection. For just two resistors, set R3 to a very large value such as 1e12 Ω.

## Resistors in series & parallel formulas

<p class="formula">Series: R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃<br/>Parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃</p>

## Worked example

With the default 100 Ω, 220 Ω and 470 Ω:

- Series: 100 + 220 + 470 = **790 Ω**
- Parallel: the conductances are 1/100 = 10.000 mS, 1/220 = 4.545 mS and 1/470 = 2.128 mS. They add to 16.673 mS, so R = 1 ÷ 0.016673 = **59.98 Ω**

The parallel total is below the smallest resistor (100 Ω), as it must be. Adding the 470 Ω part lowers the parallel result from 68.75 Ω (100 Ω and 220 Ω alone) to 59.98 Ω but adds 470 Ω to the series total: in parallel the smallest resistor dominates, while in series the largest one does.

### Current and power at 12 V

Put the same three resistors across a 12 V supply:

| Connection | Resistor | Current | Power |
|---|---|---|---|
| Series | 100 Ω | 15.19 mA | 23.1 mW |
| Series | 220 Ω | 15.19 mA | 50.8 mW |
| Series | 470 Ω | 15.19 mA | 108.4 mW |
| Parallel | 100 Ω | 120.0 mA | 1440 mW |
| Parallel | 220 Ω | 54.5 mA | 655 mW |
| Parallel | 470 Ω | 25.5 mA | 306 mW |

In series the largest resistor gets the most heat; in parallel the smallest one does. The 100 Ω resistor in parallel dissipates 1.44 W, so a common 0.25 W part would overheat and a 2 W part is the sensible choice.

## Quick rules

- Two equal resistors in parallel → half the value (2 × 1 kΩ = 500 Ω).
- n equal resistors in parallel → R ÷ n.
- Parallel total is always smaller than the smallest single resistor.

## Two resistors in parallel: shortcut

For two resistors, the parallel value is the **product over the sum**:

<p class="formula">R = R₁ × R₂ ÷ (R₁ + R₂)</p>

100 Ω and 220 Ω in parallel = 22,000 ÷ 320 = **68.75 Ω**.

## Making a value you do not have

| Target | Combination | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 50 Ω | Two 100 Ω in parallel | 50 Ω |
| 150 Ω | 100 Ω + 47 Ω in series | 147 Ω |
| 330 Ω | 220 Ω + 100 Ω + 10 Ω in series | 330 Ω |
| 4.7 kΩ | 10 kΩ ∥ 8.2 kΩ (close) | 4.51 kΩ |

## Voltage divider

Two resistors in series divide a voltage:

<p class="formula">V<sub>out</sub> = V<sub>in</sub> × R₂ ÷ (R₁ + R₂)</p>

With 12 V, R₁ = 10 kΩ and R₂ = 4.7 kΩ, V<sub>out</sub> = **3.84 V**. Dividers are used to scale sensor and battery voltages to a microcontroller's input range. See the [voltage divider calculator](/electronics/voltage-divider/).

## Current in parallel branches

In parallel, current splits in inverse proportion to resistance. With 100 Ω and 220 Ω across 12 V, the branches carry 120 mA and 54.5 mA, totalling 174.5 mA.

## Colour codes

Four-band resistors: two digit bands, a multiplier and a tolerance. Brown-black-red-gold is 1, 0, ×100 = 1,000 Ω (1 kΩ) ±5%.

## Using only two resistors

Leave R3 at a huge value such as 1e12 Ω. The parallel result becomes 68.75 Ω, exactly the 100 Ω and 220 Ω product over sum value, but the series total then includes the dummy 1e12 Ω, so read only the parallel answer in that case (or set R3 to 0 Ω for a two resistor series total).

## Equal resistors in parallel

Paralleling identical resistors is a cheap way to get a low value with a higher power rating. With 1 kΩ, 0.25 W parts:

| Number in parallel | Total resistance | Same parts in series | Power rating |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 500.0 Ω | 2 kΩ | 0.50 W |
| 3 | 333.3 Ω | 3 kΩ | 0.75 W |
| 4 | 250.0 Ω | 4 kΩ | 1.00 W |
| 5 | 200.0 Ω | 5 kΩ | 1.25 W |
| 10 | 100.0 Ω | 10 kΩ | 2.50 W |

## Tolerance of the result

If all three parts are 5% types, the series total can sit anywhere from 750.5 Ω to 829.5 Ω and the parallel total from 56.98 Ω to 62.98 Ω. Combining resistors never makes the percentage tolerance worse than that of the parts, and random errors partly cancel, which is why a series string of matched parts is used in precision dividers.

## Measuring a combination with a multimeter

Switch off the power and lift one leg of a resistor before measuring it in a circuit, otherwise parallel paths through other components make the reading too low. Touching both probe tips with your fingers adds your body resistance in parallel and pulls high value readings down, which matters above about 100 kΩ.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding parallel values directly.** 100 Ω and 220 Ω in parallel is 68.75 Ω, not 320 Ω; add the reciprocals, then take the reciprocal of the sum.
- **Forgetting the last reciprocal.** The sum 1/100 + 1/220 + 1/470 = 0.01667 is a conductance in siemens, not the answer in ohms.
- **Mixing Ω and kΩ.** Writing 4.7 instead of 4,700 for a 4.7 kΩ part gives a parallel result that is a thousand times too small.
- **Sizing power from the total.** The total dissipation does not tell you the share each part takes; check the hottest resistor, as in the table above.
- **Ignoring a short.** A 0 Ω link or a solder bridge across any parallel branch makes the whole combination 0 Ω.
