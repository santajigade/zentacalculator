  - q: What is Snell's law?
    a: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where n is the refractive index of each medium and θ is measured from the normal (the line perpendicular to the surface). Light bends towards the normal when it enters a medium with a higher index and away from it when it enters a lower one.
  - q: What is the critical angle?
    a: The angle of incidence inside the denser medium at which the refracted ray just grazes the surface (θ₂ = 90°). sin θc = n₂ ÷ n₁. Beyond it no light escapes and the ray is totally internally reflected — about 48.6° for water to air and 41.3° for crown glass to air.

## How to use the Snell's law calculator

Enter the **refractive index** of the material the light comes from, the **angle of incidence** measured from the normal, and the **refractive index** of the material it enters. The calculator gives the **angle of refraction**, the **critical angle** of that interface, the **deviation** of the ray and the **speed of light** in the second medium. Fill in any three of n₁, θ₁, n₂ and θ₂ to solve for the fourth — for example, measure both angles to find an unknown refractive index.

## Snell's law formula

<p class="formula">n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ &nbsp;·&nbsp; sin θ<sub>c</sub> = n<sub>low</sub> ÷ n<sub>high</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; v = c ÷ n</p>

with c = 299,792,458 m/s. The refractive index is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in the material.

## Worked example

Light passes from air (n₁ = 1.0003) into water (n₂ = 1.333) at 30° to the normal:

- sin θ₂ = 1.0003 × sin 30° ÷ 1.333 = **0.3752**, so θ₂ = **22.04°**
- The ray bends 7.96° towards the normal
- Critical angle for light leaving the water: sin θ<sub>c</sub> = 1.0003 ÷ 1.333, θ<sub>c</sub> = **48.63°**
- Speed of light in water = 299,792,458 ÷ 1.333 = **224,900,569 m/s**

## Practical notes

- **Optical fibres** guide light by total internal reflection between a core and a slightly lower-index cladding; the numerical aperture is NA = √(n<sub>core</sub>² − n<sub>clad</sub>²).
- **Refractometers** measure the critical angle of a sample against a prism to find its refractive index — the basis of sugar (°Brix) and concentration measurement.
- **Dispersion:** n changes with wavelength, so quote the index at a wavelength (usually 589 nm) when precision matters.
- Part of the light is always reflected at the interface; at normal incidence the reflected fraction is ((n₁ − n₂) ÷ (n₁ + n₂))², about 4% for air to glass.

## Measuring an unknown refractive index

In the classic ray box experiment a narrow beam strikes a rectangular block and the incident and refracted rays are traced on paper. With an incidence of 40° and a refracted ray measured at 25.0°, the block has n = 1.0003 × sin 40° ÷ sin 25.0° = **1.521**, typical of a crown glass. A protractor reading only good to ±0.5° moves this between 1.494 and 1.550, so repeat the trace at several angles and fit sin θ₁ against sin θ₂: the slope of that straight line is n₂ ÷ n₁. Laboratory refractometers avoid the protractor altogether by locating the sharp edge of the critical angle against a prism, which gives four decimal places.

## Sideways shift through a glass plate

A ray passing through a flat plate leaves parallel to its original direction but displaced sideways by t sin(θ₁ − θ₂) ÷ cos θ₂. For a 10 mm crown glass plate (n = 1.517) in air:

| Angle of incidence | Angle inside the glass | Lateral shift |
|---|---|---|
| 10° | 6.57° | 0.60 mm |
| 20° | 13.03° | 1.24 mm |
| 30° | 19.25° | 1.98 mm |
| 45° | 27.79° | 3.34 mm |
| 60° | 34.82° | 5.18 mm |
| 75° | 39.56° | 7.52 mm |

This shift is why a thick window or a filter placed in front of a camera or a laser rangefinder moves the image slightly, and why optical designers include plane plates in their ray traces.

## Apparent depth and Snell's window

Seen from straight above, a 2.0 m deep pool looks only 2.0 × 1.0003 ÷ 1.333 = **1.50 m** deep, because the rays bend away from the normal as they leave the water. A diver looking up sees the entire sky squeezed into a cone 97.3° wide (twice the 48.63° critical angle); outside it the surface acts as a mirror.

## Fibre optic acceptance angle

A step index fibre with a core of n = 1.468 and cladding of n = 1.462 has a critical angle of 84.82° at the core boundary and a numerical aperture of √(1.468² − 1.462²) = **0.1326**. Only light arriving within 7.62° of the fibre axis is guided, which is why connector alignment matters so much.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring the angle from the surface.** Snell's law uses the angle from the normal. A ray striking water 30° above the surface is at 60° to the normal and refracts to 40.53°, not 22.04°.
- **Looking for a critical angle going into the denser medium.** Total internal reflection only happens when light travels from the higher index to the lower one; from air into water every angle refracts.
- **Mixing up which index is which.** n₁ always belongs to the medium the light starts in; swapping them gives the reverse path, not the same one.
- **Ignoring the wavelength.** Indices are quoted at 589 nm; crown glass is about 1.513 in deep red and 1.530 in violet, enough to shift a refraction angle by a few tenths of a degree.
- **Over trusting the air value.** Taking air as exactly 1 changes the default result from 22.037° to 22.030°. That is negligible for homework but not for precision refractometry, where the temperature of the sample matters more still.
