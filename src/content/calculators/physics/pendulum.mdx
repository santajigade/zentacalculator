## How to use the pendulum period calculator

Enter the **length** (pivot to the centre of the bob) to get the period and frequency. Or enter a period to find the length needed.

## Pendulum period formula

<p class="formula">T = 2π √(L ÷ g)</p>

## Worked example

A 1 m pendulum:

T = 2π √(1 ÷ 9.81) = **2.006 s**, frequency **0.498 Hz**

## Period for different lengths

| Length | Period | Swings per minute |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 m | 0.634 s | 94.6 |
| 0.25 m | 1.003 s | 59.8 |
| 0.5 m | 1.419 s | 42.3 |
| 0.994 m | 2.000 s | 30.0 |
| 1 m | 2.006 s | 29.9 |
| 2 m | 2.837 s | 21.1 |
| 5 m | 4.486 s | 13.4 |
| 10 m | 6.345 s | 9.5 |
| 67 m | 16.423 s | 3.7 |

A length of 0.994 m gives a period of 2 seconds — the classic **seconds pendulum**, where each one-way swing takes 1 second. The 67 m row is about the length of Foucault's famous pendulum in the Panthéon, Paris.

## Small-angle assumption

The formula is accurate for swings up to about 15°. At larger angles the period is longer:

| Amplitude | Period increase |
|---|---|
| 10° | +0.2% |
| 20° | +0.8% |
| 45° | +4.0% |
| 90° | +18.0% |

## Measuring g with a pendulum

Rearrange the formula: g = 4π² × L ÷ T². Time 20 full swings to reduce timing error, then divide by 20. With L = 1.000 m and T = 2.006 s, g = 9.81 m/s².

## Pendulum clocks

A pendulum clock that runs slow has a pendulum that is too long. Raising the bob by just 1 mm on a seconds pendulum speeds it up by about 43 seconds a day.

## The same 1 m pendulum on other worlds

The period scales with 1 ÷ √g, so the same pendulum is a simple gravity meter. The last column is the length that would beat seconds (a 2 s period) in that gravity:

| Location | g | Period of a 1 m pendulum | Seconds pendulum length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equator (sea level) | 9.780 m/s² | 2.009 s | 0.991 m |
| Standard gravity | 9.807 m/s² | 2.006 s | 0.994 m |
| Poles (sea level) | 9.832 m/s² | 2.004 s | 0.996 m |
| Moon | 1.620 m/s² | 4.937 s | 0.164 m |
| Mars | 3.710 m/s² | 3.262 s | 0.376 m |
| Jupiter (cloud tops) | 24.790 m/s² | 1.262 s | 2.512 m |

Even on Earth the difference matters to a clock: a seconds pendulum regulated for standard gravity loses about 118 seconds a day if moved to the equator.

## Lab worked example

Using the calculator default of L = 1 m and g = 9.80665 m/s²: T = 2π × √(1 ÷ 9.80665) = **2.0064 s** and f = **0.4984 Hz**. Twenty full swings should therefore take 40.13 s. If your stopwatch reads 0.2 s long over those 20 swings, the measured period becomes 2.0164 s and g = 4π² × 1 ÷ 2.0164² = 9.710 m/s², an error of 1.0%. Timing more swings divides that error further.

## Measuring a real pendulum

Measure the length from the pivot point to the centre of mass of the bob, not to its bottom. Use a light, inextensible string, keep the amplitude below about 10°, and start timing as the bob passes the lowest point, where it moves fastest and the instant is easiest to judge. A light gate or a phone camera at 240 frames per second beats hand timing.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring to the bottom of the bob.** With a 5 cm bob that adds 2.5 cm to L and the predicted period becomes 2.0313 s instead of 2.0064 s.
- **Timing one way swings.** The period is a full there and back cycle; a one way swing is half of it.
- **Counting the first pass as one.** Say zero when you start the stopwatch, otherwise you time one swing fewer than you count.
- **Using the formula for a rigid rod.** A uniform 1 m rod pivoted at one end has T = 2π√(2L ÷ 3g) = 1.638 s, not 2.006 s, because its mass is spread along its length.
- **Large release angles.** At 45° the period is already 4% longer than the formula gives.
