  - q: What is a good SNR?

    a: The effective number of bits — the resolution of an ideal ADC with the same SNR. ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76) ÷ 6.02.

    a: Increase the signal level early in the chain, shield and twist cables, filter out-of-band noise, use differential inputs and average multiple readings.

## How to use the signal-to-noise ratio calculator

Enter the **RMS signal** and **RMS noise** voltages (any matching unit). You get the SNR in dB, the power ratio and the equivalent ADC resolution (ENOB).

## Signal-to-noise ratio formulas

<p class="formula">SNR = 20 log₁₀(V<sub>s</sub> ÷ V<sub>n</sub>) = 10 log₁₀(P<sub>s</sub> ÷ P<sub>n</sub>)</p>

<p class="formula">ENOB = (SNR − 1.76) ÷ 6.02</p>

## SNR reference

| SNR | Voltage ratio | ENOB |
|---|---|---|
| 20 dB | 10 : 1 | 3.0 |
| 40 dB | 100 : 1 | 6.4 |
| 50 dB | 316 : 1 | 8.0 |
| 60 dB | 1,000 : 1 | 9.7 |
| 74 dB | 5,012 : 1 | 12.0 |
| 86 dB | 19,953 : 1 | 14.0 |
| 98 dB | 79,433 : 1 | 16.0 |
| 110 dB | 316,228 : 1 | 18.0 |

An ideal 12-bit ADC reaches 74 dB and an ideal 16-bit ADC 98 dB.

## Sources of noise

| Noise | Cause | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal (Johnson) | Random electron motion in resistors | Lower resistance, narrower bandwidth |
| Mains hum (50/60 Hz) | Magnetic and capacitive pickup | Twisted pairs, shielding, notch or integrating ADC |
| Switching noise | SMPS, VFDs | Layout, ferrites, filtering |
| Quantisation | ADC step size | More bits, dithering |

## Thermal noise floor

A resistor's noise voltage is √(4kTRB). A 10 kΩ resistor at 25 °C over 10 kHz bandwidth produces about 1.3 µV RMS.

## Worked example

With the default values, a 1000 mV RMS signal carries 1 mV RMS of noise:

- Voltage ratio = 1000 ÷ 1 = 1,000
- SNR = 20 × log₁₀(1,000) = **60.00 dB**
- Power ratio = 1,000² = **1,000,000**, and 10 × log₁₀(1,000,000) gives the same 60 dB
- ENOB = (60.00 − 1.76) ÷ 6.02 = **9.67 bits**, so a converter with more than about 10 bits adds nothing until the noise is reduced

A thermocouple input with a 10 mV signal and 20 µV RMS of noise has an SNR of 54.0 dB, about 8.7 effective bits.

## Improving SNR by averaging

Averaging N readings of a steady signal reduces uncorrelated noise by √N, which adds 10 log₁₀ N dB. Starting from the 60 dB example:

| Samples averaged | Noise reduced by | SNR gain | Resulting SNR | ENOB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 × | +0.0 dB | 60.0 dB | 9.7 |
| 4 | 2 × | +6.0 dB | 66.0 dB | 10.7 |
| 16 | 4 × | +12.0 dB | 72.0 dB | 11.7 |
| 64 | 8 × | +18.1 dB | 78.1 dB | 12.7 |
| 256 | 16 × | +24.1 dB | 84.1 dB | 13.7 |
| 1,024 | 32 × | +30.1 dB | 90.1 dB | 14.7 |

Averaging only helps against random noise. Mains hum and other correlated noise need filtering or an integration time that is a whole number of mains cycles.

## Adding noise from several sources

Uncorrelated noise sources add as the root of the sum of squares, not arithmetically. Suppose the default signal (1,000 mV RMS) has 1 mV of sensor noise, a preamplifier adds 0.5 mV, and a 12 bit ADC on a 2.5 V range adds quantisation noise of one LSB ÷ √12 = 0.6104 ÷ 3.464 = 0.176 mV:

- Total noise = √(1² + 0.5² + 0.176²) = **1.132 mV**
- SNR = 20 log₁₀(1,000 ÷ 1.132) = **58.92 dB**, 1.08 dB below the sensor alone
- Halving the amplifier noise to 0.25 mV only recovers the SNR to 59.61 dB

The largest source dominates, so effort is best spent on the sensor here, not on a quieter amplifier or a bigger ADC.

## SNR from an amplifier noise density

Datasheets give voltage noise density in nV/√Hz. Multiply by the square root of the noise bandwidth, which is 1.57 times the −3 dB frequency for a single pole filter, then by the gain. For an op amp with 10 nV/√Hz, a gain of 100 and a 10 mV input signal (1 V out):

| −3 dB bandwidth | Noise bandwidth | Output noise | SNR | ENOB |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 Hz | 157 Hz | 12.5 µV | 98.0 dB | 16.0 |
| 1,000 Hz | 1,570 Hz | 39.6 µV | 88.0 dB | 14.3 |
| 10,000 Hz | 15,700 Hz | 125.3 µV | 78.0 dB | 12.7 |
| 100,000 Hz | 157,000 Hz | 396.2 µV | 68.0 dB | 11.0 |

Every tenfold cut in bandwidth gains 10 dB. Resistor and current noise add on top of this, so treat the figures as the best case.

## How noise is measured in practice

- **RMS, not peak:** SNR uses RMS values. For Gaussian noise the peak-to-peak value seen on an oscilloscope is roughly 6 to 7 times the RMS value, so divide a scope reading before using it here.
- **State the bandwidth:** noise grows with bandwidth (thermal noise with its square root), so an SNR figure means little without the bandwidth it was measured in. Set the scope or analyser bandwidth limit to match the real system.
- **Measure noise with the signal removed:** short the input or hold the process steady and log the reading spread; its standard deviation is the RMS noise.
- **Layout and cabling** matter as much as the electronics; good practice is covered in [what signal integrity means](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/what-is-signal-integrity/).

## Common mistakes

- **Using 10 log for voltage ratios.** Voltages and currents need 20 log₁₀; only power ratios use 10 log₁₀.
- **Mixing RMS signal with peak-to-peak noise,** which makes the SNR look 15 to 17 dB worse than it is.
- **Quoting ENOB from SNR alone.** Datasheet ENOB uses SINAD, which includes distortion, so the real ENOB is usually a little lower than this calculator gives.
- **Comparing SNR figures measured over different bandwidths** or with different input ranges.
- **Confusing SNR with dynamic range,** which compares the largest possible signal, not the actual one, with the noise floor.
