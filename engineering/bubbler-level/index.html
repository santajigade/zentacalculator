    A bubbler pushes a small flow of air down a dip tube, and the pressure needed to release bubbles equals the liquid head above the tube end. This bubbler level calculator turns that back pressure into level, as described in bubbler level measurement with a purge.

    Instrument engineers use it to range the transmitter and check readings. Bubblers suit sludge, slurries and corrosive liquids, because only the dip tube touches the process.

    How to use the bubbler level calculator

    Enter the back pressure measured on the purge line, the specific gravity of the liquid, the height of the dip tube end above the tank bottom and the maximum level you need to measure. The calculator gives the head above the tube end, the level from the tank bottom, the level as a percentage of maximum and the back pressure at maximum level, which is the URV for the transmitter. To find the back pressure for a known level, enter the level and press × on the back pressure.

    Bubbler level formula

    h = Pb ÷ (SG × ρw × g)  ·  L = h + z  ·  Pmax = SG × ρw × g × (Lmax − z)

    Pb is the gauge back pressure in Pa, ρw = 1,000 kg/m³, g = 9.80665 m/s² and z the height of the tube end above the bottom. The transmitter is ranged from 0 (level at the tube end) to Pmax.

    Worked example

    A tank of liquor with SG 1.05 has a dip tube ending 100 mm above the bottom and a maximum level of 3 m. The transmitter reads 250 mbar:

    • Head above the tube end h = 25,000 Pa ÷ (1.05 × 1,000 × 9.80665) = 2.428 m
    • Level from the bottom L = 2.428 + 0.100 = 2.528 m, or 84.3% of the 3 m maximum
    • Back pressure at maximum level = 1.05 × 1,000 × 9.80665 × (3 − 0.1) = 298.6 mbar, so range the transmitter 0 to 299 mbar and set the gas regulator comfortably above that

    How a bubbler is installed

    A typical panel has a filter regulator, a differential pressure flow regulator or needle valve with a small rotameter, a check valve and the pressure transmitter, teed into the purge line close to the tank. The dip tube is usually 15 to 25 mm pipe in a material that suits the liquid, often with a notch or a 45° cut at the end so bubbles leave evenly instead of pulsing. Instrument air is common; nitrogen is used where air would oxidise or contaminate the product. Because the transmitter never touches the liquid, it can be mounted at a convenient height, and the tube can be rodded out or blown down if it fouls.

    Transmitter output across the range

    With the transmitter ranged 0 to 298.6 mbar, 4 mA corresponds to the level at the tube end (0.1 m) and 20 mA to the 3 m maximum. The default reading of 250 mbar gives 4 + 16 × 250 ÷ 298.6 = 17.40 mA:

    Level from bottom Back pressure Percent of 3 m Output
    0.1 m 0.0 mbar 3.3% 4.00 mA
    0.5 m 41.2 mbar 16.7% 6.21 mA
    1.0 m 92.7 mbar 33.3% 8.97 mA
    1.5 m 144.2 mbar 50.0% 11.72 mA

    2.5 m 247.1 mbar 83.3% 17.24 mA

    Below 0.1 m the tube end is uncovered, the back pressure stays at zero and the output sits at 4 mA, so a bubbler cannot tell 0.1 m from an empty tank.

    Second example: density drift with temperature

    The same liquor is heated and its SG falls to 1.02. The level is still 2.528 m, but the head of 2.428 m now produces only 242.9 mbar. A transmitter or DCS block still configured for SG 1.05 reports 2.459 m, 69 mm low. If the density varies this much in service, add a second dip tube and compensate, or configure the density at the normal operating temperature.

    Purge gas consumption

    A steady 0.5 L/min of free air is 0.72 m³ a day, or about 263 m³ a year per tank. That is small for instrument air but worth counting when nitrogen is bought in cylinders, and a reason to fit a constant flow regulator rather than leaving a needle valve wide open.

    Common mistakes

    • Purging too hard. Friction in a long or narrow purge line adds pressure that is not liquid head, so the level reads high. Keep the flow low and the transmitter tee near the tank.
    • Forgetting the tube end offset. The bubbler measures from the tube end, not from the tank bottom; adding z back is essential for volume calculations.
    • Using the wrong density. Level is inversely proportional to SG, so a 5% density error gives a 5% level error. Use the density at operating temperature, or add a second tube for density compensation.
    • Gas supply pressure set too low. If the supply is below the maximum back pressure, purge flow stops before the tank is full and the reading freezes.
    • Ignoring pressure in a closed tank. In a vented or pressurised tank the reading must be referenced to the vapour space with a DP transmitter, or any tank pressure appears as false level.

    Frequently asked questions

    How does a bubbler level system work?

    Gas flows through a regulator and a small rotameter into a dip tube that reaches near the tank bottom. The gas pressure rises until it equals the liquid head at the tube end, then bubbles escape. From then on the pressure in the tube, measured by a gauge or transmitter, follows the level: level = back pressure ÷ (density × g).

    How much purge flow does a bubbler need?

    Only enough for a slow, steady stream of bubbles, often around 1 standard cubic foot per hour (about 0.5 L/min). Too much flow adds friction loss in the tube and makes the reading high and noisy; too little lets liquid creep up the tube and slows the response when the level rises.

    Why does the bubbler not read down to the tank bottom?

    The back pressure is the head above the tube end, so the measurement starts at the tube end. The tube is kept a little above the bottom to stay clear of sludge, and the calculator adds that height back so the level is reported from the tank bottom.

    Can a bubbler be used on a closed or pressurised tank?

    Yes, with a DP transmitter: the HP side connects to the purge line and the LP side to the vapour space. The DP is then the liquid head alone, and the gas supply pressure must exceed the tank pressure plus the maximum head.

    Can bubblers measure density as well as level?

    Yes. Two dip tubes ending at a known vertical distance apart give a pressure difference that depends only on density: ρ = ΔP ÷ (g × Δz). This is used in sugar, mining and chemical tanks, and the density can then compensate the level reading.

    What mA output should a bubbler transmitter give at a known level?

    Output = 4 + 16 × back pressure ÷ URV. For SG 1.05, a tube end 0.1 m above the bottom and a 3 m maximum, the URV is 298.6 mbar, so a reading of 250 mbar (level 2.528 m) gives 17.40 mA.

    How much purge gas does a bubbler use in a year?

    At 0.5 L/min a bubbler uses 0.72 m³ of free gas a day, about 263 m³ a year. Instrument air makes this trivial, but on bottled nitrogen it decides how often cylinders are changed.
