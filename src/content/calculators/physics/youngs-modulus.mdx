## How to use the Young's modulus calculator

Enter the **axial force**, the **cross-sectional area** of the specimen, its **original length** and the measured **extension**. The calculator gives the stress, strain and Young's modulus in MPa and GPa. To predict the extension instead, enter the modulus of the material (for example 200,000 MPa for steel) and clear the extension field. Use a negative force and extension for compression.

## Stress, strain and Young's modulus formulas

<p class="formula">σ = F ÷ A &nbsp;·&nbsp; ε = ΔL ÷ L<sub>0</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; E = σ ÷ ε</p>

<p class="formula">ΔL = F L<sub>0</sub> ÷ (A E)</p>

## Example

A 10 mm diameter steel rod, 2 m long, carries a 10 kN tensile load and stretches 1.25 mm:

- A = π × 10² ÷ 4 = **78.54 mm²**
- σ = 10,000 ÷ 78.54 mm² = **127.32 MPa**
- ε = 1.25 ÷ 2000 = **0.000625** (625 µε)
- E = 127.32 ÷ 0.000625 = **203,718 MPa ≈ 203.7 GPa** — typical for steel

## Notes

- 1 GPa = 1,000 MPa = 1 kN/mm². Most metals sit between 40 and 400 GPa.
- The modulus falls as temperature rises — carbon steel loses roughly 5–10% of its room-temperature value by 300 °C.
- For a spring-like view of the same bar, its stiffness is k = A E ÷ L<sub>0</sub>; see the [Hooke's law calculator](/physics/hookes-law/).

## Stiffness and safety of the default rod

The default bar is more than a modulus test: it is also a spring. Its axial stiffness is k = A E ÷ L<sub>0</sub> = 78.54 mm² × 203.7 GPa ÷ 2 m = **8.00 kN/mm**, so every extra kilonewton stretches it by another 0.125 mm. At 127.3 MPa it is working at 46% of the 275 MPa yield strength of S275 steel, a safety factor of 2.16, which is why it springs back fully when the load is removed.

## Same rod and load in other materials

A 10 kN pull on a 10 mm diameter bar, 2 m long, gives 127.3 MPa in every material, but the extension depends on E:

| Material | E | Extension | Typical yield strength | Still elastic? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Structural steel | 200 GPa | 1.27 mm | 275 MPa | yes |
| Stainless steel 304 | 193 GPa | 1.32 mm | 215 MPa | yes |
| Aluminium 6061-T6 | 69 GPa | 3.69 mm | 276 MPa | yes |
| Copper (annealed) | 117 GPa | 2.18 mm | 70 MPa | no, it yields |
| Titanium Ti-6Al-4V | 114 GPa | 2.23 mm | 880 MPa | yes |

Copper shows why the modulus alone is not enough: the formula predicts a small extension, but annealed copper has already yielded at this stress, so the real stretch is larger and permanent.

## How Young's modulus is measured

In a tensile test (ASTM E8 for the strength values, ASTM E111 for the modulus itself) a machined specimen is pulled while a clip on extensometer records the extension over a fixed gauge length, often 50 mm. E is the slope of the straight part of the stress strain curve, fitted between roughly 10% and 40% of yield rather than read from a single point. Bonded strain gauges give the same slope on parts that cannot be cut into specimens. Dynamic methods such as impulse excitation (ASTM E1876) tap a bar and work E out from its resonant frequency, which suits brittle ceramics and glass.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing mm² and m².** 78.54 mm² is 78.54 × 10⁻⁶ m²; dropping the 10⁻⁶ makes the stress a million times too small.
- **Using diameter as radius.** A = π d² ÷ 4. Even a 0.1 mm error on the 10 mm rod shifts the calculated E by 2.0%, so measure the diameter with a micrometer.
- **Dividing by the wrong length.** Strain uses the length the extension was measured over. Over a 50 mm extensometer the same rod only moves 0.0312 mm, not 1.25 mm.
- **Reading the modulus past yield.** Points beyond the proportional limit give a falling apparent E that is not a material property.
- **Ignoring grip slip.** Crosshead movement includes slack in the grips and machine frame, so a modulus worked out from it is usually far too low.
