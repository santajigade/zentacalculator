faq:
  - q: What is Hooke's law?
    a: The force needed to stretch or compress a spring is proportional to the change in length, F = k x, where k is the spring constant. It holds as long as the spring stays within its elastic limit; the spring then pulls back with the same force in the opposite direction (F = −k x).
  - q: How do I find the spring constant?
    a: Hang a known mass from the spring and measure the extension, then divide the weight by the extension, k = m g ÷ x. A 1 kg mass (9.81 N) that stretches a spring by 2 cm gives k = 9.81 ÷ 0.02 ≈ 490 N/m. Using several masses and taking the slope of the force–extension line is more accurate.
  - q: How much energy does a spring store?
    a: E = ½ k x², the area under the force–extension line. A 500 N/m spring compressed 4 cm stores ½ × 500 × 0.04² = 0.4 J. Doubling the compression stores four times the energy.
  - q: How do springs in series and parallel combine?
    a: In parallel the stiffnesses add, k = k₁ + k₂. In series the compliances add, 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂, so two equal springs end to end are half as stiff as one.
  - q: "How fast can a compressed spring launch an object?"
    a: "If all the stored energy becomes kinetic energy, v = √(k x² ÷ m). A 500 N/m spring compressed 4 cm stores 0.4 J and launches a 50 g ball at 4.0 m/s; friction and the spring's own moving mass make the real speed a little lower."
  - q: "Does Hooke's law apply to rubber bands?"
    a: "Only roughly, over a small stretch. Rubber gets stiffer at large extensions and returns less energy than it took to stretch it (hysteresis), so a single spring constant describes it poorly."

## How to use the Hooke's law calculator

Enter the **spring constant** and the **extension or compression** from the spring's free length. The calculator gives the **spring force**, the **elastic energy** stored and the **equivalent hanging mass** — the mass whose weight equals that force. To find the spring constant, enter the force and the extension and press × on the spring constant; to find the extension for a load, enter the force and press × on the extension.

## Hooke's law formulas

<p class="formula">F = k x &nbsp;·&nbsp; E = ½ k x² &nbsp;·&nbsp; m = F ÷ g</p>

- **k** = spring constant (stiffness) in N/m
- **x** = change in length from the free length, in metres
- **g** = 9.80665 m/s²

## Worked example

A spring with k = 500 N/m is stretched by 4 cm (0.04 m):

- Force = 500 × 0.04 = **20 N**
- Energy stored = ½ × 500 × 0.04² = **0.4 J**
- Equivalent hanging mass = 20 ÷ 9.80665 = **2.039 kg** — hanging about 2 kg from this spring stretches it by 4 cm

## Mass on a spring

A mass m on a spring oscillates with period T = 2π√(m ÷ k). A 0.5 kg mass on a 500 N/m spring bounces with a period of **0.199 s** — see the [spring–mass frequency calculator](/physics/spring-mass-frequency/).

## Second scenario: energy and launch speed

The default spring (k = 500 N/m, 2.86 lbf/in) compressed 4 cm stores 0.4 J. If it fires a 50 g ball and all of that becomes kinetic energy, the ball leaves at v = √(2 E ÷ m) = √(2 × 0.4 ÷ 0.05) = **4.0 m/s**. To store a full joule the spring must be compressed √(2 × 1 ÷ 500) = **6.32 cm**. Pushing on from 4 cm to 8 cm costs ½ × 500 × (0.08² − 0.04²) = **1.2 J**, three times the work of the first 4 cm, because the force keeps rising along the way. In the other direction, hanging a 2 kg mass from the spring stretches it 3.92 cm at rest.

## Measuring a spring constant

In a school lab, hang the spring from a clamp, add slotted masses one at a time and read the pointer against a ruler, then plot force against extension and take the slope. The straight part of the line is the spring constant; where the points bend away, the spring has passed its elastic limit or, for close-wound springs, is still overcoming initial tension. Spring makers test compression springs on a load tester that records force at two set lengths, and quote the rate between them, often between about 20% and 80% of the available travel where the coils behave most linearly.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the total length instead of the change in length.** x is measured from the free (unloaded) length, not from the clamp or the floor.
- **Leaving x in centimetres.** With k in N/m, 4 cm must be entered as 0.04 m in a hand calculation, or the force comes out 100 times too large.
- **Entering a mass as a force.** A 2 kg load is 19.61 N; dividing 2 by k instead gives an extension almost ten times too small.
- **Assuming energy doubles with extension.** It rises with x², so the second 4 cm stores three times as much as the first.
- **Ignoring initial tension and preload.** Extension springs need a starting force before they move, and compression springs in a mechanism are usually fitted already compressed, so add the preload length to x.

## Practical notes

- **Elastic limit:** beyond it the spring takes a permanent set and no longer returns to its free length; real springs also become non-linear near solid height (compression) or when the coils open up (extension).
- **Initial tension:** close-wound extension springs need a starting force before they stretch at all — add it to k x.
- To design a coil spring from wire size, coil diameter and number of turns, use the [spring rate calculator](/engineering/spring-rate/).
- For solid materials the same law appears as stress = Young's modulus × strain; steel has a Young's modulus of about 200 GPa — see the [Young's modulus calculator](/physics/youngs-modulus/).
