## How to use the protein molecular weight estimator

Enter the **number of amino acids**. The estimated molecular weight is shown in kDa. Or enter the kDa (for example from a gel) to estimate the length.

## Protein molecular weight formula

<p class="formula">MW (kDa) ≈ amino acids × 110 ÷ 1000</p>

## Example

A 350-residue protein: 350 × 110 = 38,500 Da = **38.5 kDa**.

## Gene length

Each amino acid is coded by 3 nucleotides, so a 350-residue protein needs a coding sequence of 1,053 bp (including the stop codon).

## From DNA to protein size

Each amino acid is coded by 3 nucleotides. A 1,500 bp open reading frame (including the stop codon) encodes 499 amino acids — about **55 kDa**.

## Why 110 Da?

The average mass of an amino acid in proteins is about 128 Da, but each peptide bond releases one water molecule (18 Da), giving an average residue mass of about 110 Da. Proteins rich in heavy residues (Trp, Tyr, Arg) are heavier than estimated.

## Measured vs predicted size

On SDS-PAGE gels, proteins may run at a different apparent size than predicted because of:

- Post-translational modifications (glycosylation adds mass).
- Unusual charge or shape.
- Incomplete denaturation.

Mass spectrometry gives the most accurate molecular weight.

## Daltons and kilodaltons

1 kDa = 1,000 Da. A dalton is about the mass of one hydrogen atom (1 g/mol).

## Exact mass from the sequence

The 110 Da average is good for whole proteins, but the true mass is the sum of the residue masses plus one water (18.02 Da) for the free ends. Average residue masses (amino acid minus water):

For the short peptide GAVLK, the sum is 468.60 + 18.02 = **486.61 Da**, while the 110 Da rule gives 550 Da, 13% too high. Short peptides rich in small residues such as glycine and alanine are where the estimate fails; for a full sequence, use a sequence tool such as ExPASy ProtParam.

## Tags, signal peptides and complexes

The default 350 residue protein comes out at **38.5 kDa**. Real constructs rarely match the bare gene:

- A His<sub>6</sub> tag adds 823 Da; with a typical 14 residue linker and protease site the tag region is about 20 residues, giving 40.7 kDa.
- A secreted protein loses its signal peptide. Removing a 25 residue signal leaves a mature chain of about 35.8 kDa.
- A homodimer is 77 kDa in native gel filtration, but still runs at 38.5 kDa on a reducing SDS gel because the subunits separate.

## Converting mass concentration to molarity

Assays, binding studies and crystallisation screens need molar concentrations, and the molecular weight is the conversion factor: µM = (mg/mL) ÷ kDa × 1,000. For the default 38.5 kDa protein, 1 mg/mL is **26.0 µM**.

| Protein size | 1 mg/mL equals | 10 µM equals |
|---|---|---|
| 10 kDa | 100.0 µM | 0.100 mg/mL |
| 25 kDa | 40.0 µM | 0.250 mg/mL |
| 38.5 kDa | 26.0 µM | 0.385 mg/mL |
| 50 kDa | 20.0 µM | 0.500 mg/mL |
| 100 kDa | 10.0 µM | 1.000 mg/mL |
| 150 kDa | 6.7 µM | 1.500 mg/mL |

The same rule picks a membrane for concentrating or dialysing: choose a molecular weight cutoff of a third to a half of the protein mass, so a 10 kDa cutoff device suits a 38.5 kDa protein.

## Common mistakes

- **Using 128 Da per residue.** That is the average free amino acid; inside a chain each residue has lost a water, so the estimate comes out about 16% high.
- **Counting the stop codon.** A 1,053 bp coding sequence gives 350 residues, not 351; the stop codon codes for no amino acid.
- **Forgetting cleaved and added parts.** Signal peptides, propeptides and the initiator methionine may be removed, while tags and fusion partners such as GST (about 26 kDa) add a lot of mass.
- **Reading native size from an SDS gel.** SDS separates subunits, so oligomers appear at the monomer mass.
- **Expecting glycoproteins to match.** Glycosylation can add 10 to 50% to the mass and makes bands run broad and high.
