    A transformer's own impedance is the main thing that limits current when a fault strikes its secondary terminals. This transformer short-circuit current calculator takes the kVA rating, secondary voltage and nameplate impedance and returns the full-load current, the symmetrical three-phase fault current and the fault level in MVA.

    Electrical designers use it to pick breaker breaking capacity, busbar ratings and cable withstand, as explained in this guide to short circuit current calculation.

    How to use the transformer short-circuit current calculator

    Enter the transformer rating, secondary voltage and percentage impedance from the nameplate. You get the full-load current, the maximum symmetrical three-phase short-circuit current and the fault level in MVA.

    For more background, read How to Calculate Short Circuit Fault Current.

    Transformer short-circuit current formulas

    IFL = kVA × 1,000 ÷ (√3 × V)  ·  Isc = IFL ÷ (Z% ÷ 100)

    Fault MVA = kVA ÷ (Z% ÷ 100) ÷ 1,000

    Typical impedances follow IEC 60076-5 guidance for distribution transformers.

    Choosing breaker breaking capacity

    Select LV breakers with a rated short-circuit breaking capacity (Icu/Ics) above the prospective fault current at their location — for example, 36 kA or 50 kA breakers for a 1,000 kVA, 5% transformer.

    Reducing fault current

    • Choose transformers with higher impedance (at the cost of more voltage regulation).
    • Split loads across multiple smaller transformers instead of paralleling.
    • Use current-limiting fuses or reactors.

    Beyond this estimate

    A full short-circuit study (IEC 60909) includes source impedance, motor contribution, cable impedance and X/R ratios. Use this calculator for quick, conservative checks.

    Worked example with a real upstream supply

    For the default 1,000 kVA, 415 V, 5% transformer:

    • IFL = 1,000,000 ÷ (√3 × 415) = 1,391.2 A
    • Isc = 1,391.2 ÷ 0.05 = 27.82 kA, a fault level of 20 MVA

    That assumes the 11 kV network behind the transformer has no impedance. If the utility quotes a fault level of 250 MVA at the primary, its impedance on the 1 MVA transformer base is 1 ÷ 250 = 0.40%. Adding it to the transformer gives 5.40% and the fault current falls to 25.8 kA, only about 7% lower. For LV switchgear selection the infinite bus figure is therefore a sensible, slightly conservative number.

    Effect of the upstream fault level

    1,000 kVA, 415 V, 5% transformer:

    The stronger the grid, the closer the result gets to the simple formula. Weak rural supplies matter more for small transformers on long overhead lines.

    Peak current and making capacity

    The calculator gives the symmetrical RMS current. The first peak is higher because of the DC offset. IEC 60909 gives ip = κ × √2 × Ik with κ = 1.02 + 0.98 e−3R/X. With X/R = 5, κ = 1.558 and the peak is 61.3 kA. Breakers are rated for this through their making capacity Icm, and busbar supports must withstand the force it produces.

    Where the impedance figure comes from

    Percentage impedance is measured in the factory short-circuit test: the secondary is shorted and the primary voltage is raised until rated current flows. On an 11 kV primary a 5% unit reaches full-load current at about 550 V. That voltage as a percentage of rated voltage is Z%, which is why a lower Z% always means a higher fault current.

    Common mistakes

    • Ignoring the impedance tolerance. IEC 60076-1 allows the measured impedance to be 10% below the declared value. At 4.5% the default transformer gives 30.9 kA, which can push a 30 kA rated board over its limit.
    • Forgetting parallel transformers. Two 1,000 kVA, 5% units on one busbar give about 55.6 kA, so the switchboard must be rated for the combined value.
    • Leaving out motor contribution. Running induction motors feed the fault for the first few cycles, often taken as about 4 times their combined full-load current.
    • Comparing RMS current with a peak rating. Icu and Ics are RMS values; Icm is a peak value and must be checked against ip.
    • Entering phase voltage. Use the line voltage (415 V, not 240 V); phase voltage overstates the current by √3.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I calculate transformer fault current?

    Isc = full-load current ÷ (percentage impedance ÷ 100). A 1,000 kVA, 415 V, 5% transformer gives about 27.8 kA.

    Why is this the maximum fault current?

    The method assumes an infinite source with zero impedance upstream. Real upstream impedance, cables and arc resistance reduce the actual fault current.

    Where do I find the impedance?

    On the transformer nameplate, usually 4–6% for distribution transformers and higher for large units.

    Why does fault current matter?

    Breakers and switchgear must have a breaking capacity higher than the prospective fault current, and cables must withstand it until protection operates.

    How does the upstream grid fault level change transformer fault current?

    Convert the grid fault level to an impedance on the transformer base (kVA ÷ 1,000 ÷ grid MVA) and add it to Z%. For 1,000 kVA, 5% and a 250 MVA grid, the total is 5.40% and the fault current drops from 27.8 kA to 25.8 kA.

    What is the peak short-circuit current of a transformer fault?

    Multiply the RMS fault current by κ × √2, where κ depends on the X/R ratio. With X/R = 5, κ is 1.56, so a 27.8 kA fault peaks at about 61 kA in the first half cycle.
