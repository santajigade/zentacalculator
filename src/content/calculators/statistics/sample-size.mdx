## How to use the sample size calculator

Choose the **confidence level**, set the **margin of error** and the **population size**. Leave the expected proportion at 50% unless you have a prior estimate. Round the result **up**.

## Sample size formulas

<p class="formula">n₀ = z² p(1 − p) ÷ E²   n = n₀ ÷ (1 + (n₀ − 1) ÷ N)</p>

## Worked example

95% confidence, ±5%, p = 50%, population 10,000:

- n₀ = 1.96² × 0.25 ÷ 0.0025 = 384.1 → **385**
- With the population correction: **370** responses

## Allow for non-response

If you expect only 40% of people to reply, divide the needed sample by 0.4. To get 385 completed surveys with a 40% response rate, invite about **963 people**.

## Sample size for a mean

When estimating a mean rather than a proportion:

<p class="formula">n = (z × σ ÷ E)²</p>

To estimate average weight within ±2 kg (σ ≈ 12 kg) at 95%: n = (1.96 × 12 ÷ 2)² = **139**.

## Subgroups

If you need results for subgroups (such as each region), each subgroup needs its own adequate sample. A national poll of 1,000 might have only 100 people in a small region, with a margin of about ±10%.

## Experiments and A/B tests

Comparing two groups needs a power calculation, which accounts for the smallest effect worth detecting and the chance of missing it (usually 80% power). Detecting a small difference needs a much larger sample than estimating a single proportion.

## Margin of error for a sample you already have

You can turn the formula around to see how precise a finished survey is:

<p class="formula">E = z × √(p (1 − p) ÷ n)</p>

With 400 responses and p = 50%, E = 1.96 × √(0.25 ÷ 400) = **±4.9%** at 95% confidence. Halving the margin of error needs four times as many responses.

## Cluster samples and the design effect

If you survey whole groups (schools, branches, villages) instead of picking individuals at random, people in the same group tend to answer alike. Multiply the sample size by the **design effect**, often 1.5 to 2. A 385-person target becomes about **578** with a design effect of 1.5.

## When you already know roughly what the answer will be

The 50% default gives the largest sample. If an earlier survey showed that about 10% of customers use a feature, p(1 − p) falls from 0.25 to 0.09 and the sample needed drops sharply. Values for ±5% and ±3% margins at 95% confidence:

| Expected proportion | ±5%, large population | ±5%, population 10,000 | ±3%, large population |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% or 95% | 73 | 73 | 203 |
| 10% or 90% | 139 | 137 | 385 |
| 20% or 80% | 246 | 240 | 683 |
| 30% or 70% | 323 | 313 | 897 |
| 40% or 60% | 369 | 356 | 1,025 |
| 50% | 385 | 370 | 1,068 |

A proportion of 10% and 90% need the same sample, because p(1 − p) is symmetric. Only use a value below 50% when it comes from real prior data; guessing low makes the survey too small.

## Worked example: a staff survey in a company of 600

An HR team wants ±5% at 95% confidence from 600 employees, with no prior estimate (p = 50%). The large-population figure is 385, but the population is small, so the correction applies: n = 384.15 ÷ (1 + 383.15 ÷ 600) = **235 completed responses**. Past surveys got a 60% response rate, so the team invites 235 ÷ 0.6 = **392 people**, which is almost everyone. In small groups it is often simpler to invite the whole population.

## A/B test sample size per group

Comparing two conversion rates needs a power calculation, not the formula above. To detect a lift from **10% to 12%** with a two-sided 5% significance level and 80% power, each group needs

<p class="formula">n = (z<sub>α/2</sub>√(2p̄q̄) + z<sub>β</sub>√(p₁q₁ + p₂q₂))² ÷ (p₂ − p₁)²</p>

which gives **3,841 visitors per group** (z = 1.960 and 0.842, p̄ = 0.11). Detecting a bigger lift, 10% to 13%, needs only 1,774 per group: the sample grows with the inverse square of the difference you want to see.

## Drawing the sample in practice

The formula assumes every member of the population had the same chance of being picked. In practice that means starting from a complete list (the sampling frame), such as an employee register or customer database, and selecting at random from it, or using stratified sampling so each region or department is represented in proportion. Send reminders, track who has replied, and compare the profile of respondents with the population before trusting the margin of error.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting invitations instead of responses.** The formula gives completed responses. With a 20% response rate, 385 responses needs about 1,925 invitations.
- **Using the formula for a convenience sample.** An online poll anyone can join is not random, and no sample size fixes that bias.
- **Reporting subgroups with the overall margin.** If only 100 people are under 25, their results carry about ±10%, not ±5%.
- **Forgetting the design effect** when sampling in clusters.
- **Rounding down.** 384.15 means 385 responses; stopping at 384 leaves the margin slightly wider than the target.
