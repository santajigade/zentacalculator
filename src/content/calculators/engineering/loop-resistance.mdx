## How to use the 4–20 mA loop resistance calculator

Enter the **loop supply voltage**, the transmitter's **minimum operating (lift-off) voltage** from its datasheet, the **maximum loop current**, the receiver's **input resistance**, any **barrier or isolator** resistance, and the **cable length and conductor resistance**. The calculator shows the maximum resistance the loop can tolerate, the actual resistance and the margin.

For more background, read [2-Wire, 3-Wire and 4-Wire Transmitters](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/2-wire-3-wire-4-wire-transmitters-in-industrial-automation/).

## 4–20 mA loop resistance formulas

<p class="formula">R<sub>max</sub> = (V<sub>supply</sub> − V<sub>transmitter,min</sub>) ÷ I<sub>max</sub></p>

<p class="formula">R<sub>loop</sub> = R<sub>input</sub> + R<sub>barrier</sub> + 2 × length × r<sub>cable</sub></p>

## Example

24 V supply, transmitter needs 10.5 V, 22 mA maximum, 250 Ω PLC input, 500 m of 1.5 mm² cable:

- R<sub>max</sub> = 13.5 ÷ 0.022 = **614 Ω**
- Cable = 2 × 0.5 × 12.1 = 12.1 Ω; total loop = **262 Ω**
- Margin = **352 Ω** — the loop works, with room for a barrier

## Troubleshooting a low-voltage loop

If the reading clips before 100%, measure the voltage at the transmitter terminals while forcing 20–22 mA. If it is below the lift-off voltage, raise the supply, remove a series device, or use a lower-resistance input.

## Voltage left at the transmitter

The margin in ohms is easier to judge as volts at the transmitter terminals. With the default loop drawing 22 mA through 262.1 Ω, the receiver and cable drop 5.766 V, so the transmitter sees

- V = 24 − 0.022 × 262.1 = **18.23 V**, comfortably above its 10.5 V minimum

Turned around, the smallest supply this loop could run on is 10.5 + 0.022 × 262.1 = **16.27 V**.

## Adding an intrinsic safety barrier

Put a shunt diode (Zener) barrier of about 340 Ω end to end into the same loop and the load becomes 262.1 + 340 = **602.1 Ω**. The margin shrinks to **11.5 Ω** and the transmitter is left with 10.75 V. That still works on a full 24 V, but a supply at its −10% tolerance (21.6 V) allows only 505 Ω, giving a margin of **-98 Ω**: the loop fails at high current. A galvanic isolator with a powered input, or a 26 V supply, fixes it.

Cable is rarely the limit in a plain loop; the receiver resistor and series devices use most of the budget. On an 18 V supply the 250 Ω input alone already exceeds what the transmitter can drive at 22 mA.

## Checking the loop on site

With the loop running, use the transmitter's loop test or HART command to force 22 mA (or 20.5 mA if 22 is not available). Measure the voltage across the transmitter terminals with a multimeter. It must stay at or above the datasheet minimum. Then measure across the receiver input; 250 Ω should show 5.5 V at 22 mA, and a much higher figure means a wrong resistor or a second receiver wired in series.

## Common mistakes

- **Using 20 mA as the design current.** The loop may pass at 20 mA and still collapse at the 21 mA or more used for fault signalling, so the alarm never reaches the DCS.
- **Taking the lift-off voltage for the basic model.** Display, HART and explosion proof options often raise the minimum voltage by 1 to 2 V; read the figure for the exact ordered version.
- **Forgetting series devices added later.** A field indicator, a trip amplifier or a second recorder each takes 1 to 5 V.
- **Designing on the nominal supply.** Use the lowest supply voltage after tolerance and diode drops from redundant supply modules.
- **Removing the 250 Ω to gain margin on a HART loop.** HART needs roughly 230 to 600 Ω of loop resistance; below that the handheld cannot communicate.
