## How to use the orbital period calculator

Enter the **central mass** (Earth by default) and the **orbit radius** measured from the centre (altitude + planet radius). You get the period and the orbital speed for a circular orbit. To find the orbit radius for a given period — for example a geostationary orbit — enter the period and press × on the radius.

## Orbital period formulas

<p class="formula">T = 2π √(a³ ÷ GM) &nbsp;·&nbsp; v = √(GM ÷ a) = 2πa ÷ T</p>

G = 6.6743 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². The formula assumes the orbiting body is much lighter than the central mass; for two comparable bodies replace M by M₁ + M₂.

## Worked example

The ISS at about 408 km altitude, a = 6,371 + 408 = 6,779 km around Earth (M = 5.972 × 10²⁴ kg):

- T = 2π √((6.779 × 10⁶ m)³ ÷ (6.6743 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.972 × 10²⁴)) = **5,555 s = 92.58 min**
- v = √(GM ÷ a) = **7,668 m/s = 27,605 km/h**

Each lap covers 2π × 6,779 = 42,594 km, and 86,400 s ÷ 5,555 s gives **15.55 orbits per day**. Multiplying speed by period gives the same circumference, 42,594 km, which is a quick check that the two results agree.

### Working backwards to a geostationary radius

Rearranging Kepler's law gives a = (G M T² ÷ 4π²)<sup>1/3</sup>. With the sidereal day, T = 86,164 s, the radius is **42,164 km**, an altitude of 35,793 km above the mean radius (35,786 km above the equatorial radius of 6,378 km).

The Moon's real sidereal period (27.32 days) is slightly shorter than this value because the Moon's own mass (1.2% of Earth's) also counts.

## Practical notes

- **Higher orbits are slower.** Orbital speed falls as 1/√a, so to catch a spacecraft ahead of you, you drop to a lower, faster orbit first.
- Low orbits decay because of atmospheric drag; the ISS is reboosted every few months.
- The period depends only on the semi-major axis, not on how elongated an elliptical orbit is; the speed, however, varies around an ellipse (fastest at perigee).

## Low orbits around other bodies

Using the masses in the field help and mean radii, the period of a low circular orbit is:

| Body | Altitude | Orbit radius | Period | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Moon | 100 km | 1,837.4 km | 117.8 min | 1,633 m/s |
| Mars | 400 km | 3,789.5 km | 118.0 min | 3,362 m/s |
| Venus | 250 km | 6,301.8 km | 91.9 min | 7,180 m/s |
| Earth | 408 km | 6,779.0 km | 92.6 min | 7,668 m/s |

The Moon's low orbit is slower than Earth's mainly because its mass is 81 times smaller, while Venus, almost Earth's twin in mass and size, gives a very similar period.

## Elliptical orbits from perigee and apogee

Orbits are usually published as perigee and apogee altitudes, worked out from radar, optical or GPS tracking. The semi-major axis is the planet radius plus the average of the two: for a highly elliptical orbit at 600 km by 39,700 km, a = 6,371 + (600 + 39,700) ÷ 2 = 26,521 km. Entering that gives T = 11.94 h, close to half a sidereal day. The circular speed output does not apply here; the vis viva equation v = √(GM (2 ÷ r − 1 ÷ a)) gives 9,966 m/s at perigee and only 1,508 m/s at apogee.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering altitude instead of radius.** Typing 408 km for the ISS gives a period of 82 s, an orbit inside the Earth. Add the planet radius first.
- **Using 24 hours for geostationary orbit.** A 24 h period puts the orbit at 42,241 km, about 77 km too high; the correct period is the sidereal day of 23 h 56 min 4 s.
- **Entering the mass in Earth masses.** The field needs kilograms, so Earth is 5.972 × 10²⁴, not 1.
- **Reading the speed output for an ellipse.** It is the circular orbit speed at radius a; on an elliptical path the speed changes all the way round.
