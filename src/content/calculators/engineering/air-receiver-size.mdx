## How to use the air receiver size calculator

Enter the **compressor capacity** and the **peak demand** as free air delivery, the **duration** of the peak, the local **atmospheric pressure**, the **receiver pressure at the start** of the event (the compressor unload or cut-out pressure) and the **lowest pressure** your equipment can accept. Add the compressor's **maximum load cycles per hour**. The calculator gives the volume needed for each case and recommends the larger one. If the compressor is off during the event (for example, a backup tank), enter 0 for its capacity in the demand-event case.

## Air receiver sizing formulas

<p class="formula">Demand event: V = t × (Q<sub>d</sub> − Q<sub>c</sub>) × P<sub>atm</sub> ÷ (P<sub>1</sub> − P<sub>2</sub>)</p>

<p class="formula">Load/unload cycling: V = 0.25 × Q<sub>c</sub> × P<sub>atm</sub> ÷ (f<sub>max</sub> × (P<sub>1</sub> − P<sub>2</sub>))</p>

Both assume the air in the receiver stays at about the same temperature as the intake air. The cycling formula is the one used in compressor manufacturers' sizing guides; the worst case is a demand equal to half the compressor capacity.

## Worked example

A compressor delivering 100 L/s (6 m³/min) between 7.5 and 6 bar g, with a 120 s peak demand of 150 L/s and a limit of 60 load cycles per hour, at sea level:

- Pressure band = 7.5 − 6 = **1.5 bar**
- Demand event: V = 120 × (0.15 − 0.1) × 101,325 ÷ 150,000 = **4.053 m³**
- Cycling: V = 0.25 × 0.1 × 101,325 ÷ (60/3600 × 150,000) = **1.013 m³**
- Recommended receiver: **4.053 m³** (1,071 US gal) — the next standard size is a 5 m³ tank

## Practical notes

- **Doubling the pressure band halves the receiver**, but every 1 bar of extra discharge pressure costs about 7% more compressor energy — a pressure/flow controller downstream of a large receiver is often cheaper.
- Variable-speed compressors need much less storage than load/unload machines; follow the maker's minimum system volume.
- Receivers are pressure vessels: they need a relief valve sized for the full compressor capacity, a pressure gauge, an automatic drain and periodic inspection — see the [pressure vessel thickness calculator](/engineering/pressure-vessel-thickness/).
- Include the volume of large distribution headers when they are close to the users; piping volume acts as storage too.

## Measuring real demand with a drawdown test

The receiver itself can be used as a flow meter. Stop the compressor (or isolate it) with the plant running and time how long the pressure takes to fall. For a 5 m³ receiver falling from 7.5 to 6.5 bar g in 90 s:

- Free air drawn from storage = 5 × (7.5 − 6.5) × 100 ÷ 101.325 = **4.935 m³**
- Average demand = 4.935 ÷ 90 = **54.8 L/s**

Repeat the test with production stopped and the result is the leakage rate of the system. Include the volume of the main headers in V if they are large, or the demand is overstated.

## Altitude and pressure band

Free air delivery is referred to the intake pressure, so the same flows need less stored volume at altitude. At 1,500 m the standard atmosphere is about **84.6 kPa**, and the demand event in the worked example needs 3.382 m³ instead of 4.053 m³. Widening the usable band from 1.5 to 2 bar cuts the event volume to 3.040 m³, but every extra bar of compressor discharge pressure adds energy cost, so a wider band is only worth it when the users can genuinely tolerate the lower pressure.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering compressed flow instead of free air.** The formulas use flows at intake conditions (FAD, Nm³ or scfm); a flow at 7 bar is about eight times smaller and gives an undersized tank.
- **Setting P2 to the tool pressure.** Dryers, filters and pipework take 0.5 to 1 bar between the receiver and the users, so P2 is the receiver pressure needed to keep the farthest tool supplied, not the tool's own rating.
- **Using gauge pressure for P_atm.** Atmospheric pressure converts free air into stored volume and must be absolute; only P1 and P2 may both be gauge.
- **Sizing only for cycling.** A large intermittent user such as a blast pot or a baghouse pulse system usually needs its own receiver close to it, because the main receiver cannot refill through a long, small header fast enough.
- **Ignoring the drain.** Condensate collecting in the bottom reduces the working volume and corrodes the shell; a blocked automatic drain slowly turns a correctly sized receiver into a small one.
