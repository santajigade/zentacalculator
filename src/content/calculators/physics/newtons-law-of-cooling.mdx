## How to use the Newton's law of cooling calculator

Enter the **initial temperature** of the object, the **ambient temperature**, the **cooling constant** k (per minute) and the **elapsed time**. The calculator returns the temperature at that time, the **half-time** (when the difference to ambient has halved) and the time constant 1/k. To find k from a measurement, clear k and enter the measured temperature; to find how long cooling takes, clear the time.

## Newton's law of cooling formula

<p class="formula">T(t) = T<sub>a</sub> + (T<sub>0</sub> − T<sub>a</sub>) e<sup>−kt</sup></p>

<p class="formula">t<sub>½</sub> = ln 2 ÷ k &nbsp;·&nbsp; τ = 1 ÷ k &nbsp;·&nbsp; k = h A ÷ (m c)</p>

The constant k combines the heat transfer coefficient h, surface area A, mass m and specific heat c of the object.

## Example

A cup of coffee at 90 °C in a 20 °C room with k = 0.05 per minute, after 10 minutes:

- T = 20 + (90 − 20) × e<sup>−0.05 × 10</sup> = 20 + 70 × 0.6065 = **62.46 °C**
- Half-time = ln 2 ÷ 0.05 = **13.86 min**
- Time constant = 1 ÷ 0.05 = **20 min**

After one time constant 36.8% of the difference remains; after five time constants less than 1% remains.

## Notes

- Stirring, wind or a fan raises h and therefore k — the cup cools faster.
- The same exponential law describes a thermometer or [sensor reaching a step change](/engineering/sensor-response-time/), with τ as its time constant.
- Forensic "time since death" estimates use the same principle, with empirically corrected k values.

## Time to reach a target temperature

Rearranging the law gives t = ln[(T<sub>0</sub> − T<sub>a</sub>) ÷ (T − T<sub>a</sub>)] ÷ k. With the default inputs (90 °C, 20 °C room, k = 0.05 per minute) the calculator shows **62.46 °C** after 10 min, a half-time of **13.86 min** and a time constant of **20 min**. The same coffee reaches a comfortable 60 °C after ln(70 ÷ 40) ÷ 0.05 = **11.19 min**.

How fast it gets there depends entirely on k:

| k | Time constant | Half-time | Time from 90 to 60 °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.02 /min | 50.0 min | 34.7 min | 28.0 min |
| 0.03 /min | 33.3 min | 23.1 min | 18.7 min |
| 0.05 /min | 20.0 min | 13.9 min | 11.2 min |
| 0.08 /min | 12.5 min | 8.7 min | 7.0 min |
| 0.12 /min | 8.3 min | 5.8 min | 4.7 min |

A lid, an insulated mug or a larger volume lowers k; stirring, a draught or a thin metal cup raises it.

## Finding k from two readings

The starting temperature does not have to be known. If a probe reads 77 °C at 4 min and 58 °C at 12 min in a 20 °C room, then k = ln[(77 − 20) ÷ (58 − 20)] ÷ (12 − 4) = **0.0507 per minute**. Take the readings well apart, because the logarithm magnifies small errors when the two differences are nearly equal.

## Estimating k from the physics

For a solid with a uniform temperature, k = hA ÷ (mc). A 100 g aluminium block (c = 897 J/kg·K) with 60 cm² of surface in still air (h ≈ 15 W/m²·K including radiation) gives k = 15 × 0.006 ÷ (0.1 × 897) × 60 = **0.0602 per minute**. The lumped model is valid when the Biot number hL<sub>c</sub>/k<sub>solid</sub> is below 0.1; here L<sub>c</sub> = V/A = 6.17 mm and Bi = 4.5e-04, so the block is effectively uniform. A thick block of plastic or food is not, and its centre lags the surface.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing units of k.** This calculator uses k per minute. A k quoted per second must be multiplied by 60 first.
- **Using the temperature itself instead of the difference.** The exponential applies to T − T<sub>a</sub>; percentages of the starting temperature are meaningless.
- **Assuming k is constant over a wide range.** Natural convection and radiation both strengthen at large differences, so fit k over the range you actually care about.
- **Ignoring evaporation.** An open cup of hot liquid loses a large share of its heat by evaporation, so it cools faster at first than the law predicts.
- **Applying it during a phase change.** While water freezes or wax solidifies the temperature stays flat; use the [latent heat calculator](/physics/latent-heat/) for that stage.
