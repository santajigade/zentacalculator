## How to use the three-phase motor power & current calculator

The defaults show an **11 kW motor on 415 V** — the full-load current is calculated. You can also enter a measured current to find the power being drawn. Use the motor's nameplate values for power factor and efficiency where possible.

For more background, read [Motor Efficiency Classes (IEC 60034)](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/motor-efficiency-classes-iec-60034/).

## Three-phase motor power & current formulas

<p class="formula">P<sub>in</sub> = √3 × V × I × cos φ   P<sub>out</sub> = P<sub>in</sub> × η</p>

## Worked example

11 kW motor, 415 V, cos φ = 0.85, efficiency 90%:

- P<sub>in</sub> = 11 ÷ 0.90 = 12.22 kW
- I = 12,222 ÷ (1.732 × 415 × 0.85) = **20.0 A**

## Single-phase vs three-phase

| | Single-phase | Three-phase |
|---|---|---|
| Power formula | P = V × I × pf | P = √3 × V<sub>L</sub> × I<sub>L</sub> × pf |
| Typical use | Homes, small motors under 2–3 kW | Industrial motors and large loads |
| Torque | Pulsating | Smooth |

## Starting current

Direct-on-line (DOL) starting draws **6–8 times** the full-load current briefly. Star-delta starters reduce it to about one-third, and soft starters or VFDs reduce it further. Size cables, fuses and generators to cope with starting current.

## Power factor correction

Motors draw reactive power that does no useful work but loads cables and transformers. Adding capacitors raises power factor towards 0.95–0.98, reducing current and, in many tariffs, electricity charges.

## Apparent, real and reactive power

- **Apparent power** S (kVA) = √3 × V × I
- **Real power** P (kW) = S × pf
- **Reactive power** Q (kVAr) = √(S² − P²)

## Practical notes

- **Starting current** of a direct-on-line motor is typically **6–8×** full-load current.
- **Cable sizing** must account for both current and length — check with the [cable voltage drop calculator](/electrical/cable-voltage-drop/).
- For single-phase, use P = V × I × cos φ instead.

## Apparent and reactive power for the default motor

For the 11 kW, 415 V motor at full load the calculator gives P<sub>in</sub> = **12.22 kW** and I = **20.00 A**. The same figures give:

- Apparent power S = √3 × 415 × 20.00 = **14.38 kVA**
- Reactive power Q = √(14.38² − 12.22²) = **7.57 kVAr**
- Losses inside the motor = 12.22 − 11 = **1.22 kW**, all turned into heat

The kVA figure is what a generator or transformer must supply, and the kVAr figure is what a capacitor bank would have to cancel to bring the power factor to unity.

## Estimating load from a measured current

Leave P<sub>out</sub> blank and enter a measured current instead. If a clamp meter reads **14.2 A** and a power analyser shows a power factor of **0.78**, the input power is √3 × 415 × 14.2 × 0.78 = **7.96 kW** and the shaft output is about **7.17 kW**, roughly 65% of rating. Enter the measured power factor rather than the nameplate value, because at part load the power factor falls a lot while current falls only a little. Judging load from current alone with the full-load pf would overstate it.

| Class (IEC 60034-30-1) | Efficiency | Input power | Current at pf 0.85 | Losses | Losses over 6,000 h |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IE1 | 87.6% | 12.56 kW | 20.6 A | 1.56 kW | 9,342 kWh |
| IE2 | 89.8% | 12.25 kW | 20.0 A | 1.25 kW | 7,497 kWh |
| IE3 | 91.4% | 12.04 kW | 19.7 A | 1.04 kW | 6,210 kWh |
| IE4 | 93.3% | 11.79 kW | 19.3 A | 0.79 kW | 4,740 kWh |

Changing an IE1 motor for IE3 on a 6,000 h duty saves about **3,132 kWh** a year. The current changes by well under 1 A from one class to the next, so efficiency upgrades rarely change cable or breaker sizes.

## Measuring motor power on site

A clamp meter on one phase gives current only; to get kW you need voltage and power factor as well, which a three-phase power analyser measures directly on all three phases. Check that the three phase currents agree within a few percent: a larger imbalance usually means unbalanced supply voltages, which heat the motor far more than the current difference suggests. For hp ratings, 11 kW is about 14.8 hp (1 hp = 0.7457 kW).

## Common mistakes

- **Using phase voltage in the √3 formula.** V is the line-to-line voltage (415 V), not the 240 V phase voltage; using 240 V overstates current by √3.
- **Forgetting efficiency.** Dividing 11 kW by √3 × V × pf alone gives 18.0 A, about 10% low, because the shaft kW is not the electrical input.
- **Taking full-load pf for a lightly loaded motor.** A motor at 40% load may run at pf 0.6, so current based estimates of kW must use the measured pf.
- **Sizing protection on FLC alone.** Direct-on-line starting at 6 to 8 times FLC must ride through the breaker curve without tripping.
- **Reading the hp value as input power.** Nameplate hp, like kW, is shaft output on IEC and NEMA motors.
