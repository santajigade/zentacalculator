## How to use the thermal expansion calculator

Choose the **material**, then enter the **length** and **temperature change**. The expansion is shown in mm or inches. A negative temperature change gives contraction.

## Thermal expansion formula

<p class="formula">ΔL = α × L × ΔT</p>

## Worked example

A 50 m carbon steel steam line heated from 20 °C to 80 °C (ΔT = 60 K):

ΔL = 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 50 × 60 = 0.036 m = **36 mm**

The same run in HDPE would grow about **600 mm** — plastic pipes need much more allowance.

## Coefficients of linear expansion

| Material | α (×10⁻⁶ per K) |
|---|---|
| Invar | 1.2 |
| Glass (borosilicate) | 3.3 |
| Concrete | 10–12 |
| Carbon steel | 11–13 |
| Stainless steel 304/316 | 16–17 |
| Copper | 17 |
| Aluminium | 23 |
| PVC | 50–80 |
| HDPE | 120–200 |

Plastic pipes expand about **10 times** more than steel for the same temperature change.

## Volume expansion of liquids

Liquids expand in volume: ΔV = β × V × ΔT. Water's β is about 0.00021 per K at 20 °C, rising to about 0.0006 near 80 °C. A 1,000 L closed heating system warmed from 10 °C to 80 °C expands by roughly 25–30 L, which is why an expansion vessel is needed.

## Thermal stress

If expansion is prevented, stress builds up:

<p class="formula">σ = E × α × ΔT</p>

For steel (E = 200 GPa, α = 12 × 10⁻⁶), a 50 K rise in a fully restrained pipe creates 120 MPa of stress — a large share of the yield strength of common steels.

## Bimetal strips

Two metals with different α bonded together bend when heated. Thermostats, circuit breakers and dial thermometers use this effect.

## Allowing for expansion

Long hot lines need expansion loops, bellows or offsets, and guides and anchors placed to direct the movement. Restraining expansion completely creates very large forces that can damage supports, flanges and connected equipment.
