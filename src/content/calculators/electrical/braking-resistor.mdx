## How to use the VFD braking resistor calculator

Enter the **DC bus voltage** at which the braking chopper switches on, the **peak braking power** and the **duty cycle**. You get the maximum resistor value, the average power rating and the peak current.

For more background, read [VFD Sizing and Selection Guide](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/vfd-sizing-selection-guide/).

## VFD braking resistor formulas

<p class="formula">R<sub>max</sub> = V<sub>DC</sub>² ÷ P<sub>brake</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; P<sub>avg</sub> = P<sub>brake</sub> × duty</p>

## Example

A 400 V drive (chopper at 760 V) braking 15 kW for 10% of the time:

- R<sub>max</sub> = 760² ÷ 15,000 = **38.5 Ω**
- Average power = **1.5 kW**

Choose a resistor between the drive's minimum resistance and 38.5 Ω, rated at least 1.5 kW continuous.

## Braking power from load inertia

For a flywheel or conveyor stopped at constant torque, the peak braking power at the start of the stop is P ≈ J × ω² ÷ t and the average over the stop is half of that, ½Jω² ÷ t (J in kg·m², ω in rad/s, t in s). The kinetic energy to dissipate is ½Jω².

## Alternatives

For long or frequent braking, such as cranes, centrifuges and downhill conveyors, a regenerative (active front-end) drive returns energy to the supply instead of wasting it as heat.

## Worked example with the default values

A 400 V drive whose chopper switches on at 760 V DC, braking 15 kW for 10% of the time:

- Maximum resistance = 760² ÷ 15,000 = 577,600 ÷ 15,000 = **38.5 Ω**
- Average resistor power = 15 × 0.1 = **1.5 kW**
- Peak chopper current = 760 ÷ 38.5 = **19.7 A** (the same as 15,000 ÷ 760)

If you fit a standard **33 Ω** resistor instead, it can absorb up to 760² ÷ 33 = **17.5 kW** at the threshold and draws **23.0 A** through the chopper. That is fine as long as 33 Ω is above the drive's minimum resistance and 23.0 A is within the chopper rating.

## Worked example: braking power from a flywheel

A 5 kg·m² flywheel spinning at 1,500 rpm (ω = 157.08 rad/s) is stopped in 5 s, once every 50 s:

1. Kinetic energy = ½ × 5 × 157.08² = **61.7 kJ** per stop.
2. Peak braking power at the start of the stop = 5 × 157.08² ÷ 5 = **24.7 kW**; average over the stop = **12.3 kW**.
3. Duty cycle = 5 ÷ 50 = **10%**, and the continuous resistor power is 61.7 kJ ÷ 50 s = **1.23 kW**.

Size the resistance from the peak power and the rating from the average power; motor and drive losses absorb a few percent, which adds margin.

## Practical notes

- **Respect both limits.** The resistor must be at or below R<sub>max</sub> to absorb the peak power, and at or above the drive's minimum resistance to protect the chopper transistor.
- **Use the resistor's duty rating.** Catalogue resistors are often rated for a short duty (for example 10% over 120 s). For long stops, size on the energy per stop and check the resistor's thermal time constant.
- **Fit a thermal switch.** Wire the resistor's thermostat or a thermal overload into the drive's external fault input so a stuck chopper cannot overheat it.
- **Mount it safely.** Resistors can exceed 200 °C; place them where airflow is free and away from cable trays, and use cable rated for the full DC bus voltage.
