## How to use the transistor base resistor calculator

Enter the **drive voltage**, the transistor's **V<sub>BE</sub>**, the **load current**, the **minimum hFE** from the datasheet and an **overdrive factor**. You get the base current and the base resistor value — round down to the nearest standard value.

## Base resistor formula

<p class="formula">I<sub>B</sub> = (I<sub>C</sub> ÷ h<sub>FE</sub>) × k &nbsp;·&nbsp; R<sub>B</sub> = (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>BE</sub>) ÷ I<sub>B</sub></p>

## Example

Switching a 100 mA relay from a 5 V microcontroller pin with a BC547-class transistor (hFE ≥ 100), 5× overdrive:

- I<sub>B</sub> = 100 ÷ 100 × 5 = **5 mA**
- R<sub>B</sub> = 4.3 ÷ 0.005 = **860 Ω** → use 820 Ω

## Practical tips

- Check that the microcontroller pin can supply the base current (often 10–20 mA max).
- Add a 10 kΩ resistor from base to emitter to keep the transistor off while the pin floats at power-up.
- Saturation voltage V<sub>CE(sat)</sub> is typically 0.1–0.3 V; power dissipated ≈ I<sub>C</sub> × V<sub>CE(sat)</sub>.

## Worked example with the default inputs

A 5 V pin drives a 100 mA load through an NPN transistor with minimum hFE 100, V<sub>BE</sub> 0.7 V and 5 times overdrive:

- Minimum base current = 100 mA ÷ 100 = 1 mA; with overdrive I<sub>B</sub> = **5 mA**
- R<sub>B</sub> = (5 − 0.7) ÷ 5 mA = **860 Ω**

Rounding down to the standard **820 Ω** gives I<sub>B</sub> = 4.3 ÷ 820 = **5.24 mA**, a forced beta of 100 ÷ 5.24 = **19.1**. The resistor dissipates 4.3 V × 5.24 mA = 22.5 mW, so any 0.125 W part is fine. With V<sub>CE(sat)</sub> ≈ 0.2 V the transistor dissipates about 20 mW.

From a 3.3 V pin the same load needs (3.3 − 0.7) ÷ 5 mA = **520 Ω**, so use 510 Ω. If you add a 10 kΩ base to emitter resistor, it steals 0.7 ÷ 10 kΩ = 0.07 mA; to keep 5 mA into the base the 5 V resistor becomes 848 Ω, which still rounds to 820 Ω.

## Base resistor for common loads (hFE 100, 5 times overdrive)

| Load current | Base current | R<sub>B</sub> from 3.3 V | R<sub>B</sub> from 5 V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 mA | 1 mA | 2,600 Ω | 4,300 Ω |
| 50 mA | 2.5 mA | 1,040 Ω | 1,720 Ω |
| 100 mA | 5 mA | 520 Ω | 860 Ω |
| 200 mA | 10 mA | 260 Ω | 430 Ω |
| 500 mA | 25 mA | 104 Ω | 172 Ω |

At 500 mA the 25 mA base current exceeds most microcontroller pins. Use a Darlington, a two stage driver or a logic level MOSFET for loads that large.

## Checking saturation in the circuit

With the load connected and the pin high, measure collector to emitter with a multimeter. A saturated small signal transistor reads about 0.05 to 0.3 V. A reading of 1 V or more means it is in the active region: increase the base current, or the transistor will heat and the load will see less voltage. Also check the pin voltage under load; if it sags well below the supply, the pin is overloaded.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the typical hFE.** Design with the minimum gain at your collector current, which can be a third of the headline figure.
- **Forgetting V<sub>BE</sub>.** Dividing the full 5 V by the base current gives a resistor about 16% too large.
- **Driving a PNP high side switch from a low voltage pin.** A 3.3 V pin cannot turn off a PNP whose emitter sits at 12 V; add an NPN stage to pull its base.
- **No flyback diode on a relay or solenoid.** The turn off spike can exceed the transistor's V<sub>CEO</sub> and destroy it.
- **Leaving the base floating at reset.** Pins are inputs during power up, so a pull down keeps the load off.
