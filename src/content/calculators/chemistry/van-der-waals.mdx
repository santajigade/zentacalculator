  - q: What is the van der Waals equation?
    a: (P + a n² ÷ V²)(V − n b) = n R T. The a term adds back the pressure lost to attraction between molecules, and the b term subtracts the volume the molecules themselves occupy.
  - q: What do the constants a and b mean?

## How to use the van der Waals calculator

Enter the **amount of gas**, the **temperature**, the **volume** and the gas's **van der Waals constants a and b** (see the table). The calculator returns the real-gas pressure, the ideal-gas pressure for comparison and the compressibility factor Z. Clear the volume and enter a pressure to solve for volume instead.

## Van der Waals equation

<p class="formula">P = n R T ÷ (V − n b) − a n² ÷ V²</p>

R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), T in kelvin. With a in L²·bar/mol² and b in L/mol, use V in litres and R = 0.08314 L·bar/(mol·K) to get P in bar.

## Example

1 mol of CO₂ in 1 L at 25 °C (a = 3.640, b = 0.04267):

- Ideal: P = 0.08314 × 298.15 ÷ 1 = **24.79 bar**
- Van der Waals: P = 0.08314 × 298.15 ÷ (1 − 0.04267) − 3.640 × 1² ÷ 1² = 25.89 − 3.64 = **22.25 bar**
- Z = 22.25 ÷ 24.79 = **0.8977** — attraction lowers the pressure about 10% below ideal.

| Hydrogen (H₂) | 0.2476 | 0.02661 | 25.22 bar | 1.017 |
| Nitrogen (N₂) | 1.37 | 0.0387 | 24.42 bar | 0.985 |
| Oxygen (O₂) | 1.382 | 0.03186 | 24.22 bar | 0.977 |
| Argon (Ar) | 1.355 | 0.03201 | 24.25 bar | 0.978 |
| Methane (CH₄) | 2.283 | 0.04278 | 23.61 bar | 0.953 |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | 3.64 | 0.04267 | 22.25 bar | 0.898 |

The ideal-gas pressure under the same conditions is 24.79 bar. Light gases (He, H₂) have Z above 1 because the excluded volume outweighs the weak attraction.

## What each correction does in the default case

The calculator works in SI: a = 3.64 L²·bar/mol² becomes 0.364 Pa·m⁶/mol², b = 0.04267 L/mol becomes 4.267 × 10⁻⁵ m³/mol, and V = 1 L = 0.001 m³. The two corrections pull in opposite directions:

- Excluded volume alone: n R T ÷ (V − n b) = 25.894 bar, which is 4.5% above ideal because the molecules have only 0.95733 L of free space
- Attraction: a n² ÷ V² = 3.640 bar subtracted from that
- Net: 25.894 − 3.640 = **22.254 bar**, ideal **24.790 bar**, Z = **0.8977**

For CO₂ at this density attraction wins by a wide margin. Doubling the amount to 2 mol in the same litre doubles the excluded volume and quadruples the attraction term, giving 39.64 bar and Z = 0.7996, the same state as 1 mol in 0.5 L in the table below.

Z stays within 1% of ideal down to about 5 L, then falls quickly. CO₂ at 25 °C is below its critical temperature and condenses at about 64 bar, so pushing the volume much below 0.5 L takes the equation into the region where its cubic shape gives loops instead of a real liquid and vapour balance.

## Critical point and Boyle temperature from a and b

The same two constants predict the critical point, above which the gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone: T<sub>c</sub> = 8a ÷ (27 R b), P<sub>c</sub> = a ÷ (27 b²) and V<sub>c</sub> = 3b. For the default CO₂ constants these give T<sub>c</sub> = **304.0 K**, P<sub>c</sub> = **74.0 bar** and V<sub>c</sub> = 0.128 L/mol. The measured values are 304.1 K and 73.8 bar, which agree because tabulated a and b are usually fitted to them, but the real critical volume is 0.094 L/mol, showing the model's weakness at liquid like densities. The Boyle temperature, where Z stays near 1 over a wide range of pressure, is T<sub>B</sub> = a ÷ (R b) = 1026 K in the model against roughly 715 K measured.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing units for a.** Tables give a in L²·atm/mol², L²·bar/mol² or Pa·m⁶/mol². The CO₂ value 3.592 L²·atm/mol² is 3.640 L²·bar/mol²; entering an SI value such as 0.364 here would cut the attraction term tenfold.
- **Treating b as a total volume.** b is per mole, so the excluded volume is n b; with 2 mol in 1 L it is 0.0853 L, not 0.04267 L.
- **Using it inside the two phase region.** Below T<sub>c</sub> at high density the equation can give three volumes for one pressure or even negative pressures; those are not physical states.
- **Expecting design accuracy.** Van der Waals is qualitative near and above the critical density; use Peng Robinson, Soave Redlich Kwong or measured Z charts for engineering.
- **Using pure gas constants for a mixture.** Combine them with mixing rules, for example a<sub>mix</sub> = (Σ y<sub>i</sub>√a<sub>i</sub>)² and b<sub>mix</sub> = Σ y<sub>i</sub> b<sub>i</sub>; for 79% N₂ and 21% O₂ this gives a = 1.3725 and b = 0.03726.

## Notes

- The van der Waals equation is a teaching model; for accurate design work use Peng–Robinson or tabulated Z values.
