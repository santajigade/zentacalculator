## How to use the flow meter K-factor calculator

Enter the meter's **K-factor** (pulses per litre) and either the **flow rate** or the measured **pulse frequency**. The other is calculated. Use it to set up a PLC counter, check a meter on the bench, or choose a frequency input range.

For more background, read [Turndown Ratio in Flow Meters](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/turndown-ratio-in-flow-meters/).

## Flow meter K-factor formula

<p class="formula">f (Hz) = K (pulses/L) × Q (L/s)   Q = f ÷ K</p>

## Worked example

A small turbine meter with K = 450 pulses/L at 6 L/min:

- Q = 6 ÷ 60 = 0.1 L/s
- f = 450 × 0.1 = **45 Hz**

At the maximum flow of 20 L/min the output reaches 150 Hz — check the PLC input can count that fast.

## Totalising

Volume = pulse count ÷ K-factor. 9,000 pulses ÷ 450 = **20 litres**.

## K-factor examples

| Meter type | Typical K-factor |
|---|---|
| Small paddle-wheel sensor (DN15) | 300–500 pulses/L |
| Turbine meter, DN25 | 50–150 pulses/L |
| Turbine meter, DN100 | 1–5 pulses/L |
| Positive displacement meter | 1–100 pulses/L |
| Mag meter pulse output | Configurable, e.g. 1 pulse per 10 L |

Always use the calibrated K-factor printed on the meter certificate.

## Linearity and the K-factor curve

A turbine meter's K-factor is not perfectly constant; it varies with flow rate and viscosity. Calibration certificates often give K at several flow points. Many flow computers apply a **linearisation table** to correct for this.

## Choosing the pulse output

Make sure the maximum pulse frequency is within the counter input's limit, and the minimum gives enough resolution:

- At maximum flow, frequency should be below the input's maximum (for example 10 kHz).
- At minimum flow, at least a few pulses per second gives a stable rate display.

## Tips

- Use the K-factor from the **calibration certificate**, not the nominal catalogue value.
- K-factors drift slightly with viscosity and at very low flow. Many meters list a linear range where the K-factor holds within ±0.5%.
- For long cable runs, use shielded cable and check the pulse output type (NPN, PNP, Namur or reed).

## Second example: checking K with a test volume

The default case gives **45 Hz** at 6 L/min with K = 450 pulses/L. On site you rarely trust the nameplate alone. Run the meter into a calibrated 50 L vessel and count the pulses: say the counter shows **22,610** pulses.

- Measured K = 22,610 ÷ 50 = **452.2 pulses/L**
- With the nominal 450 pulses/L the PLC would have shown 50.24 L, which is 0.49% high
- Entering 452.2 in the scaling (or a meter factor of 0.9951) removes that error

For a 200 L batch the counter preset is 200 × 452.2 = **90,440 pulses**, not the 90,000 pulses the nominal K gives.

A meter rated in US gallons works the same way: K = 1,700 pulses/gal at 25 gpm gives 708.3 Hz, and the same K is 449.1 pulses/L.

## How the pulses are counted in practice

A PLC can only count pulses in its normal digital input logic if each pulse lasts longer than one scan. At the 20 L/min maximum of this meter the frequency is 150 Hz, a period of 6.7 ms, so a 10 ms scan would miss pulses. Wire the meter to a high speed counter input and read the accumulated count instead. Also check the input filter: many digital inputs have a default 3 to 10 ms debounce that silently drops fast pulses. Reed switch outputs on water meters are the opposite case and need debounce, because contact bounce creates extra counts. For the meter itself, [how a turbine flow meter works](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/turbine-flow-meter-working-principle/) explains why the rotor speed and therefore the pulse rate is proportional to velocity.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing pulses per gallon with pulses per litre.** The error is a factor of 3.785, which is too big to miss on a total but easy to miss on a rate display nobody checks.
- **Reading the rate in a normal scan.** Missed pulses make the meter read low only at high flow, which looks like a meter fault.
- **Calculating rate from too short a gate time.** At 1 pulse per litre and 10 L/min there is one pulse every 6 s, so a 1 s rate window jumps between 0 and 60 L/min. Measure the time between pulses instead.
- **Keeping the water K-factor for a viscous liquid.** Turbine meters slow down in oil, so K can shift by several percent; calibrate on the real product.
- **Ignoring flow below the linear range.** Under the minimum rated flow, bearing friction makes K fall sharply and the totaliser under-registers.
