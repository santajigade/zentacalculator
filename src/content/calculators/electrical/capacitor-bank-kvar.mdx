## How to use the capacitor bank kVAr calculator

Enter the bank's **kVAr rating**, the **line voltage** it is connected to, the **frequency** and whether the capacitors are connected in **delta** or **star**. The calculator returns the capacitance needed in each phase, the line current and the reactance of each capacitor. To work out the kVAr of an existing bank, enter the measured µF per phase and clear the kVAr field.

## kVAr to µF formulas

<p class="formula">Delta: C = Q ÷ (3 × 2πf × V<sub>L</sub>²) &nbsp;·&nbsp; Star: C = Q ÷ (2πf × V<sub>L</sub>²)</p>

<p class="formula">I<sub>L</sub> = Q ÷ (√3 × V<sub>L</sub>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; X<sub>C</sub> = 1 ÷ (2πf C)</p>

For a single-phase capacitor, C = Q ÷ (2πf × V²) and I = Q ÷ V.

## Example

A 50 kVAr delta-connected bank on a 415 V, 50 Hz supply:

- C = 50,000 ÷ (3 × 314.16 × 415²) = **308.0 µF** per phase (a star bank would need 924.1 µF)
- I = 50,000 ÷ (1.732 × 415) = **69.6 A**
- X<sub>C</sub> = 1 ÷ (314.16 × 308.0 µF) = **10.33 Ω**

## Practical notes

- Size cables and switchgear for about 1.3–1.5 × rated current to allow for harmonics and capacitance tolerance.
- Where the [THD](/electrical/thd/) is high, use detuned (reactor) banks to avoid resonance.
- Find the kVAr you need from the load and power factors with the [power factor correction calculator](/electrical/power-factor-correction/).

## What the default bank does to a real load

Connect the default 50 kVAr, 415 V delta bank to a 200 kW load running at 0.80 power factor:

- Load reactive power = 200 × tan(cos⁻¹ 0.80) = **150.0 kVAr**
- After correction: 150.0 − 50 = 100.0 kVAr, so apparent power falls from 250.0 kVA to **223.6 kVA**
- Power factor rises from 0.80 to **0.894**
- Supply current drops from 347.8 A to **311.1 A**, freeing about 37 A of cable and transformer capacity

## Testing a bank in the field

A healthy bank draws close to its calculated current, so a clamp meter reading near 69.6 A on each line at 415 V is the quickest check. Readings that are unequal by more than about 10% point to a failed element in one phase.

With the bank isolated and discharged, a capacitance meter across two terminals of a three-terminal delta unit does not read C. It reads one capacitor in parallel with the other two in series, so the expected value is 1.5 × 308.0 = **462 µF** for each pair. Compare all three pairs; they should agree within a few percent.

IEC 60831-1 requires each unit to discharge to 75 V or less within 3 minutes. From a peak of 587 V, that needs a discharge resistance no greater than 180 ÷ (308 µF × ln(587 ÷ 75)) = **284 kΩ** per phase. Always wait the full time and short the terminals before touching them.

On 60 Hz systems the same kVAr needs 83% of the capacitance it would need at 50 Hz, so never fit a 50 Hz replacement value into a 60 Hz bank without recalculating.

## Detuned banks

Where drives create harmonics, each step gets a series reactor. A 7% reactor tunes the circuit to 50 ÷ √0.07 = **189 Hz**, below the 5th harmonic, but it lifts the voltage across the capacitor to 415 ÷ (1 − 0.07) = 446 V. That is why detuned banks on 415 V systems use capacitors rated 480 V or more.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the star formula for a delta bank.** It gives three times the capacitance needed, and the bank would deliver three times the kVAr.
- **Entering phase voltage instead of line voltage.** The formulas take V<sub>L</sub>; using 240 V instead of 415 V inflates the result by a factor of three.
- **Measuring a delta unit pair and calling it C.** The meter shows 1.5 C, so a good 308 µF phase can look like an oversized one.
- **Over-compensating a motor at its terminals.** Fixed capacitors switched with a motor should not exceed about 90% of its no-load reactive power, or the motor can self-excite after disconnection.
- **Ignoring the rated voltage on the nameplate.** A unit marked 440 V gives less than its nameplate kVAr on 415 V, so read the output at the actual supply.
