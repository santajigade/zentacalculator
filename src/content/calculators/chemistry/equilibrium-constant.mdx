## How to use the equilibrium constant calculator

Enter the **equilibrium concentrations** and **coefficients** for reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD. For a reaction with fewer species, set the unused coefficient to 0. Enter the **temperature** to convert Kc to Kp — the conversion assumes every species counted in Δn is a gas; set the coefficient of any solid or liquid to 0.

## Equilibrium constant formulas

<p class="formula">K<sub>c</sub> = [C]<sup>c</sup>[D]<sup>d</sup> / ([A]<sup>a</sup>[B]<sup>b</sup>)</p>

<p class="formula">K<sub>p</sub> = K<sub>c</sub>(RT)<sup>Δn</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; Δn = (c + d) − (a + b), R = 0.0820574 L·atm/(mol·K)</p>

## Worked example: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃

Illustrative equilibrium mixture at 500 °C: [N₂] = 0.50 M, [H₂] = 1.50 M, [NH₃] = 0.10 M:

- 1.50³ = 3.3750, so the denominator is 0.50 × 3.3750 = 1.6875
- K<sub>c</sub> = 0.10² ÷ 1.6875 = 0.0100 ÷ 1.6875 = **0.005926**
- Δn = 2 − (1 + 3) = **-2**
- T = 500 + 273.15 = 773.15 K, so RT = 0.0820574 × 773.15 = 63.443 L·atm/mol
- K<sub>p</sub> = 0.005926 × 63.443<sup>−2</sup> = 0.005926 ÷ 4,025.0 = **1.472 × 10⁻⁶**

K<sub>p</sub> is far smaller than K<sub>c</sub> because Δn is negative: four moles of gas become two, so each pressure term carries an extra factor of RT.

## (RT)<sup>Δn</sup> conversion factors

| Temperature | RT (L·atm/mol) | Δn = −2 | Δn = −1 | Δn = +1 | Δn = +2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 298.15 K | 24.465 | 0.001671 | 0.04087 | 24.47 | 598.6 |
| 500 K | 41.029 | 0.0005941 | 0.02437 | 41.03 | 1683 |
| 773.15 K | 63.443 | 0.0002484 | 0.01576 | 63.44 | 4025 |
| 1000 K | 82.057 | 0.0001485 | 0.01219 | 82.06 | 6733 |

## Checking the direction with the reaction quotient

The reaction quotient Q uses the same expression as K<sub>c</sub> but with the concentrations you have right now. Keep [N₂] = 0.50 M and [H₂] = 1.50 M from the example:

- If [NH₃] is 0.20 M, Q = 0.20² ÷ (0.50 × 1.50³) = **0.02370**. Q is larger than K<sub>c</sub> = 0.005926, so ammonia decomposes until the ratio falls back to K.
- If [NH₃] is 0.05 M, Q = 0.05² ÷ 1.6875 = **0.001481**. Q is smaller than K<sub>c</sub>, so more ammonia forms.

## Finding equilibrium concentrations with an ICE table

Going the other way, from K to concentrations, needs an initial, change, equilibrium (ICE) table. For H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI, K<sub>c</sub> is about 54.3 at 430 °C. Start with 0.500 M of each reactant and let x react:

| | H₂ | I₂ | HI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial (M) | 0.500 | 0.500 | 0 |
| Change (M) | −x | −x | +2x |
| Equilibrium (M) | 0.500 − x | 0.500 − x | 2x |

K<sub>c</sub> = (2x)² ÷ (0.500 − x)². Taking the square root of both sides gives 2x ÷ (0.500 − x) = √54.3 = 7.3689, so x = **0.3933 M**. At equilibrium [H₂] = [I₂] = **0.1067 M** and [HI] = **0.7865 M**, which is 78.7% conversion. Δn = 0 here, so K<sub>p</sub> equals K<sub>c</sub> and pressure has no effect on the position of equilibrium. You can confirm the result by entering these three concentrations with a = b = 1, c = 2, d = 0.

## How K depends on the way the equation is written

K belongs to a particular balanced equation. Using the default mixture at 500 °C (773.15 K):

| Equation | K<sub>c</sub> | Δn | K<sub>p</sub> (atm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (as written) | 5.9259 × 10⁻³ | -2 | 1.4723 × 10⁻⁶ |
| 2NH₃ ⇌ N₂ + 3H₂ (reversed) | 1.6875 × 10² | 2 | 6.7921 × 10⁵ |
| ½N₂ + 3⁄2H₂ ⇌ NH₃ (halved) | 7.6980 × 10⁻² | -1 | 1.2134 × 10⁻³ |
| 2N₂ + 6H₂ ⇌ 4NH₃ (doubled) | 3.5117 × 10⁻⁵ | -4 | 2.1676 × 10⁻¹² |

Reversing the equation inverts K, halving every coefficient takes the square root, and doubling squares it. Quote the equation alongside any K value. With pressures in bar instead of atm, the as written K<sub>p</sub> becomes 1.4340 × 10⁻⁶, because R changes to 0.0831446 L·bar/(mol·K).

## How equilibrium concentrations are measured

Equilibrium concentrations are rarely weighed out; they are measured in a mixture that has stopped changing. Coloured species are followed by spectrophotometry and the Beer-Lambert law, the classic teaching case being the blood red FeSCN²⁺ ion. Acid and base equilibria are followed with a pH electrode. Gas phase equilibria such as ammonia synthesis are sampled at temperature and analysed by gas chromatography, or worked out from the change in total pressure at constant volume. A sample must be measured quickly or quenched by cooling, otherwise the equilibrium shifts as conditions change.

## Common mistakes

- **Including pure solids and liquids.** Solid carbon or liquid water as a solvent have no concentration term; set their coefficients to 0 so they also drop out of Δn.
- **Using initial instead of equilibrium concentrations.** Values before the reaction has settled give Q, not K.
- **Putting °C into RT.** The conversion needs kelvin; 500 °C is 773.15 K, and RT is then 63.443 L·atm/mol.
- **Using R = 8.314.** That value is in J/(mol·K) and gives a meaningless K<sub>p</sub>; with concentrations in mol/L and pressures in atm, R must be 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).
- **Forgetting that K changes with temperature.** Ammonia synthesis is exothermic, so its K falls as temperature rises; a K measured at 500 °C cannot be used at 400 °C.

## Notes

- With pressures in bar use R = 0.0831446 L·bar/(mol·K).
- The reaction quotient Q uses the same expression with non-equilibrium values: if Q < K the reaction moves forward, if Q > K it moves back.
- K is linked to the standard Gibbs energy by ΔG° = −RT ln K — see the [Gibbs free energy calculator](/chemistry/gibbs-free-energy/).
