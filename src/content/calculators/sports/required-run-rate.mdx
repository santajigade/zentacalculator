## How to use the required run rate calculator

Enter the **target**, the **current score** and the **balls remaining**. Runs needed, required run rate and runs per ball are calculated.

## Required run rate formula

<p class="formula">RRR = (target − score) ÷ (balls remaining ÷ 6)</p>

## Example

Chasing 181, the team is 92 with 9 overs (54 balls) left:

- Need **89** runs
- Required rate = 89 ÷ 9 = **9.89** per over

## Chasing in T20

A required rate above about 12 an over is very hard to sustain for long, though modern T20 batting has made rates of 10–12 more achievable, especially with wickets in hand.

## Wickets in hand matter

Run rate alone ignores wickets. 60 off 30 balls with 8 wickets left is a very different chase from 60 off 30 with 2 wickets left. Win-probability models combine both.

## Rain and DLS

In rain-affected matches, the Duckworth–Lewis–Stern method sets a revised target based on resources (overs and wickets) remaining. The required run rate is then calculated against the revised target.

## How the asking rate climbs

The defaults (target 181, score 92, 54 balls left) give **89 runs needed at 9.89 per over**, or 1.65 per ball. The next few overs decide whether that stays realistic. Here is the asking rate after three more overs at different scoring speeds:

| Next 3 overs at | Runs still needed | Overs left | New required rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 per over | 71 | 6 | 11.83 |
| 8 per over | 65 | 6 | 10.83 |
| 10 per over | 59 | 6 | 9.83 |
| 12 per over | 53 | 6 | 8.83 |

Scoring at 6 an over for three overs pushes the target above 11 an over, while matching the asking rate keeps it level. This is why chasing sides try never to let the gap between current and required rate grow.

## Boundaries versus strike rotation

Boundaries take a big share of the load. If the batters hit a number of fours in the remaining 54 balls, the other balls must supply the rest:

| Boundaries hit | Runs from other balls | Other balls | Runs per other ball |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 fours (24 runs) | 65 | 48 | 1.35 |
| 9 fours (36 runs) | 53 | 45 | 1.18 |
| 12 fours (48 runs) | 41 | 42 | 0.98 |

Even with 12 fours, the remaining balls still need almost a run each, so dot balls are as costly as missed boundaries.

## Overs notation and balls remaining

Scorecards write overs as overs and balls, so 12.4 overs means 12 overs and 4 balls, which is 76 balls, not 12.4 × 6. In a 20 over chase that leaves 44 balls. In a one-day match a side on 150 after 30 overs chasing 300 needs 150 off 120 balls, an asking rate of **7.50**. Always enter balls remaining rather than a decimal overs figure.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating 12.4 overs as a decimal.** Four balls is two thirds of an over, not 0.4 of one.
- **Using the opposition total as the target.** The target is their total plus one; their total only ties the match.
- **Counting wides and no-balls as balls used.** Each gives a run and the ball is bowled again, so balls remaining does not fall.
- **Ignoring a revised target.** After a rain break, use the DLS target and the reduced overs, not the original figures.
- **Reading the rate without wickets.** 9.89 an over with eight wickets left is a fair chase; with two left it is a long shot.
