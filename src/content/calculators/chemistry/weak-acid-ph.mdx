## How to use the weak acid pH calculator

Choose **weak acid** or **weak base**, enter its **Ka** or **Kb** and the **initial concentration** in mol/L. The calculator returns the equilibrium [H⁺] (or [OH⁻] for a base), the pH and the percent dissociated. If you only know the pKa, convert it first with the [pKa calculator](/chemistry/pka/).

## Weak acid pH formula

<p class="formula">Ka = x² ÷ (C − x) &nbsp;⇒&nbsp; x = (−Ka + √(Ka² + 4 Ka C)) ÷ 2</p>

<p class="formula">Acid: pH = −log₁₀ x &nbsp;·&nbsp; Base: pH = 14 + log₁₀ x &nbsp;·&nbsp; % dissociated = x ÷ C × 100</p>

Water's own ionisation is ignored, which is accurate unless the solution is extremely dilute (below about 10⁻⁶ mol/L) or the acid extremely weak.

## Examples

0.1 mol/L acetic acid (Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵):

- x = (−1.8 × 10⁻⁵ + √((1.8 × 10⁻⁵)² + 4 × 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.1)) ÷ 2 = **1.3327 × 10⁻³ mol/L**
- pH = −log₁₀(1.3327 × 10⁻³) = **2.875**
- Percent dissociated = **1.333%**

0.1 mol/L ammonia (Kb = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵): [OH⁻] = 1.3327 × 10⁻³ mol/L, pH = 14 + log₁₀(x) = **11.125**.

## Checking the default answer

Put the result back into the equilibrium expression: Ka = x² ÷ (C − x) = (1.3327 × 10⁻³)² ÷ (0.1 − 0.0013327) = 1.800 × 10⁻⁵, which returns the Ka you entered. The shortcut x = √(Ka C) = √(1.8 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.3416 × 10⁻³ mol/L gives pH 2.872. It overestimates [H⁺] by 0.7% and moves the pH by only 0.003, because acetic acid at 0.1 M is barely dissociated.

## Finding Ka from a measured pH

In the lab Ka is usually found the other way round. Suppose a calibrated pH meter reads **3.40** in a 0.050 mol/L solution of an unknown weak acid. Then [H⁺] = 10<sup>−3.40</sup> = 3.981 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L, and Ka = x² ÷ (C − x) = **3.20 × 10⁻⁶**, so pKa = 5.50. Calibrate the meter with two buffers that bracket the reading, such as pH 4.01 and 7.00, and measure at the temperature the Ka refers to. A pH electrode responds to hydrogen ion activity, so in concentrated or salty solutions the apparent Ka differs slightly from the tabulated thermodynamic value.

## The pH of a salt such as sodium acetate

The conjugate base of a weak acid is itself a weak base, with Kb = Kw ÷ Ka. For acetate, Kb = 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ (1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 5.556 × 10⁻¹⁰. Choose weak base, enter that Kb and 0.1 mol/L, and the calculator gives [OH⁻] = 7.453 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L and **pH 8.87**. That is why a sodium acetate solution is mildly alkaline, and why the endpoint of an acetic acid titration with sodium hydroxide sits above pH 7.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering pKa instead of Ka.** A pKa of 4.74 typed into the Ka box describes an absurdly strong acid; convert first with Ka = 10<sup>−pKa</sup>.
- **Using the conjugate's constant.** Ammonia has Kb = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, but the ammonium ion is an acid with pKa 9.26; pick the constant that matches the species you dissolved.
- **Treating a polyprotic acid as one step.** For phosphoric or carbonic acid the first dissociation sets the pH, but the second and third steps matter for speciation and buffering.
- **Applying it after adding base.** Once part of the acid has been neutralised the solution is a buffer, and the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is the right tool.
- **Assuming pKw = 14 at every temperature.** At 50 °C pKw is about 13.26, so the weak base result from 14 + log x drifts by more than half a unit.

## Notes

- pH 14 + log x assumes 25 °C; at other temperatures use the local pK<sub>w</sub>.
- For a mixture of a weak acid and its salt (a buffer), use the [Henderson–Hasselbalch calculator](/chemistry/henderson-hasselbalch/) instead.
- Strong acids are fully dissociated — use the [pH calculator](/chemistry/ph/) with [H⁺] = C.
