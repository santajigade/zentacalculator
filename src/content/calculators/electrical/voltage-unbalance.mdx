## How to use the voltage unbalance calculator

Measure the three **line-to-line voltages** with a true-RMS meter and enter them. You get the average, the maximum deviation, the percentage unbalance and the approximate increase in motor winding temperature rise.

For more background, read [Motor Protection Relay Types](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/motor-protection-relay-types/) and [Power Quality Issues with VFDs and PLCs](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/power-quality-issues-vfd-plc/).

## The formula (NEMA MG 1)

<p class="formula">% unbalance = 100 × max. deviation from average ÷ average voltage</p>

Extra temperature rise ≈ 2 × (% unbalance)² percent.

## Worked example

A 30 kW motor terminal box reads 412 V (L1 to L2), 405 V (L2 to L3) and 418 V (L3 to L1):

- Average = (412 + 405 + 418) ÷ 3 = **411.7 V**
- Deviations: +0.33 V, −6.67 V and +6.33 V, so the largest is **6.67 V** (L2 to L3)
- Unbalance = 6.67 ÷ 411.7 × 100 = **1.62%**
- Extra winding temperature rise ≈ 2 × 1.62² = **5.2%**
- Derating factor read from the curve between 1% and 2% ≈ 0.97, so the motor should carry no more than about 29.1 kW
- The IEC definition (negative to positive sequence ratio) gives 1.82% for the same three voltages, a little higher than the NEMA figure because the two definitions differ
- Expect a current unbalance of roughly 10% to 16%, which is enough to worry a motor protection relay set at 10%

## Motor derating (NEMA MG 1, approximate)

| Voltage unbalance | Derating factor |
|---|---|
| 0–1% | 1.00 |
| 2% | 0.95 |
| 3% | 0.88 |
| 4% | 0.82 |
| 5% | 0.75 |

Do not operate motors above 5% unbalance.

## Current unbalance

A small voltage unbalance causes a much larger current unbalance — typically 6 to 10 times larger. A 2% voltage unbalance can produce 15–20% current unbalance, which may trip motor protection relays set for phase unbalance.

## Other definitions

IEC 61000 uses the ratio of negative-sequence to positive-sequence voltage. For small unbalances it gives results close to the NEMA method above.

## Unbalance, heating and current

The temperature figure is the increase in winding temperature rise (2 × unbalance²), and the current column uses the usual rule of thumb that current unbalance is 6 to 10 times the voltage unbalance:

## How to measure voltage unbalance

- Measure **line-to-line** voltages at the motor terminals or at the starter, with the motor running at normal load. Unbalance at no load can look better than it is.
- Use one true-RMS meter for all three readings, so meter error does not show up as unbalance. A power quality analyser that logs over a day catches unbalance that comes and goes with single-phase loads.
- Compare the result with the **current unbalance** on a clamp meter. If the currents are far more unbalanced than 6 to 10 times the voltage figure, suspect the motor (a shorted turn or a high-resistance joint) rather than the supply.
- Rotate the supply leads one position (L1 to L2, L2 to L3, L3 to L1) and measure the currents again. If the high current follows the supply phase, the supply is the cause; if it stays on the same motor lead, the motor or its cable is.

## Common mistakes

- **Using line-to-neutral voltages.** NEMA MG 1 defines unbalance on line-to-line voltages. Phase voltages include zero-sequence components and give a different answer.
- **Dividing by the lowest or rated voltage.** The denominator is the average of the three measured voltages, not 400 V or 415 V nameplate.
- **Measuring at the main board only.** Long feeders and single-phase loads downstream change the balance; measure where the motor is connected.
- **Ignoring low unbalance.** At 3% the winding runs about 18% hotter in temperature rise, which shortens insulation life well before any trip.
- **Treating the 6 to 10 times rule as exact.** Current unbalance depends on motor design and load; use it to judge, and measure the real currents.
