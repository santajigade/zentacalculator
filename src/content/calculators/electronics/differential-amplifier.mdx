## How to use the differential amplifier calculator

Enter the two input voltages and the four resistors. **R₁** and **R₂** set the inverting gain; **R₃** and **R₄** form the divider on the non-inverting input. The calculator returns the output, the differential gain and the common-mode gain — try changing R₄ by 1% to see how mismatch creates common-mode error.

## Differential amplifier formula

<p class="formula">V<sub>out</sub> = V₂ · R₄/(R₃ + R₄) · (1 + R₂/R₁) − V₁ · R₂/R₁</p>

<p class="formula">Matched (R₁ = R₃, R₂ = R₄): V<sub>out</sub> = (R₂/R₁)(V₂ − V₁)</p>

Writing V<sub>out</sub> = A<sub>d</sub>(V₂ − V₁) + A<sub>cm</sub>(V₁ + V₂)/2 separates the wanted differential gain from the unwanted common-mode gain.

## Worked example

V₁ = 1 V, V₂ = 1.2 V, R₁ = R₃ = 10 kΩ, R₂ = R₄ = 100 kΩ:

- V<sub>out</sub> = 1.2 × (100/110) × 11 − 1 × 10 = **2.00 V**
- Differential gain = **10**, common-mode gain = **0**

## Worst-case CMRR from resistor tolerance

| Gain | 5% | 1% | 0.1% | 0.01% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 dB | 34 dB | 54 dB | 74 dB |
| 10 | 35 dB | 49 dB | 69 dB | 89 dB |
| 100 | 54 dB | 68 dB | 88 dB | 108 dB |

Values from CMRR ≈ (1 + G)/(4t); the op-amp's own CMRR sets a further limit.

## Notes

- The input impedance is R₁ at the V₁ input and R₃ + R₄ at the V₂ input — drive it from low-impedance sources.
- For a reference offset, connect the bottom of R₄ to a reference voltage instead of ground; it adds directly to the output.

## Second example: one resistor 1% high

Keep the default inputs but let R₄ be 101 kΩ instead of 100 kΩ:

- R₄ ÷ (R₃ + R₄) × (1 + R₂/R₁) = 101/111 × 11 = 10.0090
- V<sub>out</sub> = 10.0090 × 1.2 − 10 × 1 = **2.0108 V** instead of 2.0000 V
- A<sub>cm</sub> = 10.0090 − 10 = **0.00901**, A<sub>d</sub> = **10.0045**
- CMRR = 20 log(10.0045 ÷ 0.00901) = **60.9 dB**

That is better than the 49 dB worst case in the table above because only one resistor is off; the table assumes all four sit at the edge of tolerance in the worst direction.

## Why mismatch matters on a high-side shunt

Now put the same 0.2 V difference on top of a 12 V rail (V₁ = 12 V, V₂ = 12.2 V). With matched resistors the output is still 2.0 V, but with R₄ 1% high it becomes 2.110 V, an error of 110 mV, or 5.5% of reading. The error scales with common-mode voltage:

| R₄ error | Common-mode gain | CMRR | Output error at 12 V common mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01% | 0.00009 | 100.8 dB | 1.1 mV |
| 0.1% | 0.00091 | 80.8 dB | 10.9 mV |
| 0.5% | 0.00452 | 66.9 dB | 54.3 mV |
| 1% | 0.00901 | 60.9 dB | 108.1 mV |
| 5% | 0.04348 | 47.3 dB | 521.7 mV |

## Checking the op-amp input range

With the default values the non-inverting input sits at 1.2 × 100/110 = **1.091 V**, and the inverting input follows it. On a 12 V rail the inputs sit near 11.09 V, so the op-amp needs a supply above that or a different resistor ratio. To measure CMRR on the bench, tie both inputs together, drive them with a known voltage, and divide the output change by the input change.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring source resistance.** A 100 Ω source on the V₁ side makes R₁ effectively 10.1 kΩ, giving A<sub>cm</sub> = 0.0090 even with perfect resistors.
- **Driving the reference pin from a divider.** The bottom of R₄ must see a low impedance, otherwise the divider resistance adds to R₄ and unbalances the ratio; buffer it.
- **Swapping V₁ and V₂.** The output polarity reverses, and on a single supply the op-amp simply sits at the negative rail.
- **Forgetting output swing.** A gain of 10 on a 0.5 V difference needs 5 V of output, which a rail-to-rail op-amp on 5 V cannot quite reach.
- **Mixing tolerances in one circuit.** One 5% resistor among 0.1% parts sets the CMRR on its own.
