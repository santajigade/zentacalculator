## How to use the boiling point elevation calculator

Choose the **solvent**, enter the **van 't Hoff factor**, the **molality** and the solvent's normal boiling point. The calculator gives the rise in boiling point and the new boiling point.

## Boiling point elevation formula

<p class="formula">ΔT<sub>b</sub> = i × K<sub>b</sub> × m</p>

Even very salty water boils only a degree or two above 100 °C.

Pressure cookers raise the pressure so water boils at about 120 °C, speeding up cooking.

## Molar mass from boiling point

Ebullioscopy works like cryoscopy: m = ΔTb ÷ (i × Kb), then molar mass = mass of solute ÷ (m × kg of solvent). Freezing point depression is usually preferred because the effect is larger.

## Colligative properties

Freezing point depression, boiling point elevation, vapour pressure lowering and [osmotic pressure](/chemistry/osmotic-pressure/) all depend on the number of dissolved particles, not their identity.

## Worked example with the default values

A 1 mol/kg solution of sodium chloride in water (i = 2, K<sub>b</sub> = 0.512 °C·kg/mol):

- ΔT<sub>b</sub> = 2 × 0.512 × 1 = **1.024 °C**
- New boiling point = 100 + 1.024 = **101.02 °C**

To make this solution, dissolve 29.22 g of NaCl (58.44 g/mol) in 500 g of water. Note that molality is per kilogram of solvent, so the water is weighed, not made up to a volume.

## Where Kb comes from

The ebullioscopic constant follows from the solvent's own properties: K<sub>b</sub> = R × T<sub>b</sub>² × M ÷ ΔH<sub>vap</sub>. For water, 8.314 × 373.15² × 0.018015 ÷ 40,660 = **0.513 °C·kg/mol**, which matches the tabulated 0.512. Solvents with a high boiling point and a low heat of vaporisation per mole have large K<sub>b</sub> values.

## Ideal and measured van 't Hoff factors

| Solute | Ideal i | Measured i (dilute, about 0.05 mol/kg) |
|---|---|---|
| Sucrose, glucose, urea | 1 | 1.0 |
| NaCl, KCl | 2 | about 1.9 |
| MgSO₄ | 2 | about 1.3 |
| CaCl₂, MgCl₂ | 3 | about 2.7 |
| FeCl₃ | 4 | about 3.4 |

Ion pairing makes real electrolytes behave as if they release fewer particles, so the ideal i overstates the rise, especially above about 0.1 mol/kg.

## Finding molar mass by ebullioscopy

10 g of an unknown non-electrolyte in 200 g of benzene raises the boiling point by 0.80 °C. The molality is 0.80 ÷ 2.53 = 0.3162 mol/kg, so the moles dissolved are 0.3162 × 0.2 = 0.06324 mol and the molar mass is 10 ÷ 0.06324 = **158 g/mol**. Measure the rise with a Beckmann or digital thermometer reading to 0.01 °C, and record barometric pressure, because a small pressure change moves the boiling point more than the solute does.

## Common mistakes

- **Using molarity instead of molality.** K<sub>b</sub> is defined per kilogram of solvent; mol/L gives a small error in water and a larger one in dense or light solvents.
- **Dividing by the mass of solution.** Only the solvent mass goes in the denominator of molality.
- **Forgetting the van 't Hoff factor.** Salts split into ions; using i = 1 for NaCl halves the predicted rise.
- **Applying the formula to concentrated solutions.** The linear law is for dilute, ideal solutions; strong brines deviate noticeably.
- **Ignoring a volatile solute.** The formula assumes the solute does not evaporate; ethanol in water lowers, not raises, the boiling point.
