## How to use the car carbon footprint calculator

Choose the **fuel**, then enter your **yearly distance** and **fuel consumption**. Fuel used, total CO₂ and grams of CO₂ per km are calculated.

## Car carbon footprint formulas

<p class="formula">Fuel = distance × consumption ÷ 100<br/>CO₂ = fuel × emission factor</p>

## Example

12,000 km a year at 7 L/100 km (petrol):

- Fuel = **840 L**
- CO₂ = 840 × 2.31 = **1.94 t**
- About **162 g CO₂/km**

Factors are for fuel combustion only; the full life cycle (fuel production, car manufacture) adds more.

## CO₂ per litre of fuel

These come from the carbon content of the fuel. Including production and transport of the fuel (well-to-wheel) adds roughly 15–25%.

## Ways to cut car emissions

- **Drive smoothly**: gentle acceleration and braking can save 10–20% fuel.
- **Check tyre pressure**: under-inflated tyres raise consumption.
- **Remove roof racks and extra weight** when not needed.
- **Combine trips** and avoid short cold-engine journeys.
- **Choose a more efficient car** or an EV for your next purchase — compare with the [EV vs petrol calculator](/ecology/ev-vs-petrol/).

## Putting it in context

A typical petrol car driven 12,000 km a year emits about 2 tonnes of CO₂. Global average emissions are roughly 4–5 tonnes per person per year from all sources.

## Worked example with the default values

The defaults are a petrol car using 7 L/100 km over 12,000 km a year:

- Fuel used = 12,000 × 7 ÷ 100 = **840 L**
- CO₂ = 840 × 2.31 = 1,940.4 kg = **1.94 t**
- CO₂ per km = 7 × 2.31 × 10 = **162 g/km**

Over a 15-year life at the same use, that car emits about 29 tonnes from the tailpipe. Adding roughly 20% for producing and delivering the fuel raises one year to about 2.33 t.

## Where the emission factor comes from

The factor is set by chemistry. Petrol has a density of about 0.745 kg/L and is about 86% carbon by mass; each kilogram of carbon becomes 44.01 ÷ 12.011 = 3.664 kg of CO₂. So one litre gives 0.745 × 0.86 × 3.664 = **2.35 kg**, close to the 2.31 used here (blends with ethanol come in a little lower). Diesel is denser, 0.835 kg/L at about 86.5% carbon, giving 2.65 kg/L. That is why a diesel can burn less fuel yet emit a similar mass of CO₂.

## Measuring your own fuel consumption

Dashboard figures are often 5% to 10% optimistic, and official WLTP or ARAI ratings are measured on a test cycle without traffic, air conditioning or roof loads. The brim-to-brim method gives a real number: fill the tank until the pump clicks off, reset the trip meter, drive normally, then fill again at the same pump. If you drove 642 km and the second fill took 41.3 L, consumption is 41.3 ÷ 642 × 100 = **6.43 L/100 km**. Repeat over two or three tanks and average them.

If your figures are in miles per gallon, convert first: L/100 km = 235.2 ÷ US mpg, or 282.5 ÷ UK mpg. The default 7 L/100 km is 33.6 US mpg or 40.4 UK mpg.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering km per litre as L/100 km.** Indian buyers often think in km/L. 15 km/L is 100 ÷ 15 = 6.67 L/100 km, not 15.
- **Using the official rating instead of real use.** Short trips, idling in traffic and air conditioning can push real consumption 20% to 40% above the brochure figure.
- **Mixing US and UK mpg.** A UK mpg figure is about 20% higher than the US figure for the same car, because the imperial gallon is larger.
- **Counting CNG or electricity as zero.** CNG is not in the list above but still emits about 2.7 kg CO₂ per kg of gas, and an EV's footprint depends on the grid that charges it.
- **Comparing tailpipe figures with life-cycle figures.** Published life-cycle numbers include fuel production and car manufacture, so they are always higher than this tank-to-wheel result.
