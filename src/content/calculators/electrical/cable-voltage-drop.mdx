## How to use the cable voltage drop calculator

Choose the **circuit type** and **conductor material**, then enter the **supply voltage**, **current**, **one-way cable length** and **cross-section**. The voltage drop appears in volts and as a percentage, with a colour guide. To size a cable, press × on the cross-section and type your allowed drop %.

For more background, read [Cable Sizing for Motor Feeders](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/cable-sizing-motor-feeders/).

## Cable voltage drop formula

<p class="formula">Single-phase: V<sub>d</sub> = 2 × L × I × ρ ÷ A<br/>Three-phase: V<sub>d</sub> = √3 × L × I × ρ ÷ A</p>

- **ρ** — resistivity: copper 1.72 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m, aluminium 2.82 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m
- **L** — one-way length
- **A** — conductor cross-section

## Worked examples

**Single-phase:** 16 A at 230 V over 30 m of 2.5 mm² copper:
V<sub>d</sub> = 2 × 30 × 16 × 1.72 × 10⁻⁸ ÷ 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ = **6.6 V (2.9%)**

**Three-phase:** 32 A at 415 V over 50 m of 6 mm² copper:
V<sub>d</sub> = 1.732 × 50 × 32 × 1.72 × 10⁻⁸ ÷ 6 × 10⁻⁶ = **7.9 V (1.9%)**

## Voltage drop limits

Common design limits (as a percentage of supply voltage):

| Circuit | Typical limit |
|---|---|
| Lighting | 3% |
| Other final circuits | 5% |
| Motor starting | 10–15% (briefly) |

Limits vary by country. IEC 60364 and BS 7671 use 3% for lighting and 5% for other uses from the origin of the installation.

## Temperature effect

Copper resistance rises about 0.39% per °C. At a conductor temperature of 70 °C, resistance is about 20% higher than at 20 °C, so voltage drop is higher in fully loaded cables.

## Choosing a cable size

1. Size for current-carrying capacity first.
2. Check voltage drop over the run length.
3. If the drop is too high, step up one or two sizes.
4. Check fault-loop impedance and protective device coordination.

## Remember

Voltage drop is only one check. The cable must also carry the current safely (current rating with installation derating) and allow protective devices to disconnect a fault in time.

## Sizing the cable for a 3% limit

The default circuit, 16 A over 30 m of 2.5 mm² copper at 230 V, drops **6.60 V (2.87%)**. Working backwards for a 3% lighting limit:

- A<sub>min</sub> = 2 × 30 × 16 × 1.72 × 10⁻⁸ ÷ (0.03 × 230) = **2.39 mm²**, so 2.5 mm² just passes
- Stretch the same run to 50 m and A<sub>min</sub> becomes **3.99 mm²**, which means 4 mm²
- Current rating is checked separately, as in [choosing cable size for load current](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/cable-size-for-load-current/), and the larger of the two sizes wins
- In aluminium the same drop needs 2.5 × 2.82 ÷ 1.72 = **4.10 mm²**, so the next standard size, 6 mm²

| Size | Loop mV/A/m at 20 °C | Max length for 3% | Max length for 5% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5 mm² | 22.93 | 19 m | 31 m |
| 2.5 mm² | 13.76 | 31 m | 52 m |
| 4 mm² | 8.60 | 50 m | 84 m |
| 6 mm² | 5.73 | 75 m | 125 m |
| 10 mm² | 3.44 | 125 m | 209 m |
| 16 mm² | 2.15 | 201 m | 334 m |

These lengths scale inversely with current: at 32 A each one halves.

## Why datasheet mV/A/m values are higher

From resistivity alone, 2.5 mm² twin cable gives 13.76 mV/A/m. Wiring regulation tables quote about 18 mV/A/m because they assume the conductor is at its 70 °C operating temperature. Correcting the default example by 1 + 0.00393 × 50 = 1.196 raises the drop to **7.90 V (3.44%)**, which now fails a 3% limit. Use the hot value for cables that run near their rated current and the 20 °C value only for lightly loaded runs.

## Checking voltage drop on site

Measure the voltage at the distribution board and at the far end at the same moment, with the load running. If the board reads 230.4 V and the socket reads 223.8 V at 16 A, the drop is 6.6 V and the loop resistance is 6.6 ÷ 16 = **0.412 Ω**, against 0.413 Ω expected for 60 m of 2.5 mm² conductor. A reading well above the calculated value usually points to a loose terminal or a longer route than drawn. A low-resistance ohmmeter on the dead circuit (an R1 + R2 style test) gives the same figure without load.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering the total conductor length.** L is the one-way route length; the factor 2 or √3 already accounts for the return path.
- **Using 3% from the board instead of from the origin.** The limit usually applies from the supply origin, so the submain drop must be added to the final circuit drop.
- **Using phase voltage for a three phase run.** With the √3 factor, compare the drop against the line voltage (400 V or 415 V), not 230 V.
- **Ignoring motor starting current.** A motor drawing 6 times its running current gives 6 times the drop during start, which can stall contactors.
- **Applying the resistive formula to large cables.** Above about 25 mm² reactance becomes significant and the manufacturer's mV/A/m (r and x) values give a more accurate answer.
