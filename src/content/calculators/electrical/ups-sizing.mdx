## How to use the UPS size calculator

Add up the **load** of everything the UPS will power, in watts. Enter the UPS's **output power factor** and a **margin** for growth. You get the minimum kVA and kW rating; choose the next standard size up.

## UPS size formula

<p class="formula">UPS kVA = Load W × (1 + margin) ÷ PF ÷ 1,000</p>

## Typical loads

| Equipment | Typical power |
|---|---|
| Desktop PC with monitor | 150 to 300 W |
| Laptop | 45 to 100 W |
| Network switch (24-port) | 30 to 60 W |
| Wi-Fi router | 10 to 20 W |
| 1U server | 250 to 500 W |
| Laser printer (printing) | 400 to 800 W |
| PLC control panel | 100 to 500 W |
| LED lighting per room | 30 to 60 W |

Use measured or nameplate input power. Avoid connecting laser printers, heaters and motors to small UPSs, because their inrush current can overload them.

## Standard UPS ratings

1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 160 and 200 kVA are common sizes.

## Choosing the topology

| Type | Best for |
|---|---|
| Offline (standby) | Home PCs, small loads |
| Line-interactive | Offices, network racks; handles voltage swings |
| Online double-conversion | Servers, industrial control, medical, sensitive loads |

## Backup time

The UPS rating sets how much load it can carry; the battery sets how long. See the [battery backup calculator](/everyday-life/inverter-battery-backup/) to size the batteries.

## Worked example with the default values

A 4 kW load, a UPS output power factor of 0.9 and a 25% margin:

- Design load = 4,000 × (1 + 0.25) = **5.0 kW**, the minimum watt rating
- Minimum kVA = 5,000 ÷ 0.9 ÷ 1,000 = **5.56 kVA**

The next standard size is **6 kVA**, which at PF 0.9 is rated 5.4 kW. The present 4 kW load then uses 74% of the UPS, leaving room for growth.

## Example: sizing for a server rack

| Equipment | Qty | Each | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1U servers | 4 | 350 W | 1,400 W |
| 24-port switch | 2 | 45 W | 90 W |
| Firewall | 1 | 60 W | 60 W |
| Storage array | 1 | 500 W | 500 W |
| Monitor and KVM | 1 | 40 W | 40 W |

The rack draws **2,090 W**. With a 25% margin and a PF 0.9 UPS the minimum is 2.90 kVA, so a 3 kVA unit fits. An older PF 0.8 UPS would need 3.27 kVA for the same load, which shows why the power factor rating matters as much as the kVA figure.

## Practical notes

- **Measure where you can.** A clamp meter or a PDU with metering shows real running watts, which are often 50 to 70% of nameplate figures for IT equipment.
- **Check inrush and start-up loads.** Motors, compressors, laser printers and transformers can draw several times their running power for a moment; a UPS overloaded at switch-on drops to bypass or shuts down.
- **Three-phase UPS.** For units above about 10 kVA, also check that the load is balanced across phases so no single phase exceeds one third of the rating.
- **Battery is separate.** At the default 4 kW load, 15 minutes of backup needs roughly 1.11 kWh from the battery allowing for 90% inverter efficiency; size it with the [battery backup calculator](/everyday-life/inverter-battery-backup/).

## Common mistakes

- **Adding VA ratings of power supplies.** A PC power supply labelled 500 W rarely draws 500 W; use measured or typical input power, or the sum is far too high.
- **Sizing by kVA alone.** A 6 kVA UPS rated at PF 0.8 supplies only 4.8 kW, less than a 5 kW load even though the kVA looks ample.
- **Running at 100% from day one.** A fully loaded UPS has no room for growth, runs hotter and gives shorter battery runtime.
- **Ignoring redundancy.** For N+1 designs, the load must fit on the remaining modules after one fails, not on the total installed capacity.
