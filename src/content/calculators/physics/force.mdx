## How to use the force calculator

Enter any **two** of mass, acceleration and force. The third is calculated, in newtons, kN, kgf or lbf.

## Force formula

<p class="formula">F = m × a</p>

## Worked example

A 1,200 kg car accelerating at 3 m/s² needs a net force of 1,200 × 3 = **3,600 N**, which is 3.6 kN or 809 lbf. At that steady rate it would reach 100 km/h (27.78 m/s) in 27.78 ÷ 3 = 9.26 s.

## Net force

F is the **net** force — the total after friction, drag and other forces are taken into account. A car's engine must provide more than 3,600 N to overcome rolling resistance and air drag as well.

## Force examples

| Situation | Mass | Acceleration | Force |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pushing a shopping trolley | 30 kg | 0.5 m/s² | 15 N |
| Car accelerating | 1,200 kg | 3 m/s² | 3,600 N |
| Lift starting upward | 800 kg | 1 m/s² | 800 N (plus weight) |
| Sprinter starting | 75 kg | 6 m/s² | 450 N |
| Football kick | 0.43 kg | 2,000 m/s² (briefly) | 860 N |

## Newton's three laws

1. **Inertia**: an object stays at rest or keeps moving at a constant speed unless a net force acts on it.
2. **F = ma**: the net force equals mass times acceleration.
3. **Action and reaction**: every force has an equal and opposite force on the other object.

## Weight is a force

Weight is the force of gravity: W = m × g. A 70 kg person weighs about 686 N on Earth. See the [weight calculator](/physics/weight/) for other planets.

## Friction

Friction resists sliding. The maximum friction force is μ × N, where μ is the coefficient of friction and N is the normal force. Rubber on dry concrete has μ ≈ 0.7–0.8; steel on ice is under 0.05.

## From net force to the force the engine must supply

The 3,600 N above is what is left over to accelerate the car. At 36 km/h (10 m/s), the tyres and the air also push back:

1. Rolling resistance with C<sub>rr</sub> = 0.012: 1,200 × 9.807 × 0.012 = 141.2 N
2. Air drag (C<sub>d</sub> 0.30, 2.2 m²): 0.5 × 1.225 × 10² × 0.30 × 2.2 = 40.4 N
3. Driving force at the wheels = 3,600 + 141.2 + 40.4 = **3,781.6 N**

So the drivetrain must deliver about 5% more than F = ma alone suggests, and the share grows as speed rises.

## Force for a target 0 to 100 km/h time

Assuming constant acceleration of the default 1,200 kg car:

| 0 to 100 km/h | Acceleration | In g | Net force |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 s | 6.94 m/s² | 0.71 g | 8,333 N |
| 6 s | 4.63 m/s² | 0.47 g | 5,556 N |
| 8 s | 3.47 m/s² | 0.35 g | 4,167 N |
| 10 s | 2.78 m/s² | 0.28 g | 3,333 N |
| 12 s | 2.31 m/s² | 0.24 g | 2,778 N |
| 15 s | 1.85 m/s² | 0.19 g | 2,222 N |

Real cars accelerate harder at low speed and less near the top, so these are average forces.

## Lifting: when gravity is part of the sum

For vertical motion the weight must be added. A lift car of 800 kg accelerating upward at 1 m/s² needs a cable tension of 800 × (9.807 + 1) = **8,645.3 N**, against 7,845.3 N when it hangs still. That extra 800 N is the F = ma part, and it is why cranes and hoists are rated with dynamic factors.

## How force is measured

Load cells are the standard tool: strain gauges bonded to a steel element change resistance as it flexes, and a bridge circuit turns that into a signal proportional to force. Spring balances and handheld force gauges do the same job mechanically for small loads, and piezoelectric sensors capture fast impacts such as a kick or a hammer blow. Force plates under a sprinter measure the push against the ground directly.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the applied force instead of the net force.** F = ma uses the sum of all forces; a 500 N push against 200 N of friction accelerates the object with 300 N.
- **Entering mass in kgf or weight in kg.** Mass is in kg; a force written as 50 kgf is 490 N.
- **Using km/h per second as m/s².** 10 km/h per second is only 2.78 m/s².
- **Leaving out the weight in vertical problems.** A lifting cable carries m (g + a), not just m a.
- **Mixing lb and lbf.** In imperial units, 1 lbf accelerates 1 lb at 32.17 ft/s², not 1 ft/s².
