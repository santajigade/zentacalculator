## How to use the absolute to gauge pressure calculator

Enter the **gauge pressure** (use a negative value for vacuum) and the **altitude**. The calculator estimates atmospheric pressure with the standard-atmosphere formula and returns the absolute pressure. To convert absolute to gauge, clear the gauge pressure and enter the absolute value. If you have a barometer reading, clear the altitude and enter the atmospheric pressure directly.

## Absolute pressure formulas

<p class="formula">p<sub>abs</sub> = p<sub>gauge</sub> + p<sub>atm</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; p<sub>atm</sub> = 101,325 × (1 − 2.25577×10⁻⁵ z)<sup>5.25588</sup> Pa</p>

## Worked example

2 bar(g) at sea level:

- p<sub>atm</sub> = **1.01325 bar**
- p<sub>abs</sub> = 2 + 1.01325 = **3.01325 bar(a)**

## Notes

- Weather changes atmospheric pressure by about ±3% around the standard value.
- Gas flow corrections and compressor calculations always need absolute pressure.

## Worked example at altitude

A pump suction gauge reads **−0.5 bar(g)** in Pune, about 560 m above sea level:

- Atmospheric pressure at 560 m ≈ **0.948 bar(a)**
- Absolute suction pressure = 0.948 − 0.5 = **0.448 bar(a)**

At sea level the same gauge reading would mean 0.513 bar(a). That 13% difference matters when you check NPSH or whether a liquid will flash or boil.

## Gauge, absolute and sealed gauge transmitters

| Type | Reference | Typical units | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gauge (vented) | Local atmosphere | bar(g), psig | Pumps, compressors, hydraulics, most process lines |
| Absolute | Sealed vacuum | bar(a), psia | Vacuum, distillation, gas flow compensation, boiling points |
| Sealed gauge | Sealed at about 1 atm | bar(sg) | High pressures where a vent could let in moisture |

## Vacuum readings in other units

Vacuum equipment is specified in several ways. At sea level (1,013.25 mbar atmosphere) the same gauge readings convert as follows:

| Gauge reading | Absolute | Absolute in torr | Vacuum in inHg | Vacuum as % of atmosphere |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| -0.2 bar(g) | 813 mbar(a) | 610.0 torr | 5.91 inHg vacuum | 19.7% |
| -0.5 bar(g) | 513 mbar(a) | 385.0 torr | 14.76 inHg vacuum | 49.3% |
| -0.8 bar(g) | 213 mbar(a) | 160.0 torr | 23.62 inHg vacuum | 79.0% |
| -0.9 bar(g) | 113 mbar(a) | 84.9 torr | 26.58 inHg vacuum | 88.8% |
| -0.95 bar(g) | 63 mbar(a) | 47.4 torr | 28.05 inHg vacuum | 93.8% |
| -0.99 bar(g) | 23 mbar(a) | 17.4 torr | 29.23 inHg vacuum | 97.7% |

A rough vacuum pump that pulls 50 mbar(a) is already at about 95% vacuum, while the last few percent down to 1 mbar(a) take a much better pump and tight seals. This is why vacuum below about 100 mbar is always specified in absolute units.

## Checking a gauge against a barometer

To check that a gauge transmitter and an absolute transmitter agree, read a local barometer and add its value to the gauge reading. With the default 2 bar(g) and a barometer showing 1,005 mbar, the absolute transmitter should read 3.005 bar(a), not the 3.01325 bar(a) the standard atmosphere predicts. The 8.3 mbar difference is weather, not a calibration fault.

## Pressure ratios need absolute values

A ratio of pressures is meaningless in gauge units. An air compressor drawing at 0.2 bar(g) and discharging at 6 bar(g) would appear to have a ratio of 30. In absolute terms at sea level it is (6 + 1.013) ÷ (0.2 + 1.013) = **5.78**. The same gauge readings on a plant at 1,500 m, where the atmosphere is 0.8456 bar, give a ratio of **6.55**, because the absolute suction pressure is lower. With air drawn in at 30 °C, the ideal (isentropic) discharge temperature T₂ = T₁ × r<sup>0.286</sup> rises from 227 °C at sea level to 245 °C at altitude, which is why compressor and blower ratings are always quoted against absolute inlet conditions.

## Boiling point of water at a vacuum reading

The temperature at which water boils depends on the absolute pressure, so a vacuum gauge alone does not fix it. Saturation temperatures from the Antoine equation:

| Gauge reading | At sea level | At 1,500 m |
|---|---|---|
| −0.3 bar(g) | 713 mbar(a), 90.5 °C | 546 mbar(a), 83.6 °C |
| −0.5 bar(g) | 513 mbar(a), 82.0 °C | 346 mbar(a), 72.5 °C |
| −0.6 bar(g) | 413 mbar(a), 76.7 °C | 246 mbar(a), 64.6 °C |
| −0.7 bar(g) | 313 mbar(a), 70.2 °C | 146 mbar(a), 53.4 °C |
| −0.8 bar(g) | 213 mbar(a), 61.5 °C | 46 mbar(a), 31.3 °C |

At −0.8 bar(g) the two sites differ by 30 °C, enough to decide whether an evaporator boils or a pump suction flashes.

## Common mistakes

- **Using gauge pressure in the gas laws.** Ideal gas, density and flow compensation formulas need absolute pressure.
- **Assuming 1.013 bar everywhere.** At 1,600 m atmospheric pressure is only about 0.835 bar.
- **Ignoring the weather.** Barometric pressure moves about ±30 mbar. On low-pressure or vacuum work, use an absolute transmitter or a local barometer reading.
- **Mixing up vacuum notations.** "600 mbar vacuum" is −600 mbar(g), while "600 mbar absolute" is a much weaker vacuum. Always check which one a datasheet means.
- **Calibrating a vacuum gauge against an absolute reference without a barometer.** Weather alone can shift the expected gauge reading by 20 to 30 mbar.
