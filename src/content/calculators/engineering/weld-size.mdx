## How to use the fillet weld size calculator

Enter the **leg size**, the **effective weld length**, the **load** and the **allowable shear stress** on the throat. The calculator returns the throat thickness, the actual stress, the weld's load capacity and its utilisation (below 100% passes). Clear the leg size and enter the load to find the fillet size you need.

## Fillet weld formula

<p class="formula">a = 0.707 × s &nbsp;·&nbsp; τ = F ÷ (a × L) &nbsp;·&nbsp; F<sub>max</sub> = τ<sub>allow</sub> × a × L</p>

## Example

A 6 mm fillet, 100 mm long, made with E70 electrodes (τ<sub>allow</sub> = 145 MPa), carries 50 kN:

- Throat a = 0.707 × 6 = **4.24 mm**
- Stress τ = 50,000 ÷ (4.24 × 100) = **117.9 MPa**
- Capacity = 145 × 4.24 × 100 = **61.5 kN**; utilisation 81% — OK

## Notes

- This simplified method treats the load as shear on the throat in any direction; it is conservative for welds loaded transversely.
- The parent metal next to the weld must also be checked.
- Compare the weld stress with your required [factor of safety](/engineering/factor-of-safety/).

## Sizing a weld for a load

To find the leg needed for 80 kN on the same 100 mm of weld at 145 MPa:

- Required throat a = 80 × 1,000 ÷ (145 × 100) = **5.52 mm**
- Required leg s = 5.52 ÷ 0.707 = **7.80 mm**, so specify an **8 mm** fillet

If the joint is welded on both sides, each weld line counts, so two 100 mm runs of 6 mm fillet give 123.0 kN. For an unequal-leg fillet the throat is s₁ s₂ ÷ √(s₁² + s₂²); a 6 × 10 mm fillet has a throat of **5.14 mm**, only a little more than a 6 mm equal-leg weld.

## Inspection and practice

The leg size is checked with a fillet weld gauge after welding, and a concave weld face reduces the true throat below 0.707 × leg, so inspectors check the throat too. Effective length excludes the start and stop craters; many codes deduct about twice the leg size from each run and set a minimum length of around four times the leg. Intermittent welds carry load only over their weld lengths, not the gaps between them.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the leg as the throat.** Designing with the leg size overstates capacity by about 41%.
- **Counting the full run as effective length.** Craters at each end carry little load; deduct them, especially on short welds.
- **Ignoring eccentric loads.** A load offset from the weld group adds bending and torsion stress; the simple F ÷ (aL) check is then unsafe.
- **Mixing factored and working loads.** Allowable stresses (AWS) go with service loads; Eurocode design strengths go with factored loads.
- **Forgetting the parent metal.** The plate next to the weld can fail in shear before the weld does, so check its capacity as well.
