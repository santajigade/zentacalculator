## How to use the scientific notation calculator

Enter a **number** in ordinary decimal form (you can also type E notation such as 6.02e23). The calculator returns the **exponent** and **coefficient** of its scientific notation and the matching **engineering notation**. To convert back, enter a coefficient and exponent and the decimal number is filled in.

## Scientific notation formula

<p class="formula">x = a × 10ⁿ &nbsp;with&nbsp; n = ⌊log₁₀|x|⌋ &nbsp;and&nbsp; a = x ÷ 10ⁿ</p>

<p class="formula">Engineering: n<sub>eng</sub> = 3 × ⌊n ÷ 3⌋ &nbsp;·&nbsp; a<sub>eng</sub> = x ÷ 10<sup>n<sub>eng</sub></sup></p>

## Worked example

Convert 45,600,000:

- log₁₀(45,600,000) = 7.6590, so the exponent is **7**
- Coefficient = 45,600,000 ÷ 10⁷ = **4.56** → **4.56 × 10⁷** (E notation 4.56E7)
- Engineering exponent = 3 × ⌊7 ÷ 3⌋ = **6**, coefficient **45.6** → 45.6 × 10⁶ = 45.6 mega

For a small number, 0.00045 → log₁₀ = −3.35, exponent **−4**, coefficient **4.5** → 4.5 × 10⁻⁴ (engineering 450 × 10⁻⁶ = 450 micro).

## Practical notes

- Zero has no scientific-notation exponent; it is written simply as 0.
- **Significant figures** are the digits in the coefficient: 4.560 × 10⁷ states four, 4.56 × 10⁷ three.
- Engineering exponents map to SI prefixes: 10³ kilo, 10⁶ mega, 10⁹ giga, 10⁻³ milli, 10⁻⁶ micro, 10⁻⁹ nano, 10⁻¹² pico.

## Default example check

The calculator starts with 45,600,000. Its exponent is ⌊log₁₀ 45,600,000⌋ = ⌊7.6590⌋ = **7**, the coefficient is **4.56**, the engineering exponent is **6** and the engineering coefficient is **45.6**.

## Arithmetic in scientific notation

- **Adding or subtracting:** rewrite both numbers with the same exponent first. 4.56 × 10⁷ + 3.2 × 10⁵ = 4.56 × 10⁷ + 0.032 × 10⁷ = **4.592 × 10⁷**.
- **Dividing:** divide the coefficients and subtract the exponents. (4.56 × 10⁷) ÷ (1.2 × 10⁻³) = 3.8 × 10⁷⁻⁽⁻³⁾ = **3.8 × 10¹⁰**.
- **Multiplying with renormalising:** (4.56 × 10⁷) × (6.5 × 10⁻⁴) = 29.64 × 10³, which is not normalised, so move the point once to get **2.964 × 10⁴**.

## Rounding to significant figures

The coefficient carries the precision. Rounding 45,600,000 to fewer significant figures:

| Significant figures | Scientific notation | Decimal value |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 × 10⁷ | 50,000,000 |
| 2 | 4.6 × 10⁷ | 46,000,000 |
| 3 | 4.56 × 10⁷ | 45,600,000 |
| 4 | 4.560 × 10⁷ | 45,600,000 |

Trailing zeros in the coefficient are meaningful: 4.560 × 10⁷ claims a precision of ±5,000, while 4.56 × 10⁷ claims ±50,000.

## Where you meet it

Spreadsheets switch to E notation when a number is too wide for the column, showing 4.56E+07. Spreadsheet programs keep only 15 significant digits, so a 16 digit card or account number typed as a number is stored in scientific form and its last digit becomes 0; format such columns as text before pasting. Instruments and data loggers often export readings like 1.23E-06 A, which is 1.23 µA in engineering terms.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing 10E7 for 10⁷.** On calculators and in code, 10E7 means 10 × 10⁷ = 10⁸. Type 1E7 for ten million.
- **Leaving the coefficient above 10.** 45.6 × 10⁶ is valid engineering notation but not normalised scientific notation; it should be 4.56 × 10⁷.
- **Getting the exponent sign backwards for small numbers.** 0.00045 needs the point moved four places right, so the exponent is −4, not +4.
- **Adding exponents when adding numbers.** Exponents add only when multiplying; for sums, match the exponents first.
- **Dropping significant zeros.** Writing 4.5 × 10⁷ for a value measured as 4.50 × 10⁷ throws away a digit of precision.
