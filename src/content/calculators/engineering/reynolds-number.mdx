## How to use the Reynolds number calculator

Enter the fluid's **density** and **viscosity**, the **velocity** and the pipe's **inner diameter**. The Reynolds number and flow regime appear immediately. Need velocity from a flow rate? Use the [pipe flow velocity calculator](/engineering/pipe-flow-velocity/) first.

## Reynolds number formula

<p class="formula">Re = ρ v D ÷ μ</p>

- **ρ** — density (kg/m³)
- **v** — mean velocity (m/s)
- **D** — inner diameter (m)
- **μ** — dynamic viscosity (Pa·s; 1 cP = 0.001 Pa·s)

Re has no units — as long as the inputs are consistent. The calculator handles the conversions.

## Worked example

Water at 20 °C (ρ = 998.2 kg/m³, μ = 1.002 cP) flowing at 2 m/s in a 100 mm pipe:

Re = 998.2 × 2 × 0.1 ÷ 0.001002 ≈ **199,000** → turbulent

## Flow regimes in pipes

| Reynolds number | Regime |
|---|---|
| < 2,300 | Laminar |
| 2,300 – 4,000 | Transitional |
| > 4,000 | Turbulent |

## Reynolds number for water in pipes

At 20 °C (ρ = 998 kg/m³, μ = 1.002 mPa·s):

| Diameter | 0.5 m/s | 1 m/s | 2 m/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 mm | 7,470 | 14,940 | 29,880 |
| 25 mm | 12,450 | 24,900 | 49,800 |
| 50 mm | 24,900 | 49,800 | 99,601 |
| 100 mm | 49,800 | 99,601 | 199,202 |
| 200 mm | 99,601 | 199,202 | 398,403 |

Water in industrial pipes is almost always turbulent.

## Kinematic viscosity form

If you have kinematic viscosity ν (in m²/s or centistokes), use:

<p class="formula">Re = v × D ÷ ν</p>

1 cSt = 10<sup>−6</sup> m²/s. Water at 20 °C is about 1.0 cSt.

## Why it matters for flow meters

Many flow meters have Reynolds-number limits:

- **Orifice plates**: discharge coefficients are well defined above about Re = 5,000–10,000.
- **Vortex meters**: need Re above about 10,000–20,000 to shed vortices reliably.
- **Turbine meters**: calibration shifts with viscosity at low Re.
- **Electromagnetic and Coriolis meters**: largely unaffected.

## Viscous liquids

Oils and syrups can have viscosities hundreds of times higher than water. Flow of a 100 cP oil at 1 m/s in a 50 mm pipe gives Re ≈ 450 — laminar. Pressure-drop and meter behaviour are then very different from water.

## Typical viscosities at 20 °C

| Fluid | Viscosity (cP) |
|---|---|
| Water | 1.0 |
| Diesel | 2 – 4 |
| Olive oil | ~84 |
| Glycerine | ~1,400 |
| Honey | 2,000 – 10,000 |

Viscosity changes strongly with temperature — use a value for your operating temperature.

## Result with the default values

The defaults describe water at about 20 °C (998 kg/m³, 1.002 cP) at 2 m/s in a 100 mm bore. Re = 998 × 2 × 0.1 ÷ 0.001002 = **199,202**, well into the turbulent band.

## Example: air in a rectangular duct

For a duct that is not round, use the hydraulic diameter D<sub>h</sub> = 4A ÷ P. A 400 × 200 mm duct has D<sub>h</sub> = 2 × 0.4 × 0.2 ÷ (0.4 + 0.2) = **266.7 mm**. With air at 20 °C (1.204 kg/m³, 0.0181 cP) moving at 6 m/s:

Re = 1.204 × 6 × 0.2667 ÷ 0.0000181 = **106,431**, turbulent. Enter 266.7 mm as the diameter and 0.0181 cP as the viscosity to get the same figure.

Laminar flow in water needs very low speeds. In a 25 mm tube, water at 20 °C stays laminar only below about **92 mm/s**. Viscous oils are the opposite case: a 100 cP oil (880 kg/m³) at 1 m/s in a 50 mm pipe gives Re = 440, and the laminar velocity profile takes about 0.05 × Re × D = **1.1 m** of straight pipe to develop fully.

## Water temperature and Reynolds number

Viscosity falls quickly as water warms, so the same flow becomes more turbulent. Water at 1 m/s in a 50 mm bore:

| Temperature | Density (kg/m³) | Viscosity (cP) | Re |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 °C | 999.8 | 1.792 | 27,896 |
| 20 °C | 998.2 | 1.002 | 49,810 |
| 40 °C | 992.2 | 0.653 | 75,972 |
| 60 °C | 983.2 | 0.467 | 105,268 |
| 80 °C | 971.8 | 0.355 | 136,873 |
| 100 °C | 958.4 | 0.282 | 169,929 |

Between 0 °C and 60 °C the Reynolds number rises from 27,896 to 105,268, more than three and a half times, with no change in flow.

## How it is used in practice

Plant engineers rarely measure Reynolds number directly. They calculate it at minimum, normal and maximum flow from the process data sheet, using viscosity at operating temperature from the fluid supplier or a laboratory viscometer. The minimum flow case is the one that decides whether a vortex meter will keep counting or whether an orifice coefficient is still valid, so meter sizing tools print Re for each flow case.

## Common mistakes

- **Typing kinematic viscosity into the dynamic field.** Centistokes and centipoise are equal only when density is 1,000 kg/m³; for an oil at 880 kg/m³, 100 cSt is 88 cP.
- **Using nominal pipe size as the diameter.** A DN100 schedule 80 pipe has a bore of about 97 mm, not 100 mm, and the difference grows on small lines.
- **Taking viscosity at the wrong temperature.** Lube oil can change viscosity by a factor of ten between a cold start and normal running.
- **Using 2,300 for a non-circular duct without the hydraulic diameter.** The regime limits apply only when D<sub>h</sub> is used.
- **Using standard density for gas at line pressure.** Compressed air at 7 bar(g) is about eight times denser than free air, so its Reynolds number is about eight times higher at the same velocity.
