## How to use the Zener diode resistor calculator

Enter the **minimum input voltage**, the **Zener voltage**, the **maximum load current** and the **minimum Zener current** to keep it regulating. You get the series resistor value, its power, and the Zener's worst-case dissipation when the load is removed. Choose the next lower standard resistor and a Zener rated at least twice the calculated power.

## Zener resistor formula

<p class="formula">R = (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>Z</sub>) ÷ (I<sub>L</sub> + I<sub>Z,min</sub>)</p>

<p class="formula">P<sub>R</sub> = (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>Z</sub>)² ÷ R &nbsp;·&nbsp; P<sub>Z,max</sub> = V<sub>Z</sub> × (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>Z</sub>) ÷ R</p>

## Worked example

A 5.1 V Zener must supply a load of up to 20 mA from a supply that never falls below 12 V, keeping at least 5 mA in the Zener:

- Voltage across the resistor = 12 − 5.1 = **6.9 V**
- R = 6.9 ÷ (20 + 5) mA = 6.9 ÷ 0.025 A = **276 Ω**
- Resistor power = 6.9² ÷ 276 = **172.5 mW**
- Zener power with the load removed = 5.1 × 6.9 ÷ 276 = **127.5 mW**

Choose the next lower E24 value, **270 Ω**, so the Zener current only rises: it becomes 25.6 mA in total, leaving 5.6 mA in the Zener at full load. With 270 Ω the resistor dissipates 176 mW and the unloaded Zener 130 mW, so 0.5 W parts give a safe 2 to 3 times margin.

## Common Zener voltages (E24 series)

2.7, 3.3, 3.6, 3.9, 4.7, 5.1, 5.6, 6.2, 6.8, 8.2, 9.1, 10, 12, 15, 18, 24 V. Popular packages: BZX55/BZX79 (0.5 W) and 1N47xx (1 W).

## Other uses

- **Overvoltage clamps** on sensor inputs.
- **Reference voltages** for comparators.
- **Intrinsic safety barriers** use Zeners to limit voltage into hazardous areas.

## Design for the highest input voltage too

The resistor is sized at the lowest input voltage, but the Zener dissipates the most at the highest input with no load. If the 12 V supply in the example can rise to 14 V, the 270 Ω resistor passes 33.0 mA, the unloaded Zener dissipates **168 mW** and the resistor **293 mW**. Always check the power at the maximum input, and derate the Zener above 25 °C ambient as its datasheet shows (often about 4 mW per °C for a 0.5 W glass part).

## Practical notes

- **Regulation is modest.** The Zener's dynamic resistance (typically 5 to 30 Ω for small parts) means the output moves by tens of millivolts as the load current changes. Add an emitter follower transistor or use a regulator IC for tighter control.
- **Choose the Zener test current.** The datasheet voltage is quoted at a test current, often 5 mA for 0.5 W parts. Running far below it gives a lower, softer voltage.
- **Add a capacitor.** A 100 nF to 10 µF capacitor across the Zener reduces its noise, which is significant in avalanche diodes above about 6 V.

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing at the nominal input voltage.** If the supply sags below the design value, the Zener current falls to zero and the output drops out of regulation.
- **Rounding the resistor up.** A higher standard value reduces the current and can starve the Zener at full load; round down instead.
- **Forgetting the no-load case.** When the load is disconnected all the current flows into the Zener, which is when it overheats.
- **Using a Zener for large currents.** Above roughly 50 mA the wasted power grows quickly; a linear regulator or a switching converter is more efficient.
- **Ignoring tolerance.** A 5.1 V Zener with 5% tolerance can be anywhere from about 4.85 to 5.36 V, which matters when it is used as a reference.
