## How to use the photon energy calculator

Enter the **wavelength in nanometres**. The calculator returns the frequency in THz, the photon energy in electronvolts and joules and the energy of one mole of photons. Enter the **optical power** of a source to find how many photons it emits each second. To go from energy to wavelength, clear the wavelength and enter the energy in eV.

## Photon energy formula

<p class="formula">E = h f = h c ÷ λ</p>

<p class="formula">E (eV) ≈ 1239.84 ÷ λ (nm) &nbsp;·&nbsp; N = P ÷ E</p>

h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (Planck constant), c = 299,792,458 m/s and 1 eV = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ J — all exact SI values.

## Worked example

A 532 nm green laser with 1 mW output, the calculator defaults:

- f = 299,792,458 ÷ 532 × 10⁻⁹ = **563.52 THz**
- E = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 563.52 × 10¹² = **3.7339 × 10⁻¹⁹ J** = **2.3305 eV**
- Per mole: **224.9 kJ/mol**
- Photons per second = 0.001 ÷ 3.7339 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **2.678 × 10¹⁵**

## Notes

- Shorter wavelength means higher energy: UV-C photons (about 4.9 eV) can break chemical bonds, which is why they disinfect.
- An LED's forward voltage is roughly its photon energy in eV — a blue LED needs about 2.8–3.3 V, a red one about 1.8–2.2 V.
- For a matter wave instead of light, use the [de Broglie wavelength calculator](/physics/de-broglie-wavelength/).

Each step uses the exact SI constants: f = c ÷ λ = 299,792,458 ÷ (532 × 10⁻⁹) Hz, then E = h f, then the result is divided by the elementary charge for eV and multiplied by Avogadro's number for the molar value. The shortcut 1239.84 ÷ 532 gives the same 2.3305 eV.

## From photons to detector current

A photodiode turns each absorbed photon into at most one electron. Its ideal responsivity is therefore the electron charge divided by the photon energy, R = q ÷ E = λ (nm) ÷ 1239.84 A/W. For the 532 nm laser:

- Ideal responsivity = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 3.7339 × 10⁻¹⁹ = **0.429 A/W**
- With a quantum efficiency of 80%, a typical silicon value in the green, the photocurrent from 1 mW is 1 mW × 0.429 × 0.8 = **343 µA**

The same 1 mW gives a larger current at longer wavelengths, because each photon carries less energy and so there are more of them:

| Wavelength | Photon energy | Ideal responsivity | Photons per second in 1 mW |
|---|---|---|---|
| 400 nm | 3.100 eV | 0.323 A/W | 2.014 × 10¹⁵ |
| 532 nm | 2.331 eV | 0.429 A/W | 2.678 × 10¹⁵ |
| 650 nm | 1.907 eV | 0.524 A/W | 3.272 × 10¹⁵ |
| 850 nm | 1.459 eV | 0.686 A/W | 4.279 × 10¹⁵ |
| 940 nm | 1.319 eV | 0.758 A/W | 4.732 × 10¹⁵ |
| 1550 nm | 0.800 eV | 1.250 A/W | 7.803 × 10¹⁵ |

Silicon stops responding near 1107 nm, where the photon energy falls below its 1.12 eV band gap, so the 1550 nm row needs an InGaAs or germanium detector.

## How light power is measured in practice

Laser power meters use either a calibrated photodiode head, which reads in amps and divides by the responsivity at the wavelength you select, or a thermal head that absorbs the beam and measures the temperature rise. That is why a photodiode meter set to the wrong wavelength reads wrongly while a thermal head does not care. Spectrometers report wavelength directly, and this calculator converts that to energy.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering metres or micrometres in the nanometre box.** 1.55 µm must be typed as 1550, otherwise the energy is a thousand times too large.
- **Confusing photon energy with beam power.** A brighter 532 nm laser emits more photons per second; each photon still carries 2.331 eV.
- **Using vacuum wavelength rules inside glass.** The photon energy is fixed by frequency; the wavelength shortens in a medium but the energy does not change.
- **Mixing kJ/mol with eV per photon.** 1 eV per photon equals about 96.49 kJ/mol, so 2.331 eV is 224.9 kJ/mol, not 2.331 kJ/mol.
- **Assuming LED voltage equals photon energy exactly.** Real forward voltage is higher because of series resistance and the junction design.
