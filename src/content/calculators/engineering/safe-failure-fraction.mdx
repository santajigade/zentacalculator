  - q: How is safe failure fraction calculated?
    a: SFF = (λS + λDD) ÷ (λS + λDD + λDU) — all safe failures plus dangerous detected failures, divided by the total failure rate. Only the dangerous undetected failures count against it.
  - q: What SFF is needed for SIL 2?
    a: Under IEC 61508 Route 1H with no redundancy (HFT 0), a Type A device needs SFF ≥ 60% and a Type B device SFF ≥ 90% for SIL 2. With HFT 1, Type A needs no minimum (below 60% is enough) and Type B needs SFF ≥ 60%.
  - q: What is the difference between SFF and diagnostic coverage?
    a: Diagnostic coverage looks only at dangerous failures, DC = λDD ÷ (λDD + λDU). SFF also counts safe failures, so a device with many safe failures can have a high SFF even with modest diagnostics.
  - q: What are Type A and Type B devices?
    a: Type A devices are simple, with well-known failure modes and field data (relays, solenoid valves, simple transmitters). Type B devices contain complex components such as microprocessors whose failure modes are not fully known — most smart transmitters and logic solvers.

## How to use the safe failure fraction calculator

Enter the **safe**, **dangerous detected** and **dangerous undetected** failure rates in FIT from the device's FMEDA report or safety manual (λS can include λSD + λSU, and λDD includes failures that the diagnostics drive to a safe state). The calculator returns the total failure rate, the SFF with its IEC 61508 band, the diagnostic coverage and the equivalent MTTF.

## Safe failure fraction formula

<p class="formula">SFF = (λ<sub>S</sub> + λ<sub>DD</sub>) ÷ (λ<sub>S</sub> + λ<sub>DD</sub> + λ<sub>DU</sub>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; DC = λ<sub>DD</sub> ÷ (λ<sub>DD</sub> + λ<sub>DU</sub>)</p>

## Worked example

A pressure transmitter with λ<sub>S</sub> = 300 FIT, λ<sub>DD</sub> = 450 FIT and λ<sub>DU</sub> = 50 FIT:

- Total failure rate = **800 FIT** (MTTF ≈ 143 years)
- SFF = (300 + 450) ÷ 800 = **93.75%**
- DC = 450 ÷ (450 + 50) = **90%**
- As a Type B device with HFT 0 it is limited to **SIL 2** (90–99% SFF); in a 1oo2 arrangement (HFT 1) it could be used up to SIL 3

## Maximum SIL by SFF and hardware fault tolerance (IEC 61508-2, Route 1H)

| SFF | Type A, HFT 0 | Type A, HFT 1 | Type A, HFT 2 | Type B, HFT 0 | Type B, HFT 1 | Type B, HFT 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| < 60% | SIL 1 | SIL 2 | SIL 3 | Not allowed | SIL 1 | SIL 2 |
| 60% – < 90% | SIL 2 | SIL 3 | SIL 4 | SIL 1 | SIL 2 | SIL 3 |
| 90% – < 99% | SIL 3 | SIL 4 | SIL 4 | SIL 2 | SIL 3 | SIL 4 |
| ≥ 99% | SIL 3 | SIL 4 | SIL 4 | SIL 3 | SIL 4 | SIL 4 |

HFT N means the subsystem still works after N dangerous faults (1oo1 = 0, 1oo2 = 1, 1oo3 = 2).

## Practical notes

- The architectural limit is separate from the **PFDavg** limit — a SIF must meet both, and the PFDavg depends mainly on λ<sub>DU</sub> and the proof-test interval.
- IEC 61511 (process sector) allows Route 2H or its own HFT table based on prior use instead of SFF; check which route your project uses.
- "No effect" and "no part" failures are excluded from the SFF calculation in IEC 61508 Edition 2.
- Detected failures only count as safe if the diagnostic response actually takes the process to a safe state (or alarms and the repair is done within the MTTR assumed).

## How diagnostic coverage moves SFF

Keeping the example device's λ<sub>S</sub> = 300 FIT and its total dangerous rate of 500 FIT, but changing how much of that dangerous rate the diagnostics catch:

| Diagnostic coverage | λ<sub>DD</sub> | λ<sub>DU</sub> | SFF | Type B, HFT 0 limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 0 FIT | 500 FIT | 37.50% | Not allowed |
| 50% | 250 FIT | 250 FIT | 68.75% | SIL 1 |
| 60% | 300 FIT | 200 FIT | 75.00% | SIL 1 |
| 80% | 400 FIT | 100 FIT | 87.50% | SIL 1 |
| 90% | 450 FIT | 50 FIT | 93.75% | SIL 2 |
| 95% | 475 FIT | 25 FIT | 96.88% | SIL 2 |
| 99% | 495 FIT | 5 FIT | 99.38% | SIL 3 |

The safe failures alone give this device a floor of 37.5% SFF. Each step in diagnostic coverage then converts undetected dangerous failures into detected ones, and the jump from 90% to 99% DC is what lifts a Type B single channel from SIL 2 to SIL 3 territory.

## Where the numbers come from: the FMEDA

A failure modes, effects and diagnostic analysis lists every component of the device, its failure rate and each failure mode, then classifies the effect of each mode on the safety function as safe, dangerous detected, dangerous undetected or no effect. The FMEDA must be done for the exact configuration used: the same transmitter can have a different λ<sub>DU</sub> when its output is set to fail low instead of fail high, or when the logic solver is not set to trip on an out of range current below 3.6 mA or above 21 mA.

## SFF and PFDavg are separate checks

A high SFF does not by itself give a low PFD<sub>avg</sub>. The example transmitter's λ<sub>DU</sub> of 50 FIT, proof tested yearly, contributes 50 × 10⁻⁹ × 8,760 ÷ 2 = **2.19 × 10⁻⁴** to the loop's PFD<sub>avg</sub> on its own. A safety function has to meet the SIL target for both the architectural constraint and the PFD<sub>avg</sub>, and the weaker of the two sets the SIL that can be claimed.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting "no effect" failures as safe.** IEC 61508 Edition 2 excludes them, and including them inflates the SFF of devices with many passive parts.
- **Treating λ<sub>DD</sub> as safe when nothing acts on the diagnostic.** If a detected fault only raises an alarm that nobody responds to within the assumed repair time, it behaves as dangerous.
- **Using a single device's SFF for a whole subsystem.** The architectural limit applies per subsystem: sensor, logic solver and final element are each checked with their own HFT.
- **Applying the Type A table to a smart transmitter.** Any device with a microprocessor or software is normally Type B, which has stricter limits.
- **Mixing data from different FMEDA reports.** Failure rates from different sources often use different assumptions about operating stress, so take λ<sub>S</sub>, λ<sub>DD</sub> and λ<sub>DU</sub> for one device from the same report.
