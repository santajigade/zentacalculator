## How to use the availability calculator

Enter the **MTBF**, the **MTTR** and a **mission time**. You get availability, expected downtime per year, failure rate, and the probability of running through the mission time without a failure (assuming a constant failure rate).

## Availability formulas

<p class="formula">A = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR) &nbsp;·&nbsp; λ = 1 ÷ MTBF &nbsp;·&nbsp; R(t) = e<sup>−t/MTBF</sup></p>

## Worked example

A transmitter has an MTBF of 87,600 h (10 years), an average repair time of 8 h and you want the chance of a fault-free year (8,760 h):

- Availability A = 87,600 ÷ (87,600 + 8) = **99.99087%**
- Downtime per year = (1 − A) × 8,760 = **0.80 h** (about 48 minutes)
- Failure rate λ = 8,760 ÷ 87,600 = **0.1 failures per year**
- Probability of no failure in a year R = e<sup>−8,760/87,600</sup> = **90.48%**

So roughly 1 transmitter in 10 of this type will fail in any given year, but each one is out of service for well under an hour a year on average.

## Where MTBF and MTTR come from

- **Field data:** MTBF = total operating hours ÷ number of failures. For example, 40 identical pumps running 3 years with 6 failures give 1,051,200 ÷ 6 = 175,200 h, about 20 years.
- **Databases and vendors:** OREDA, exida and manufacturer FMEDA reports publish failure rates for valves, transmitters and logic solvers. Vendor figures usually exclude misuse and process-related failures, so field MTBF is often lower.
- **MTTR** must include the whole outage: detecting the fault, getting a technician and spare to site, the repair itself, testing and restart. Logistics often take longer than the repair.

## Redundancy

Two identical units in parallel, where either can do the job, have unavailability of roughly (1 − A)². Two 99% units give about 99.99% — the reason redundant PLCs, transmitters and pumps are used on critical duties.

## Second example: a complete control loop

A flow loop works only if every element works, so the availabilities of the parts in series multiply. Typical figures for one loop:

| Element | MTBF | MTTR | Availability | Downtime per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure transmitter | 87,600 h | 8 h | 99.9909% | 48.0 min |
| PLC analogue I/O card | 200,000 h | 4 h | 99.9980% | 10.5 min |
| Control valve and positioner | 50,000 h | 24 h | 99.9520% | 252.2 min |

- Loop availability = product of the three = **99.9409%**
- Expected loop downtime = 5.18 h a year, about 311 minutes
- Failure rates add in series: 0.3190 failures a year, so the loop MTBF is 27,461 h (3.1 years)

The valve, with the lowest MTBF and the longest repair, causes most of the outage. Fitting a second valve in parallel that can take over raises its availability to 99.999977% and the loop to 99.9888%, cutting downtime to 59 minutes a year. Spending on the weakest element gives the biggest return.

## Chance of surviving longer missions

For the default MTBF of 87,600 h, with a constant failure rate:

| Mission time | Hours | Probability of no failure | Probability of at least one failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 month | 730 h | 99.17% | 0.83% |
| 6 months | 4,380 h | 95.12% | 4.88% |
| 1 year | 8,760 h | 90.48% | 9.52% |
| 5 years | 43,800 h | 60.65% | 39.35% |
| 10 years | 87,600 h | 36.79% | 63.21% |
| 20 years | 175,200 h | 13.53% | 86.47% |

Half of a population has failed at least once by MTBF × ln 2 = 60,720 h, about 6.9 years, well before the MTBF itself.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading MTBF as a lifetime.** MTBF describes random failures during useful life. It says nothing about wear-out, so a 100-year MTBF valve still needs overhaul.
- **Leaving waiting time out of MTTR.** A 1 h repair that waits 2 days for a spare part has an MTTR of about 49 h for availability purposes.
- **Multiplying availabilities of redundant units.** Multiplying applies to items in series (all must work). For parallel redundancy combine the unavailabilities instead.
- **Mixing MTBF and MTTF.** For repairable items the difference is the MTTR, which is negligible here. For non-repairable parts such as fuses or bulbs, use MTTF.
- **Ignoring planned downtime.** This formula covers unplanned failures only. Scheduled shutdowns and proof tests reduce operational availability further.
