## How to use the line and phase voltage calculator

Choose **star** or **delta**, enter the **line voltage** and **line current** (or the phase values, using the × buttons), and the **power factor**. The calculator gives the phase voltage and current and the total three-phase power.

## Line and phase formulas

| Connection | Voltage | Current |
|---|---|---|
| Star (wye) | V<sub>L</sub> = √3 × V<sub>ph</sub> | I<sub>L</sub> = I<sub>ph</sub> |
| Delta | V<sub>L</sub> = V<sub>ph</sub> | I<sub>L</sub> = √3 × I<sub>ph</sub> |

<p class="formula">P = √3 × V<sub>L</sub> × I<sub>L</sub> × PF = 3 × V<sub>ph</sub> × I<sub>ph</sub> × PF</p>

## Motor windings

A motor nameplate marked **Δ 415 V / Y 690 V** has windings rated at 415 V. On a 415 V supply it runs in delta, and can be started in star (where each winding gets 240 V) — the basis of the star-delta starter. See the [motor starting current calculator](/electrical/motor-starting-current/).

## Worked example with the default values

A star connected load on a 415 V supply drawing 10 A per line at 0.85 power factor:

- Phase voltage = 415 ÷ √3 = **239.6 V**
- Phase current = line current = **10 A**
- Power = √3 × 415 × 10 × 0.85 = **6.11 kW**

Switch the connection to delta with the same line values and each phase sees the full **415 V** but carries only 10 ÷ √3 = **5.77 A**. The power is unchanged: 3 × 415 × 5.774 × 0.85 = 6.11 kW, which confirms that the √3 formula with line values works for both.

## Checking it with a meter

With a true RMS multimeter, measure between each pair of lines (L1 L2, L2 L3, L3 L1) and then from each line to neutral. On a healthy 415 V star supply the line readings sit within about 2% of each other and the line to neutral readings near 240 V. A clamp meter on each line conductor gives the line current; on a delta motor the winding current can only be measured inside the terminal box on the link between winding ends, and it should be about 58% of the line reading.

## The 30 degree shift

In star, each line voltage is the phasor difference of two phase voltages, so it is √3 times larger and leads the first of them by 30°. This shift is why a Dyn11 transformer secondary is 30° ahead of its primary, and why two transformers of different vector groups must never be paralleled.

## Common mistakes

- **Applying √3 twice.** P = √3 × V<sub>L</sub> × I<sub>L</sub> already contains it; using phase voltage in that formula gives a result 1.73 times too small.
- **Looking for a neutral on a delta system.** A plain delta has none; a centre tapped 240 V high leg delta has a "wild leg" at 208 V to neutral that must not feed 120 V loads.
- **Wiring a delta rated motor in star on the same supply.** Each winding then gets 1 ÷ √3 of its voltage and the motor develops only 33% of its torque.
- **Sizing delta winding conductors on line current.** Inside a delta the winding carries line current ÷ √3, so internal links can be smaller than the supply cable.
- **Reading line to neutral as line voltage.** A meter showing 240 V on a "415 V" system is normal when one probe is on neutral.
