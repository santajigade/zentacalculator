    A Zener diode with a series resistor is the simplest voltage regulator: the Zener holds a fixed voltage while the resistor drops the rest and limits current. This Zener diode resistor calculator sizes that resistor and checks the power in both parts.

    Electronics hobbyists, students and circuit designers use it for references, clamps and small supplies. For the device itself, see what a Zener diode is.

    How to use the Zener diode resistor calculator

    Enter the minimum input voltage, the Zener voltage, the maximum load current and the minimum Zener current to keep it regulating. You get the series resistor value, its power, and the Zener’s worst-case dissipation when the load is removed. Choose the next lower standard resistor and a Zener rated at least twice the calculated power.

    Zener resistor formula

    R = (Vin − VZ) ÷ (IL + IZ,min)

    PR = (Vin − VZ)² ÷ R  ·  PZ,max = VZ × (Vin − VZ) ÷ R

    Worked example

    A 5.1 V Zener must supply a load of up to 20 mA from a supply that never falls below 12 V, keeping at least 5 mA in the Zener:

    • Voltage across the resistor = 12 − 5.1 = 6.9 V
    • R = 6.9 ÷ (20 + 5) mA = 6.9 ÷ 0.025 A = 276 Ω
    • Resistor power = 6.9² ÷ 276 = 172.5 mW
    • Zener power with the load removed = 5.1 × 6.9 ÷ 276 = 127.5 mW

    Choose the next lower E24 value, 270 Ω, so the Zener current only rises: it becomes 25.6 mA in total, leaving 5.6 mA in the Zener at full load. With 270 Ω the resistor dissipates 176 mW and the unloaded Zener 130 mW, so 0.5 W parts give a safe 2 to 3 times margin.

    Common Zener voltages (E24 series)

    2.7, 3.3, 3.6, 3.9, 4.7, 5.1, 5.6, 6.2, 6.8, 8.2, 9.1, 10, 12, 15, 18, 24 V. Popular packages: BZX55/BZX79 (0.5 W) and 1N47xx (1 W).

    Other uses

    • Overvoltage clamps on sensor inputs.
    • Reference voltages for comparators.
    • Intrinsic safety barriers use Zeners to limit voltage into hazardous areas.

    Design for the highest input voltage too

    The resistor is sized at the lowest input voltage, but the Zener dissipates the most at the highest input with no load. If the 12 V supply in the example can rise to 14 V, the 270 Ω resistor passes 33.0 mA, the unloaded Zener dissipates 168 mW and the resistor 293 mW. Always check the power at the maximum input, and derate the Zener above 25 °C ambient as its datasheet shows (often about 4 mW per °C for a 0.5 W glass part).

    How much the output really moves

    A Zener is not a perfect voltage source: above its knee it behaves like the rated voltage in series with a small dynamic resistance rz. With the 270 Ω resistor from the example and a typical rz of 10 Ω:

    • Load regulation: when the load goes from 0 to 20 mA, the output falls by about IL × (rz ∥ R) = 20 mA × 9.64 Ω = 193 mV
    • Line regulation: a change in input appears at the output scaled by rz ÷ (R + rz) = 3.6%, so a 2 V rise in supply lifts the output by about 71 mV
    Dynamic resistance rz Output drop, 0 to 20 mA load Line regulation Output rise for +2 V input
    5 Ω 98 mV 1.8% 36 mV
    10 Ω 193 mV 3.6% 71 mV
    20 Ω 372 mV 6.9% 138 mV
    30 Ω 540 mV 10.0% 200 mV

    Read rz (often written ZZT) from the datasheet at the test current; it rises sharply near the knee, so a starved Zener regulates far worse than these figures. To measure it, set two Zener currents such as 5 mA and 15 mA with a bench supply and divide the change in Zener voltage by the change in current.

    If 0.2 V of load regulation is too much, buffer the Zener with an NPN emitter follower. The Zener then only supplies the base current, about 0.2 mA for a gain of 100, so the series resistor rises to roughly 1327 Ω and the load change seen by the Zener falls to about 2 mV, at the cost of the transistor’s 0.6 to 0.7 V base emitter drop.

    Practical notes

    • Regulation is modest. The Zener’s dynamic resistance (typically 5 to 30 Ω for small parts) means the output moves by tens of millivolts as the load current changes. Add an emitter follower transistor or use a regulator IC for tighter control.
    • Choose the Zener test current. The datasheet voltage is quoted at a test current, often 5 mA for 0.5 W parts. Running far below it gives a lower, softer voltage.
    • Add a capacitor. A 100 nF to 10 µF capacitor across the Zener reduces its noise, which is significant in avalanche diodes above about 6 V.

    Common mistakes

    • Sizing at the nominal input voltage. If the supply sags below the design value, the Zener current falls to zero and the output drops out of regulation.
    • Rounding the resistor up. A higher standard value reduces the current and can starve the Zener at full load; round down instead.
    • Forgetting the no-load case. When the load is disconnected all the current flows into the Zener, which is when it overheats.
    • Using a Zener for large currents. Above roughly 50 mA the wasted power grows quickly; a linear regulator or a switching converter is more efficient.
    • Ignoring tolerance. A 5.1 V Zener with 5% tolerance can be anywhere from about 4.85 to 5.36 V, which matters when it is used as a reference.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I calculate the Zener series resistor?

    R = (V_in − V_z) ÷ (I_load + I_z,min), using the lowest input voltage and highest load current so the Zener never drops out of regulation.

    How much power does the Zener dissipate?

    The most when the load is disconnected — then all the resistor current flows through the Zener. P = V_z × (V_in − V_z) ÷ R.

    When should I use a regulator IC instead?

    For more than a few tens of milliamps, or when efficiency matters. Zener regulators waste power and regulate less tightly than ICs.

    Why does Zener voltage change with temperature?

    Zeners below about 5 V have a negative temperature coefficient and those above have a positive one; around 5.1–5.6 V the effects nearly cancel.

    What wattage Zener diode do I need?

    At least twice the no-load Zener power at the highest input voltage. In the example, 130 mW at 12 V rises to 168 mW at 14 V, so a 0.5 W Zener is suitable and a 0.25 W part is not.

    Can I connect two Zener diodes in series?

    Yes, two Zeners in series give the sum of their voltages and share the power, for example 5.1 V plus 6.8 V for 11.9 V. Do not connect Zeners in parallel to share current, because the one with the lower voltage takes nearly all of it.

    How much does the output of a Zener regulator change with load?

    About the load current change times the Zener dynamic resistance in parallel with the series resistor. With 270 Ω and r<sub>z</sub> = 10 Ω, going from no load to 20 mA lowers a 5.1 V output by roughly 193 mV.
