## How to use the earthing resistance calculator

Enter the **soil resistivity** (measured with a Wenner four-pin test, or estimated from the table), and the **length** and **diameter** of the rod or pipe. The calculator gives the resistance of a single electrode.

For more background, read [Earthing in Industrial Process Plants](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/earthing-aspects-in-industrial-process-plants/).

## The formula (Dwight)

<p class="formula">R = ρ ÷ (2πL) × [ln(8L ÷ d) − 1]</p>

## Soil resistivity

| Soil | Typical resistivity (Ω·m) |
|---|---|
| Marshy ground, clay with moisture | 2–50 |
| Loam and garden soil | 5–100 |
| Clay, sandy clay | 20–200 |
| Sand and gravel | 100–1,000 |
| Dry sand | 500–5,000 |
| Rock | 1,000–10,000+ |

Resistivity rises sharply when soil dries out or freezes, so measure in the dry season where possible.

## Single rod resistance (16 mm rod)

| Length | 50 Ω·m | 100 Ω·m | 300 Ω·m |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.2 m | 35.8 Ω | 71.6 Ω | 214.7 Ω |
| 2 m | 23.5 Ω | 47.0 Ω | 141.0 Ω |
| 3 m | 16.7 Ω | 33.5 Ω | 100.5 Ω |
| 4.5 m | 11.9 Ω | 23.8 Ω | 71.3 Ω |
| 6 m | 9.3 Ω | 18.6 Ω | 55.8 Ω |

## Multiple rods

Rods in parallel do not divide resistance perfectly because their resistance areas overlap. Spaced at least one rod length apart, two rods give about 55–60% of one rod's resistance, three about 40%, and four about 33%. Closer spacing gives less improvement.

## Testing

Measure earth resistance with the fall-of-potential (three-pole) method, with the current spike at least 5–10 times the electrode length away. Clamp-on testers work only where there is a parallel return path.

## Worked example with the default values

A 3 m rod of 16 mm diameter in soil of 100 Ω·m:

1. ρ ÷ (2πL) = 100 ÷ (2π × 3) = **5.305 Ω**
2. 8L ÷ d = 24 ÷ 0.016 = 1,500, and ln(1,500) = **7.313**
3. R = 5.305 × (7.313 − 1) = **33.5 Ω**

That is well above a typical 10 Ω target. Using the spacing factors above, two rods give about 19.4 Ω, three about 13.4 Ω and four about 11.1 Ω. A single rod would need to be about **12.3 m** long to reach 10 Ω on its own, which is why deep driven rods with couplers are common in poor soil.

## How much the diameter helps

At 3 m length in 100 Ω·m soil:

| Rod diameter | Resistance | Change from 16 mm |
|---|---|---|
| 12.7 mm | 34.7 Ω | +3.7% |
| 16 mm | 33.5 Ω | +0.0% |
| 20 mm | 32.3 Ω | -3.5% |
| 25 mm | 31.1 Ω | -7.1% |
| 40 mm | 28.6 Ω | -14.5% |

Going from 16 mm to 40 mm pipe saves only a few ohms; the thicker rod is chosen for mechanical strength and corrosion life, not resistance.

## Measuring soil resistivity first

A Wenner four-pin survey drives four probes in a line at equal spacing a and passes current between the outer pair. Soil resistivity is ρ = 2πaR. With a = 3 m and a meter reading of 5.3 Ω, ρ = 2π × 3 × 5.3 = **100 Ω·m**. The probe spacing roughly equals the depth sampled, so repeat at 1.5, 3 and 6 m to see whether a deeper rod will reach better ground.

## Common mistakes

- **Designing on wet-season resistivity.** If the soil dries to 200 Ω·m, the same rod rises to 67.0 Ω, double the value measured after rain.
- **Spacing rods too close.** Rods 1 m apart share most of their resistance area; keep spacing at least equal to the rod length.
- **Counting the rod alone in a TT system.** One 33.5 Ω rod passes only about 6.9 A at 230 V, far too little to trip a fuse or MCB, so an RCD is essential.
- **Testing with the test spike too close.** If the current probe is inside the rod's resistance area the reading is low; check by moving the potential probe 10% either side of 62% of the distance.
- **Measuring without disconnecting the earth link.** The parallel path through the supply earth makes a poor electrode look good.
