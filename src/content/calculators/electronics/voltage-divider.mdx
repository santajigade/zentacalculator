## How to use the voltage divider calculator

Enter the **input voltage** and the two resistors (**R1** on top, **R2** to ground). You get the output voltage, the current through the divider and the power it wastes. To design a divider, enter the target output and one resistor and press × on the other.

## Voltage divider formula

<p class="formula">V<sub>out</sub> = V<sub>in</sub> × R₂ ÷ (R₁ + R₂)</p>

## Common divider ratios

| R1 | R2 | Ratio | 12 V in | 24 V in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 k | 10 k | 0.500 | 6.00 V | 12.00 V |
| 10 k | 4.7 k | 0.320 | 3.84 V | 7.67 V |
| 10 k | 3.3 k | 0.248 | 2.98 V | 5.95 V |
| 22 k | 10 k | 0.312 | 3.75 V | 7.50 V |
| 39 k | 10 k | 0.204 | 2.45 V | 4.90 V |
| 62 k | 10 k | 0.139 | 1.67 V | 3.33 V |
| 100 k | 10 k | 0.091 | 1.09 V | 2.18 V |

## Loading effect

A load resistance R<sub>L</sub> across R2 acts in parallel with it, reducing the output. Keep R<sub>L</sub> at least ten times R2, or buffer the output with an op-amp [voltage follower](/electronics/op-amp-gain/).

## Choosing resistance values

- **Lower values** (1–10 kΩ): stiffer output, more current wasted.
- **Higher values** (100 kΩ–1 MΩ): low power for battery circuits, but more sensitive to noise and ADC input current.

## Measuring with a divider

Dividers scale high voltages for ADCs and meters. Use 1% or better resistors with low temperature coefficients for accurate measurement, and make sure each resistor's voltage rating is not exceeded.

## Worked example with the default values

12 V across R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 = 4.7 kΩ:

- Total resistance = 14.7 kΩ, so I = 12 ÷ 14,700 = **0.816 mA**
- V<sub>out</sub> = 12 × 4.7 ÷ 14.7 = **3.837 V**
- Power = 12 × 0.816 mA = **9.80 mW**, of which R1 takes 6.66 mW and R2 3.13 mW

Even the larger share is tiny, so 0.125 W parts are fine here. Seen from the output, the divider behaves like a 3.837 V source with an internal resistance of R1 ∥ R2 = **3.20 kΩ**, which is the number that decides how much a load pulls it down.

## How much a load pulls the output down

Using that 3.20 kΩ source resistance:

| Load across R2 | Output | Change |
|---|---|---|
| 4.7 kΩ | 2.283 V | -40.49% |
| 47.0 kΩ | 3.592 V | -6.37% |
| 100.0 kΩ | 3.718 V | -3.10% |
| 1,000.0 kΩ | 3.825 V | -0.32% |
| 10,000.0 kΩ | 3.836 V | -0.03% |

The common rule of keeping the load at ten times R2 (47 kΩ here) still costs -6.4%. For a measurement input, aim for a load at least 100 to 1,000 times the source resistance, or buffer it.

## Resistor tolerance

With 1% resistors the worst case is R1 low and R2 high, or the reverse. The default divider can then give anything from 3.785 V to 3.889 V, about ±1.4%. Ratio errors do not cancel unless both resistors come from the same matched network.

## In practice

When feeding a microcontroller ADC, the sampling capacitor charges through the source resistance on every conversion; many datasheets ask for 10 kΩ or less, which the 3.20 kΩ default meets. Scaling both resistors up ten times to 100 kΩ and 47 kΩ cuts the current to 82 µA, but then a 10 to 100 nF capacitor from the output to ground is needed to hold the voltage during sampling. Check a built divider by measuring V<sub>out</sub> with the load connected, not with the meter alone.

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping R1 and R2.** The output is taken across the bottom resistor; reversing them gives 12 × 10 ÷ 14.7 = 8.16 V, which could damage a 3.3 V input.
- **Powering a load from a divider.** Any load current changes the output, and the resistors waste power continuously.
- **Ignoring resistor voltage rating.** A small SMD resistor is often rated around 50 to 200 V, so a mains or high voltage divider needs several resistors in series.
- **Leaving a divider on a battery permanently.** Even a fraction of a milliamp drains a small battery over months; switch it with a MOSFET or use megohm values.
- **Using very high values near noise sources.** Megohm dividers pick up interference and leakage, so add a filter capacitor across R2.
