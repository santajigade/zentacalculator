## How to use the inclined plane calculator

Enter the **mass**, the **angle** of the slope and the **coefficient of friction** (use 0 for a frictionless slope). You get the gravity component along the slope, the normal force, the friction force, the acceleration of a sliding object and the force needed to push it up the slope at constant speed.

## Inclined plane formulas

<p class="formula">F<sub>∥</sub> = m g sin θ &nbsp;·&nbsp; N = m g cos θ &nbsp;·&nbsp; F<sub>f</sub> = μ N</p>

<p class="formula">a = g (sin θ − μ cos θ) &nbsp;·&nbsp; F<sub>up</sub> = m g (sin θ + μ cos θ)</p>

## Worked example

A 10 kg block on a 30° slope with μ = 0.2 (g = 9.80665 m/s², sin 30° = 0.5, cos 30° = 0.8660):

- F<sub>∥</sub> = 10 × 9.807 × 0.5 = **49.03 N**
- N = 10 × 9.807 × 0.866 = **84.93 N**, friction = **16.99 N**
- a = 9.807 × (0.5 − 0.2 × 0.866) = **3.205 m/s²** down the slope
- Push up at constant speed = **66.02 N**

## Acceleration by angle (m/s²)

| Angle | No friction | μ = 0.2 |
|---|---|---|
| 5° | 0.855 | -1.099 |
| 10° | 1.703 | -0.229 |
| 15° | 2.538 | 0.644 |
| 20° | 3.354 | 1.511 |
| 30° | 4.903 | 3.205 |
| 45° | 6.934 | 5.547 |
| 60° | 8.493 | 7.512 |

Negative values mean friction prevents sliding.

### Following the block down the slope

If the block starts from rest and slides 2 m along the incline at 3.205 m/s², it reaches v = √(2 a L) = **3.58 m/s** after t = √(2 L ÷ a) = **1.12 s**. It has dropped 1.0 m vertically, and without friction it would arrive at √(2 g h) = 4.43 m/s. The difference is the 34.0 J that friction turned into heat over the 2 m (friction force 16.99 N × 2 m).

## Trading force for distance on a ramp

A ramp lowers the force but not the work. Raising the same 10 kg load by 0.5 m with μ = 0.2:

| Ramp angle | Ramp length | Push force | Work done |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10° | 2.88 m | 36.3 N | 104.6 J |
| 20° | 1.46 m | 52.0 N | 76.0 J |
| 30° | 1.00 m | 66.0 N | 66.0 J |
| 45° | 0.71 m | 83.2 N | 58.8 J |

Lifting straight up needs 98.1 N and 49.0 J. Every ramp needs less force, but friction makes the total work higher, and the shallowest ramp wastes the most because the load rubs over the longest distance.

## Measuring the friction coefficient

Put the block on a board and raise one end slowly until it just starts to slide. The angle at that moment gives the static coefficient, μ<sub>s</sub> = tan θ. Keep tilting until the block slides at a steady speed after a light tap: tan θ at that angle is the kinetic coefficient this calculator uses. Repose angles for a few coefficients are μ = 0.1: 5.7°, 0.3: 16.7°, 0.5: 26.6° and 0.8: 38.7°.

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping sin and cos.** The pull along the slope uses sin θ and the normal force uses cos θ; at 0° the parallel force must be zero.
- **Using the static coefficient for a sliding block.** Static friction only sets whether the block starts to move; once it slides, use the kinetic value, which is usually lower.
- **Reading a negative acceleration as motion uphill.** It means friction would stop the block; a block at rest simply stays put.
- **Entering the slope as a percentage grade.** A 30% grade is arctan 0.30 = 16.7°, not 30°.
- **Forgetting the friction direction when pushing up.** Friction always opposes the motion, so it adds to the push uphill and reduces the pull when lowering.
