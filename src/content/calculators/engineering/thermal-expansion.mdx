## How to use the thermal expansion calculator

Choose the **material**, then enter the **length** and **temperature change**. The expansion is shown in mm or inches. A negative temperature change gives contraction.

## Thermal expansion formula

<p class="formula">ΔL = α × L × ΔT</p>

## Worked example

A 50 m carbon steel steam line heated from 20 °C to 80 °C (ΔT = 60 K):

ΔL = 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 50 × 60 = 0.036 m = **36 mm**

The same run in HDPE would grow about **600 mm** — plastic pipes need much more allowance.

Plastic pipes expand about **10 times** more than steel for the same temperature change.

## Volume expansion of liquids

Liquids expand in volume: ΔV = β × V × ΔT. Water's β is about 0.00021 per K at 20 °C, rising to about 0.0006 near 80 °C. A 1,000 L closed heating system warmed from 10 °C to 80 °C expands by roughly 25–30 L, which is why an expansion vessel is needed.

## Thermal stress

If expansion is prevented, stress builds up:

<p class="formula">σ = E × α × ΔT</p>

For steel (E = 200 GPa, α = 12 × 10⁻⁶), a 50 K rise in a fully restrained pipe creates 120 MPa of stress — a large share of the yield strength of common steels.

## Bimetal strips

Two metals with different α bonded together bend when heated. Thermostats, circuit breakers and dial thermometers use this effect.

## Allowing for expansion

Long hot lines need expansion loops, bellows or offsets, and guides and anchors placed to direct the movement. Restraining expansion completely creates very large forces that can damage supports, flanges and connected equipment.

## Design for the full temperature range

Pipe stress work uses the whole swing from the coldest to the hottest condition, not the difference from installation. Take an 80 m carbon steel line installed at 10 °C, operating at 180 °C and shut down in winter at −5 °C:

- Growth from installation to operating: 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 80 × 170 = **163.2 mm**
- Shrinkage from installation to winter shutdown: 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 80 × 15 = **14.4 mm**
- Total movement the loop or bellows must take: **177.6 mm**

Contraction matters as much as growth for plastics. A 50 m HDPE water main laid in summer and cooled by 30 K shortens by **300 mm** (enter ΔT = −30), which is enough to pull a push-fit joint apart if the pipe is not anchored or snaked in the trench. For steam lines, the layout of loops and anchors is covered in [pipe expansion loops in steam piping](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/pipe-expansion-loop-in-steam-piping/).

## Force from a fully restrained pipe

If the default 60 K rise is fully prevented, the stress is σ = E α ΔT = 200,000 MPa × 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 60 = **144 MPa**, independent of the pipe length. For a DN100 schedule 40 steel pipe (114.3 mm OD, 102.26 mm ID, metal area 2,048 mm²) that stress pushes on the anchors with about **295 kN**. Anchors and nozzle connections are rarely designed for such loads, which is why the movement is allowed to happen and guided instead.

## How expansion is measured

Coefficients are measured with a push rod dilatometer (ASTM E228), which heats a small sample in a furnace and records its length change with a displacement transducer, or with interferometry for low expansion materials such as glass and Invar. On a running plant, movement is checked with travel indicators on spring hangers and with marks on pipe shoes at guides, read cold and hot. The readings are compared with the stress analysis; a shoe that has walked off its support or a hanger at the end of its travel points to a blocked guide or a missing anchor.

## Common mistakes

- **Using installation to operating ΔT only.** The lowest ambient or shutdown temperature adds contraction, and the loop must absorb the full range.
- **Taking α at room temperature for a hot line.** The mean coefficient of carbon steel rises from about 11.7 × 10⁻⁶ near 20 °C to about 13 × 10⁻⁶ at 300 °C, so tables of mean α to the operating temperature give better answers for steam and hot oil.
- **Converting a °F difference with the temperature formula.** A 100 °F rise is 55.6 K, not 37.8 °C.
- **Treating plastics like metal.** HDPE moves more than 16 times as much as carbon steel and also creeps, so supports and anchors follow different rules.
- **Ignoring differential expansion.** A stainless liner in a carbon steel shell, or an aluminium cover on a steel frame, moves by the difference in α, which loosens bolts or bows the assembly.
