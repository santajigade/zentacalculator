## How to use the ellipse calculator

Enter the **semi-major axis** (half the longest width) and the **semi-minor axis** (half the shortest width). You get the area, perimeter, eccentricity and the distance from the centre to each focus.

## Ellipse formulas

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Area | π a b |
| Perimeter (Ramanujan) | π [3(a + b) − √((3a + b)(a + 3b))] |
| Eccentricity | √(1 − b² ÷ a²) |
| Focus distance | √(a² − b²) |

## Example

a = 10 cm, b = 6 cm:

- Area = π × 10 × 6 = **188.50 cm²**
- Perimeter ≈ **51.05 cm**
- Eccentricity = **0.8000**

When b = a, the ellipse is a circle and the eccentricity is 0.

## Planet orbits

Planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus. Earth's orbit has an eccentricity of about 0.017, almost a circle. Halley's Comet has about 0.967, a long, thin ellipse.

## Drawing an ellipse

Push two pins into a board at the foci, loop a string of length 2a around them and trace with a pencil keeping the string tight. This is the gardener's method for oval flower beds.

## Worked example with the default values

For a = 10 cm and b = 6 cm:

1. Area = π × 10 × 6 = **188.50 cm²**
2. Perimeter: 3(a + b) = 48; √((3a + b)(a + 3b)) = √(36 × 28) = 31.7490; P = π × (48 − 31.7490) = **51.05 cm**
3. Eccentricity = √(1 − 6² ÷ 10²) = √(0.64) = **0.8000**
4. Centre to each focus = √(10² − 6²) = √64 = **8.00 cm**, so the foci are 16 cm apart.

The exact perimeter, found by numerical integration of the elliptic integral, is 51.0540 cm, so Ramanujan's result is off by only 0.00002 cm.

## How accurate are the perimeter shortcuts?

| 8 | 56.723 | 56.723 | 0.0000% | 56.549 | 0.31% |
| 6 | 51.054 | 51.054 | 0.0000% | 50.265 | 1.54% |
| 4 | 46.026 | 46.026 | 0.0013% | 43.982 | 4.44% |

Ramanujan's formula stays within a small fraction of a percent even for a very flat ellipse, while π(a + b) underestimates by several percent once b drops below about half of a. For extreme shapes his second formula, π(a + b)[1 + 3h ÷ (10 + √(4 − 3h))] with h = ((a − b) ÷ (a + b))², is better still: at b = 1 it gives 40.6393 against the exact 40.6397.

## Practical example: an oval dining table

A table top 1.8 m long and 1 m wide has a = 0.9 m and b = 0.5 m. Its area is π × 0.9 × 0.5 = **1.414 m²**, about 78.5% of the 1.80 m² rectangle it is cut from (always π ÷ 4). The edge banding needed is the perimeter, **4.488 m**; order a little extra for the joint and trimming. The same steps size an oval rug, a pond liner outline or an elliptical manway gasket.

## Ellipses in orbits

With the Sun at one focus, a planet's closest distance is a(1 − e) and its farthest is a(1 + e). For Earth, a = 149.6 million km and e = 0.0167, giving about 147.1 million km at perihelion and 152.1 million km at aphelion.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering full axes instead of semi-axes.** A 1.8 m by 1.0 m oval has a = 0.9 m and b = 0.5 m; using the full widths gives four times the area.
- **Swapping a and b.** a must be the longer semi-axis. With b larger than a, the eccentricity and focus distance become meaningless.
- **Using 2π√((a² + b²) ÷ 2) as exact.** That common shortcut gives 51.81 cm for the default ellipse, 1.49% high; it is only an estimate.
- **Treating any oval as an ellipse.** Egg shapes and racetrack ovals (two semicircles joined by straight lines) have different area and perimeter formulas.
