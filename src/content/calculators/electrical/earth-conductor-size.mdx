## How to use the earth conductor size calculator

Enter the **prospective earth fault current**, the protective device's **disconnection time** at that current, and choose the **conductor and insulation** type. The result is the minimum cross-section; select the next standard size up.

For more background, read [Earth Fault Loop Impedance (Zs) Testing](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/earth-fault-loop-impedance-zs-testing/).

## The adiabatic equation

<p class="formula">S = √(I² × t) ÷ k</p>

- **S** = cross-section in mm²
- **I** = fault current in A (rms)
- **t** = disconnection time in seconds
- **k** = material factor

| 2 kA | 4.4 mm² | 8.8 mm² | 14.0 mm² | 31 mm² |
| 5 kA | 11.1 mm² | 22.1 mm² | 35.0 mm² | 78 mm² |
| 10 kA | 22.1 mm² | 44.2 mm² | 69.9 mm² | 156 mm² |
| 20 kA | 44.2 mm² | 88.5 mm² | 139.9 mm² | 313 mm² |
| 31.5 kA | 69.7 mm² | 139.3 mm² | 220.3 mm² | 493 mm² |

## Standard sizes

1.5, 2.5, 4, 6, 10, 16, 25, 35, 50, 70, 95, 120, 150, 185, 240 and 300 mm².

## Simplified method (IEC 60364-5-54 Table 54.2)

| Line conductor S | Protective conductor (same material) |
|---|---|
| S ≤ 16 mm² | S |
| 16 < S ≤ 35 mm² | 16 mm² |
| S > 35 mm² | S ÷ 2 |

## Check the earth fault loop too

A conductor large enough thermally must also have low enough impedance for the protective device to trip within the required time. Verify the earth fault loop impedance Zs on site.

## Worked example with the default values

A 10 kA earth fault is cleared in 0.4 s and the protective conductor is a separate PVC insulated copper wire (k = 143):

- I²t = 10,000² × 0.4 = 4.0 × 10⁷ A²s
- S = √(4.0 × 10⁷) ÷ 143 = 6,325 ÷ 143 = **44.2 mm²**
- Next standard size: **50 mm²**

The same fault needs a different size for each conductor and insulation:

| Conductor | k | Minimum S | Standard size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copper, separate, PVC | 143 | 44.2 mm² | 50 mm² |
| Copper, separate, XLPE/EPR | 176 | 35.9 mm² | 50 mm² |
| Copper, core of PVC cable | 115 | 55.0 mm² | 70 mm² |
| Bare copper, no fire risk | 159 | 39.8 mm² | 50 mm² |
| Aluminium, separate, PVC | 95 | 66.6 mm² | 70 mm² |
| Aluminium, separate, XLPE | 116 | 54.5 mm² | 70 mm² |
| Steel, separate, PVC | 52 | 121.6 mm² | 150 mm² |

## Where k comes from

IEC 60364-5-54 Annex A derives k from the conductor's heat capacity, resistivity and the allowed temperature rise: k = √[Q<sub>c</sub>(B + 20) ÷ ρ<sub>20</sub> × ln((B + θ<sub>f</sub>) ÷ (B + θ<sub>i</sub>))]. For copper (Q<sub>c</sub> = 3.45 × 10⁻³ J/°C mm³, B = 234.5 °C, ρ<sub>20</sub> = 17.241 × 10⁻⁶ Ω mm) and PVC with a final 160 °C, starting at 30 °C gives **143** for a separate conductor, while starting at 70 °C, because a core inside a loaded cable is already warm, gives **115**. That is why the same copper has two very different k values.

## Lower fault current is not always easier

The equation rewards a short disconnection time much more than a low current. A 3 kA fault that a slow fuse clears in 2 s needs √(3,000² × 2) ÷ 143 = **29.7 mm²**, not far below the 10 kA case, and at even lower currents the device may not trip in time at all. Always read t from the device's time current curve at the actual earth fault current. For devices that clear in under 0.1 s, such as MCBs and current limiting fuses in their instantaneous region, use the let through I²t from the manufacturer instead of I² × t.

## Checking it on site

After installation the protective conductor is verified by a continuity test (R1 + R2) and an earth fault loop impedance test. The measured Zs gives the real fault current, I = U<sub>0</sub> ÷ Z<sub>s</sub>, which should be at least the value used in the sizing so the trip time assumed in the calculation holds.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the three phase fault level.** The earth fault current is usually lower because of the earth return impedance, which can mean a longer trip time.
- **Taking the breaker's nominal 0.4 s instead of its curve.** The real clearing time at the calculated current may be longer or much shorter.
- **Choosing k for a separate conductor when it is a cable core.** A core in a PVC cable has k = 115, needing about 24% more copper than k = 143.
- **Rounding down to the nearest size.** 44.2 mm² means 50 mm², not 35 mm².
- **Ignoring steel armour and conduit k values.** Steel has k around 46 to 52, so armour used as the protective conductor needs about three times the copper area.
