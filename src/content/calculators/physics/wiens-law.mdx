## How to use the Wien's law calculator

Enter the **temperature** of the radiating body (switch the unit to °C or °F if you prefer). The calculator returns the peak wavelength in nanometres and micrometres and the peak frequency of the spectrum. To estimate a temperature, enter the peak wavelength and clear the temperature field.

## Wien's displacement law formula

<p class="formula">λ<sub>max</sub> = b ÷ T &nbsp;·&nbsp; b = 2.897771955 × 10⁻³ m·K</p>

<p class="formula">ν<sub>max</sub> = 5.878925757 × 10¹⁰ Hz/K × T</p>

## Example

The Sun's photosphere is about 5,778 K:

- λ<sub>max</sub> = 2.897772 × 10⁻³ ÷ 5778 = **501.5 nm** (blue-green light)
- ν<sub>max</sub> = 5.879 × 10¹⁰ × 5778 = **339.7 THz** — a wavelength of 883 nm in the per-frequency spectrum

## Notes

- Only ideal black bodies follow the law exactly; real surfaces have emissivity below 1, but their peak position is usually close.
- The total power radiated rises with T⁴ — see the [Stefan–Boltzmann law calculator](/physics/stefan-boltzmann-law/).
- To convert the peak wavelength to photon energy, use the [photon energy calculator](/physics/photon-energy/).

## What the Sun's peak means

At the default 5,778 K the peak sits at 501.5 nm, and a photon at that wavelength carries E = 1,239.84 ÷ 501.5 = **2.47 eV**. The per-frequency peak of 339.7 THz corresponds to 883 nm, in the near infrared, which shows how much the "peak" depends on how the spectrum is plotted. Integrating Planck's law shows that only **25.0%** of a black body's power is emitted at wavelengths shorter than λ<sub>max</sub>; the long tail toward the infrared carries the other 75.0%. The Sun looks white rather than green because its spectrum is broad and our eyes respond to the whole visible band.

## Choosing the band for an infrared thermometer

Industrial pyrometers are built around the shift in peak wavelength with process temperature:

| Process temperature | Absolute | Peak wavelength |
|---|---|---|
| 100 °C | 373.15 K | 7.77 µm |
| 300 °C | 573.15 K | 5.06 µm |
| 500 °C | 773.15 K | 3.75 µm |
| 800 °C | 1,073.15 K | 2.70 µm |
| 1,000 °C | 1,273.15 K | 2.28 µm |
| 1,200 °C | 1,473.15 K | 1.97 µm |
| 1,500 °C | 1,773.15 K | 1.63 µm |

General purpose handheld thermometers work in the 8 to 14 µm atmospheric window, which suits objects from below 0 °C to a few hundred °C. Instruments for molten metal and glass use short bands near 1 µm or 1.6 µm, on the short-wavelength side of the peak. There the signal changes very steeply with temperature, so an error in the emissivity setting causes a much smaller temperature error. Ratio (two-colour) pyrometers compare two nearby short wavelengths to cancel emissivity and dust effects.

## Sensitivity to measurement error

Since T = b ÷ λ<sub>max</sub>, a relative error in the peak wavelength gives the same relative error in temperature. Locating the solar peak 1% too long would put the temperature about 58 K too low. Broad, flat peaks make the exact maximum hard to find, which is why astronomers usually fit the whole spectrum or use colour indices instead.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering degrees Celsius as kelvin.** 1,000 °C is 1,273.15 K; treating it as 1,000 K shifts the peak from 2.28 µm to 2.90 µm.
- **Mixing the wavelength and frequency peaks.** Converting ν<sub>max</sub> with λ = c ÷ ν gives about 1.76 × λ<sub>max</sub>, not the same wavelength.
- **Assuming the visible colour is the peak colour.** A body with a peak in the green still looks white.
- **Applying it to line sources.** Neon signs, LEDs and sodium lamps emit lines, not a thermal spectrum, so their colour gives no temperature.
- **Measuring through glass.** Ordinary window glass blocks most radiation beyond about 4 to 5 µm, so a thermal camera cannot see a room-temperature object behind it.
