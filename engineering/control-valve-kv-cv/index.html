    The flow coefficient, Kv in metric units or Cv in US units, tells you how much liquid a valve passes at a given pressure drop. This control valve Kv / Cv calculator solves for flow, pressure drop or the coefficient, whichever you leave blank. Read about the control valve flow coefficient.

    Instrument and process engineers use it to size a new valve, check whether an installed valve can pass more flow, and apply the basic requirements for control valve sizing before ordering.

    How to use the control valve kv / cv calculator

    Fill in any three of flow, pressure drop, specific gravity and Kv (or Cv). The rest is calculated:

    • Sizing: enter flow, ΔP and SG → required Kv and Cv.
    • Checking capacity: enter the valve’s Kv and the available ΔP → maximum flow.
    • Pressure drop: enter Kv and flow → ΔP across the valve.

    For more background, read Control Valve Rangeability and Turndown.

    The formulas (liquids)

    Q = Kv × √(ΔP ÷ SG) (Q in m³/h, ΔP in bar)
    Cv = 1.156 × Kv

    The US form is Q (gpm) = Cv × √(ΔP (psi) ÷ SG). Units are converted for you.

    Worked examples

    Required Kv: 10 m³/h of water with 1 bar drop → Kv = 10 × √(1 ÷ 1) = 10 m³/h, Cv = 11.56.

    Capacity check: a valve with Kv 10 on a liquid of SG 0.85 with only 0.5 bar available passes 10 × √(0.5 ÷ 0.85) = 7.67 m³/h.

    Valve makers often offer Kv values in a preferred-number series (1, 1.6, 2.5, 4, 6.3, 10…).

    Valve authority

    A control valve controls well only if it takes a reasonable share of the system pressure drop:

    Authority = ΔPvalve ÷ (ΔPvalve + ΔPrest of system)

    Aim for an authority of about 0.3–0.5. Below about 0.25 the valve has little control over flow until it is nearly closed.

    Choked flow and cavitation

    At high pressure drops, liquid can flash or cavitate inside the valve, limiting flow and damaging the trim. The simple Kv formula assumes no choking. For high ΔP, check the maximum allowable pressure drop using the valve’s liquid pressure recovery factor (FL) per IEC 60534.

    Gases and steam

    For gases and steam, the Kv formula is different and depends on inlet pressure, temperature and whether flow is critical. Use the manufacturer’s sizing tool or IEC 60534-2-1 for gas and steam valves.

    Sizing tips

    • Size for the pressure drop at the valve, not the whole system.
    • Aim for the valve to be 60–80% open at normal flow and below about 90% at maximum flow.
    • Check for cavitation and flashing when ΔP is a large share of inlet pressure.
    • Remember the installed characteristic: as flow rises, pipe losses rise and the valve’s ΔP falls.

    Worked example: sizing a cooling water valve

    The calculator opens with 10 m³/h of water (SG 1) across a 1 bar drop, which gives Kv = 10 × √(1 ÷ 1) = 10.00 m³/h and Cv = 1.156 × 10 = 11.56.

    A more realistic duty: a heat exchanger needs 45 m³/h of cooling water at maximum load, and the pump curve and pipe losses leave 0.8 bar for the valve.

    • Required Kv = 45 ÷ √0.8 = 50.31 m³/h, so Cv = 58.2
    • Check in US units: 45 m³/h is 198.1 gpm and 0.8 bar is 11.60 psi, so Cv = 198.1 ÷ √11.60 = 58.2, the same answer
    • The next valve in the preferred series is Kvs 63, which passes 63 × √0.8 = 56.3 m³/h fully open
    • With a linear trim the valve would sit near 80% open at maximum load, inside the usual control band

    If the loop is later filled with 30% glycol (SG about 1.04), the same flow needs Kv = 45 ÷ √(0.8 ÷ 1.04) = 51.31, only 2% more, so the selected valve still has margin.

    How Kv is measured

    Valve makers find Kv on a flow test rig, following IEC 60534-2-3 or ANSI/ISA-75.02. Water between 5 and 40 °C flows through the valve at set travel positions, with pressure taps 2 pipe diameters upstream and 6 diameters downstream. The measured flow and pressure drop give Kv at each opening, and the value at rated travel is published as Kvs (or rated Cv). The full curve of Kv against travel is the inherent characteristic: linear, equal percentage or quick opening.

    Common mistakes

    • Using the whole system pressure drop. Only the drop across the valve body belongs in the formula; pipe, exchanger and strainer losses are separate.
    • Picking the valve size from the line size. A valve sized by Kv is often one or two sizes smaller than the pipe, with reducers either side.
    • Treating the calculated Kv as the Kvs to order. The calculated figure is what the duty needs; the catalogue Kvs must be larger so the valve still controls.
    • Leaving SG at 1 for brine, glycol or oil. Denser liquids need a larger Kv for the same flow and pressure drop.
    • Mixing m³/h with litres per minute. Kv is defined in m³/h; 100 L/min is 6 m³/h, not 100.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is Kv?

    Kv is the flow of water in m³/h through a valve with a 1 bar pressure drop. It is the metric flow coefficient.

    What is Cv?

    Cv is the flow of water in US gallons per minute with a 1 psi pressure drop. Cv = 1.156 × Kv.

    How much margin should I add?

    A common rule is to select a valve whose rated Kv is about 1.25–1.5 times the calculated value, so it controls at roughly 60–80% open at normal flow.

    Does this work for gas or steam?

    No. Gases and steam need compressible-flow equations. This calculator is for non-flashing, non-cavitating liquids.

    What is the difference between Kv and Cv?

    Kv is flow in m³/h of water at 1 bar pressure drop. Cv is flow in US gpm of water at 1 psi drop. Cv = 1.156 × Kv.

    What Kv should I select?

    Calculate the Kv needed at maximum flow, then pick a valve whose rated Kv is roughly 1.25–1.5 times larger, so the valve works mostly between 20% and 80% open.

    What is the difference between Kv and Kvs?

    Kv is the flow coefficient at any valve opening, while Kvs is the value at full rated travel published in the catalogue. You calculate the Kv your duty needs, then choose a valve whose Kvs is larger.

    Can I size a glycol or brine valve with this calculator?

    Yes, as long as the liquid does not flash or cavitate. Enter its specific gravity: 45 m³/h at 0.8 bar needs Kv 50.3 for water but 51.3 for a glycol mix of SG 1.04.
