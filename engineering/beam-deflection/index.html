    How much a beam sags depends on the load, the cube of its span and its flexural stiffness E·I. This beam deflection calculator gives the maximum deflection and bending moment for a simply supported beam with a central point load and for a cantilever loaded at its tip.

    Structural engineers, fabricators and students use it for quick serviceability checks, and the same elastic bending is what a beam-type load cell uses to measure force.

    How to use the beam deflection calculator

    Enter the point load, the span, the material’s Young’s modulus and the section’s second moment of area I in cm⁴ (from a steel table or the area moment of inertia calculator). The calculator returns the maximum deflection and bending moment for a simply supported beam loaded at mid-span and for a cantilever loaded at its tip. Self-weight is not included.

    Beam deflection formulas

    Simply supported, centre load: δ = P L³ ÷ (48 E I)  ·  M = P L ÷ 4

    Cantilever, end load: δ = P L³ ÷ (3 E I)  ·  M = P L

    Example

    A steel IPE 200 (I = 1,943 cm⁴, E = 200 GPa) spanning 3 m with a 5 kN load:

    • EI = 200 × 10⁹ × 1,943 × 10⁻⁸ = 3.89 × 10⁶ N·m²
    • Simply supported: δ = 5,000 × 3³ ÷ (48 × 3.89 × 10⁶) = 0.72 mm (span/4,145); M = 5 × 3 ÷ 4 = 3.75 kN·m
    • As a 3 m cantilever: δ = 5,000 × 3³ ÷ (3 × 3.89 × 10⁶) = 11.6 mm; M = 15 kN·m

    Notes

    • Check the bending stress as well as deflection — use the bending stress calculator with the moment from this page.
    • These formulas assume small, elastic deflections of a straight, uniform beam.

    Adding the beam’s own weight

    The calculator covers the point load only. An IPE 200 weighs about 22.4 kg/m, a uniform load of 220 N/m. Over the default 3 m simple span it adds 5wL⁴ ÷ (384EI) = 0.060 mm, so the total mid-span sag is about 0.78 mm. Because the maximum deflections of both cases occur at mid-span, they can simply be added (superposition holds for elastic, small deflections).

    Second example: a timber floor joist

    A 75 × 200 mm softwood joist (I = b h³ ÷ 12 = 5,000 cm⁴, E = 11,000 MPa) spans 3.6 m with a 2 kN point load at mid-span:

    • δ = 2,000 × 3.6³ ÷ (48 × 11 × 10⁹ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ m⁴) = 3.5 mm
    • The L/360 limit for a floor is 10 mm, so the joist passes for this load
    • Laid flat (I = 703 cm⁴) the same joist would sag 25 mm, which shows why joists are always set on edge

    Enter E = 11,000 MPa and I = 5,000 cm⁴ to reproduce it.

    The load column comes from P = 48EIδ ÷ L³; bending stress usually governs long before these loads are reached, so check both.

    How deflection is measured

    On a load test the beam is loaded in steps, often with water bags or calibrated weights, and the mid-span sag is read with a dial gauge or LVDT on a stand fixed to the ground, not to the structure. Support settlement is measured at each end and subtracted. Recovery is checked after unloading: a beam that does not return close to zero has yielded or slipped at its connections.

    Common mistakes

    • Entering I in mm⁴ or m⁴. The field expects cm⁴; 1 cm⁴ = 10,000 mm⁴, so a value from a mm⁴ table is 10,000 times too large.
    • Using the strong axis for a load that bends the weak axis. An IPE 200 has Iy ≈ 142 cm⁴ about its minor axis, 14 times less stiff.
    • Using the clear span instead of the span between bearing centres. Because δ grows with L³, a 5% longer span gives about 16% more deflection.
    • Ignoring long term creep in timber and concrete. Sustained loads can double timber deflection over time; design codes apply a creep factor.
    • Treating a partly fixed end as fully fixed. Bolted end plates rarely give full fixity, so the PL³ ÷ (192EI) case is optimistic.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the deflection formula for a simply supported beam?

    With a point load P at mid-span, δ = PL³ ÷ (48EI). For a uniformly distributed load w per metre, δ = 5wL⁴ ÷ (384EI). Both occur at mid-span.

    What is the deflection of a cantilever beam?

    With a point load P at the free end, δ = PL³ ÷ (3EI) — 16 times the simply supported value for the same span. With a uniform load w, δ = wL⁴ ÷ (8EI).

    What deflection limit should a beam meet?

    Typical serviceability limits are span/360 for floors with brittle finishes, span/250 for general floors and span/180 for roofs. Check your design code.

    What is EI?

    The flexural rigidity — Young's modulus of the material times the second moment of area of the cross-section. Doubling the depth of a rectangular beam increases I, and the stiffness, eightfold.

    How do I include a uniformly distributed load and a point load together?

    Work out each deflection separately and add them, because both peak at mid-span on a simply supported beam. For the default IPE 200 the 5 kN point load gives 0.72 mm and its own weight adds 0.060 mm.

    How do I find I for a rectangular timber or steel section?

    Use I = b h³ ÷ 12 with h the depth in the direction of bending. A 75 × 200 mm joist on edge has 5,000 cm⁴; laid flat it has only 703 cm⁴.
