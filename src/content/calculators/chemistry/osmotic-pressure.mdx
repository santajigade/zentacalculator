## How to use the osmotic pressure calculator

Enter the **van 't Hoff factor**, **molarity** and **temperature**. The osmotic pressure appears in atm, bar, kPa or psi.

## Osmotic pressure formula

<p class="formula">π = i M R T</p>

## Worked example: saline at body temperature

0.15 M NaCl (i = 2) at 37 °C (310.15 K):

π = 2 × 150 mol/m³ × 8.314 × 310.15 = 774 kPa = **7.64 atm**

This is close to the osmotic pressure of blood, which is why 0.9% saline is used for IV fluids.

## Osmotic pressure of common solutions at 37 °C

| Solution | i × c (osmol/L) | Osmotic pressure |
|---|---|---|
| 0.9% saline (0.154 M NaCl) | 0.308 | 7.84 atm |
| 5% glucose (0.278 M) | 0.278 | 7.08 atm |
| Seawater (approx.) | 1.1 | 28.00 atm |
| 0.1 M sucrose | 0.1 | 2.55 atm |

## Isotonic, hypotonic and hypertonic

| Term | Compared with cells | Effect on red blood cells |
|---|---|---|
| Isotonic | Same osmolarity (about 0.29 osmol/L) | No change |
| Hypotonic | Lower | Swell and may burst |
| Hypertonic | Higher | Shrink |

0.9% saline and 5% glucose are approximately isotonic, which is why they are used for intravenous drips.

## Reverse osmosis

Desalination plants push seawater through a membrane at a pressure higher than its osmotic pressure (about 27 atm), forcing water through and leaving the salt behind. Typical operating pressures are 55–80 bar.

## The van 't Hoff factor

i is the number of particles each formula unit produces in solution: about 1 for sugars, about 2 for NaCl, about 3 for CaCl₂. Real values are slightly lower because of ion pairing.

## Finding molar mass of proteins

Osmotic pressure can measure molar masses of large molecules: M = mass ÷ (π × V ÷ RT). Even dilute protein solutions give measurable pressures.

Suppose 2.00 g of a purified protein is dissolved to make 100.0 mL of solution at 25 °C, and in a membrane osmometer the solution column rises 7.60 cm above the pure water side. Taking the solution density as 1.00 g/cm³:

- π = ρ g h = 1000 × 9.80665 × 0.0760 = **745.3 Pa**
- Particle concentration = π ÷ RT = 745.3 ÷ (8.314 × 298.15) = **0.3007 mol/m³**
- Mass concentration = 2.00 g ÷ 0.1000 L = 20.0 g/L = 20.0 kg/m³
- M = 20.0 ÷ 0.3007 = **66.5 kg/mol**, or about 66,522 g/mol, close to haemoglobin

The same 20 g/L of NaCl would give about 17.0 bar, over 2,000 times more, because each gram contains far more particles. The protein would lower the freezing point by only 0.56 mK, which no routine instrument can read, so membrane osmometry is the practical method for macromolecules.

## The default result in every unit

With the default inputs the calculator works in SI units: c = 0.15 mol/L = 150 mol/m³ and T = 37 + 273.15 = 310.15 K, so RT = 2578.7 J/mol.

- π = 2 × 150 × 2578.7 = **773,619 Pa** = 773.6 kPa
- = **7.635 atm** = 7.736 bar = 112.2 psi = 5,803 mmHg

That pressure would hold up a column of water about 79 m tall, which shows how strongly a membrane pulls water towards dissolved salt. In real 0.15 M NaCl the ions do not act fully independently: the osmotic coefficient is about 0.93, so the measured value is nearer 7.10 atm, equivalent to about 279 mOsm/L. That is why 0.9% saline sits close to plasma (275 to 295 mOsm/kg) even though the ideal formula gives slightly more.

## How osmotic pressure is measured in practice

Hospital and research labs rarely measure π directly. A freezing point osmometer supercools a sample of a few tens to a few hundred microlitres and reads the depression, ΔT<sub>f</sub> = K<sub>f</sub> × osmolality with K<sub>f</sub> = 1.86 K·kg/mol for water. Normal serum at 290 mOsm/kg freezes at about −0.539 °C, and converting that osmolality with π = c R T gives 7.38 atm at body temperature. Vapour pressure osmometers work on the same particle count through the dew point, and membrane osmometers, which do read π as a liquid head, are used for polymers and proteins.

## Ideal osmotic pressure of salt solutions at 25 °C

Values use i = 2 and molarity from NaCl at 58.44 g/mol. They set the minimum pressure a reverse osmosis pump must exceed before any permeate flows.

| NaCl content | Molarity | Osmolarity | Ideal π |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 g/L (Fresh to slightly brackish) | 0.0171 mol/L | 34 mOsm/L | 0.85 bar |
| 5 g/L (Brackish groundwater) | 0.0856 mol/L | 171 mOsm/L | 4.24 bar |
| 10 g/L (Strong brackish) | 0.1711 mol/L | 342 mOsm/L | 8.48 bar |
| 35 g/L (Seawater strength) | 0.5989 mol/L | 1198 mOsm/L | 29.69 bar |
| 70 g/L (RO brine reject) | 1.1978 mol/L | 2396 mOsm/L | 59.39 bar |

Natural seawater measures about 27 bar rather than the ideal 29.7 bar because of its osmotic coefficient and mix of ions. As water is removed the brine concentrates, so an RO train has to work against the reject concentration, not the feed.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the Celsius number in a hand calculation.** Putting 37 in place of 310.15 K gives 0.911 atm, about eight times too low. The calculator converts for you, but written work must use kelvin.
- **Pairing R = 8.314 with mol/L.** That R needs mol/m³, so multiply molarity by 1000, or use R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) to get atm directly.
- **Treating i as an exact whole number.** Ion pairing makes the effective factor for NaCl about 1.86 at physiological strength and smaller still in strong brines.
- **Counting solutes that cross the membrane.** Urea, ethanol and glycerol pass through cell membranes, so they raise osmolarity without raising tonicity; only impermeant solutes pull water.
- **Applying π = iMRT to concentrated solutions.** The relation is a dilute limit; above about 1 mol/L use molality and measured osmotic coefficients.
