## How to use the capacitors in series and parallel calculator

Choose **series** or **parallel** and enter up to four capacitor values. Enter **0** for any capacitor you are not using. The result is the equivalent capacitance.

## Capacitors in series and parallel formulas

<p class="formula">Parallel: C = C₁ + C₂ + C₃ + … &nbsp;·&nbsp; Series: 1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + 1/C₃ + …</p>

For two capacitors in series: C = C₁C₂ ÷ (C₁ + C₂).

## Examples

| Capacitors | Series | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| 10 µF + 10 µF | 5 µF | 20 µF |
| 10 µF + 22 µF | 6.88 µF | 32 µF |
| 100 nF + 100 nF + 100 nF | 33.3 nF | 300 nF |
| 1 µF + 100 nF | 90.9 nF | 1.1 µF |

## Worked example

A 10 µF and a 22 µF capacitor are connected in series across **50 V**:

- 1/C = 1/10 + 1/22 = 0.14545 per µF, so **C = 6.875 µF** (or 10 × 22 ÷ (10 + 22))
- Both capacitors carry the same charge: Q = 6.875 µF × 50 V = **343.8 µC**
- Voltage on the 10 µF part = Q ÷ C1 = **34.4 V**, and on the 22 µF part **15.6 V**

The smaller capacitor takes the larger share: the 10 µF part needs a rating above 34.4 V (a 50 V part), while the 22 µF part would manage with 25 V. Connected in parallel instead, the same two parts give **32 µF** and each sees the full 50 V.

## Voltage ratings

- **Parallel**: every capacitor sees the full voltage, so the lowest voltage rating applies.
- **Series**: voltage divides in inverse proportion to capacitance — the smallest capacitor takes the most voltage. For equal capacitors, ratings add, but add equal balancing resistors across each one.

## Compared with resistors

Capacitors in parallel behave like resistors in series, and vice versa. See the [resistors in series and parallel calculator](/physics/resistors-series-parallel/).

## Equal capacitors in series and parallel

Identical capacitors divide the capacitance by the number in series and multiply the voltage rating; in parallel the capacitance multiplies and the rating stays the same:

| Bank | Series C | Series rating | Parallel C | Parallel rating |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 × 100 µF, 50 V | 50.0 µF | 100 V | 200 µF | 50 V |
| 3 × 100 µF, 50 V | 33.3 µF | 150 V | 300 µF | 50 V |
| 4 × 100 µF, 50 V | 25.0 µF | 200 V | 400 µF | 50 V |

## Practical notes

- **Balancing resistors.** Leakage current, not capacitance, sets the DC voltage split in a series stack over time. A resistor of equal value across each capacitor, passing roughly 5 to 10 times the leakage current, keeps the voltages equal.
- **Electrolytic tolerance.** Aluminium electrolytics are often ±20%, so two nominally equal parts in series may share the voltage 55:45. Leave margin on the rating.
- **Parallel for low ESR.** Several smaller capacitors in parallel have lower ESR and ESL than one large one, which is why decoupling and power supply output stages use banks of them.
- **Mixed values for decoupling.** Paralleling a 100 nF ceramic with a 10 µF capacitor covers a wider frequency range than either alone.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding series capacitors directly.** Two 10 µF capacitors in series make 5 µF, not 20 µF.
- **Assuming the voltage splits equally between unequal capacitors.** It splits in inverse proportion to capacitance, so the smallest part sees the most voltage.
- **Using the highest voltage rating in a parallel bank.** All parallel parts see the full voltage, so the lowest rating limits the bank.
- **Mixing units.** Entering one value in nF and another in µF without converting gives an answer off by a factor of 1,000.
- **Putting polarised electrolytics in series the wrong way.** For a non-polar pair, connect them back to back (negative to negative), and remember that only half the capacitance remains.

## Reactance of the combination on AC

On AC each capacitor behaves as a reactance X<sub>C</sub> = 1 ÷ (2πfC). Series reactances add like series resistors, so the series pair always has more reactance than either part. For the default 10 µF and 22 µF pair:

| Frequency | 10 µF alone | 22 µF alone | Series (6.875 µF) | Parallel (32 µF) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 Hz | 318.31 Ω | 144.69 Ω | 463.00 Ω | 99.472 Ω |
| 120 Hz | 132.63 Ω | 60.29 Ω | 192.92 Ω | 41.447 Ω |
| 1 kHz | 15.92 Ω | 7.23 Ω | 23.15 Ω | 4.974 Ω |
| 10 kHz | 1.59 Ω | 0.72 Ω | 2.31 Ω | 0.497 Ω |
| 100 kHz | 0.16 Ω | 0.07 Ω | 0.23 Ω | 0.050 Ω |

At 50 Hz, 318.3 Ω + 144.7 Ω = 463.0 Ω, which is exactly the reactance of a single 6.875 µF capacitor. This is a quick way to check a series result by hand.

## Tolerance on the total

With ±20% electrolytics the default series pair can measure anywhere from **5.50 µF** (both parts low) to **8.25 µF** (both high). The voltage split is worse: if the 10 µF part is 20% low and the 22 µF part 20% high, the 10 µF part sees **38.4 V** of the 50 V instead of 34.4 V. Measure the parts with an LCR meter before building a series stack for high voltage.
