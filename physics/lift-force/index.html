    Lift is the aerodynamic force a wing produces at right angles to the oncoming air, and it grows with air density, wing area, lift coefficient and the square of speed. This lift force calculator applies L = ½ρv²SC_L and converts the result to the mass it can hold up.

    Pilots, aeronautics students, drone builders and model aircraft designers use it to size wings, estimate stall and take-off speeds and see how altitude or heavy loads change performance.

    How to use the lift force calculator

    Enter the air density, the airspeed, the wing (reference) area and the lift coefficient. The calculator returns the lift force and the mass that lift can hold up against gravity. To find the speed needed to fly, enter the mass and clear the airspeed. The same equation works for hydrofoils — use water’s density.

    Lift equation

    L = ½ ρ v² S CL  ·  m = L ÷ g

    Example

    A light aircraft with 16 m² of wing flies at 50 m/s (97 knots) in sea-level air with CL = 0.5:

    • L = 0.5 × 1.225 × 50² × 16 × 0.5 = 12,250 N
    • Mass supported = 12,250 ÷ 9.80665 = 1,249 kg

    Air density and lift with altitude (ISA)

    Same wing, speed and CL as the example; the last column is the true airspeed that restores sea-level lift.

    Notes

    • CL rises roughly linearly with angle of attack (about 0.1 per degree for a typical wing) until the stall.
    • Lift always comes with induced drag; estimate the parasitic part with the drag force calculator.
    • Use true airspeed, not indicated airspeed, with the actual air density.

    Dynamic pressure: the part the pilot controls

    The product ½ρv² is the dynamic pressure q, the quantity a pitot-static system actually senses. With the default inputs q = 0.5 × 1.225 × 50² = 1,531.25 Pa. Lift is then simply q × S × CL = 1,531.25 × 16 × 0.5 = 12,250 N. In a wind tunnel the process runs backwards: a force balance measures L, the tunnel instruments give q, and the lift coefficient is found as CL = L ÷ (q S). That is how published airfoil and wing CL curves are produced.

    Speed needed to carry 1,249 kg at different lift coefficients

    Holding lift at the default 12,250 N in sea-level air, the wing must fly slower as CL (angle of attack) rises:

    CL Airspeed for level flight In knots
    0.3 64.5 m/s 125 kn
    0.5 50.0 m/s 97 kn
    0.8 39.5 m/s 77 kn
    1 35.4 m/s 69 kn
    1.2 32.3 m/s 63 kn
    1.5 28.9 m/s 56 kn

    Taking CL,max = 1.5 for the clean wing gives a stall speed of 28.9 m/s; with flaps raising CL,max to 2.1 it drops to 24.4 m/s. Lift-off is normally planned at about 1.1 to 1.2 times the stall speed in the take-off configuration.

    Extra lift needed in a turn

    In a level turn the lift vector tilts, so the wing must produce W ÷ cos φ to keep the vertical component equal to the weight. The ratio is the load factor n, and the stall speed rises with √n:

    Bank angle Load factor n Lift required Clean stall speed
    0° 1.000 12,250 N 28.9 m/s
    15° 1.035 12,682 N 29.4 m/s
    30° 1.155 14,145 N 31.0 m/s
    45° 1.414 17,324 N 34.3 m/s
    60° 2.000 24,500 N 40.8 m/s

    At 60° of bank the wing carries 2 g, so a turn flown near the stall speed in level flight will stall long before the bank is reached.

    Common mistakes

    • Using knots or km/h as m/s. 50 knots is only 25.7 m/s, and because speed is squared the lift comes out almost four times too high.
    • Using the wrong reference area. S is the planform wing area quoted by the manufacturer, not the wetted surface area of both sides of the wing.
    • Using a 2D airfoil coefficient for the whole wing. Section data (cl) from airfoil catalogues is higher than the finite wing CL, especially for low aspect ratio wings.
    • Assuming CL keeps rising with angle of attack. Past the stall the flow separates and CL falls, so the equation with a large CL gives lift the wing cannot make.
    • Forgetting that lift must exceed weight in manoeuvres. Turns and pull-ups need load factors above 1, as the bank table shows.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the lift equation?

    L = ½ ρ v² S C_L, where ρ is air density, v the true airspeed, S the wing area and C_L the lift coefficient. Doubling the speed quadruples the lift.

    What is a typical lift coefficient?

    Cruise values are about 0.3–0.6. A clean wing reaches a maximum of roughly 1.2–1.6 just before stalling, and flaps raise C_L,max to about 2–3.

    How do I find the take-off or stall speed?

    Set the lift equal to the weight and solve v = √(2mg ÷ (ρ S C_L,max)). Enter the mass, clear the airspeed field and the calculator solves for it.

    Why do aircraft need longer runways at high altitude?

    Thinner air means less lift at the same speed, so the aircraft must reach a higher true airspeed — about 16% higher at 3,000 m — before it can lift off.

    What is dynamic pressure and how does it relate to lift?

    Dynamic pressure is q = ½ρv², the pressure rise when moving air is brought to rest. Lift is q × S × C_L. At 50 m/s in sea-level air q is 1,531 Pa, so each square metre of wing at C_L = 0.5 carries 766 N.

    How much more lift does a wing need in a banked turn?

    Lift must equal weight ÷ cos(bank angle). At 30° of bank that is 1.155 times the weight, and at 60° it is twice the weight, which also raises the stall speed by √2, about 41%.
