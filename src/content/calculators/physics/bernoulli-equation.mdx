  - q: What is Bernoulli's equation?
    a: P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant along a streamline. P is static pressure, ½ρv² the dynamic pressure and ρgh the hydrostatic term, all in pascals (J/m³).
  - q: Why does pressure drop when velocity increases?
    a: Energy is conserved. If the fluid speeds up through a narrower section, its kinetic energy rises, so its pressure energy must fall — the principle behind venturi meters, carburettors and aspirators.
  - q: When is Bernoulli's equation not valid?
    a: It assumes steady, incompressible, inviscid flow with no pumps or turbines between the points. For real pipes add the friction head loss, and for gases above about Mach 0.3 use compressible-flow relations.

    a: The Bernoulli sum expressed as a height of fluid — pressure head P/ρg plus velocity head v²/2g plus elevation z. Without losses it is the same at every point.

## How to use the Bernoulli equation calculator

Enter the **fluid density**, the **pressure**, **velocity** and **elevation** at point 1, and the velocity and elevation at point 2. The calculator returns the pressure at point 2 and the total head of the flow. To find a velocity or height instead, enter P2 and clear the field you want. Use absolute or gauge pressure consistently at both points.

## Bernoulli equation formula

<p class="formula">P<sub>1</sub> + ½ρv<sub>1</sub>² + ρgh<sub>1</sub> = P<sub>2</sub> + ½ρv<sub>2</sub>² + ρgh<sub>2</sub></p>

<p class="formula">H = P ÷ (ρg) + v² ÷ (2g) + h</p>

## Example

Water (1,000 kg/m³) flows at 2 m/s and 200 kPa at ground level, then rises 3 m into a narrower pipe where it moves at 5 m/s:

- Velocity term: ½ × 1000 × (2² − 5²) = **−10.5 kPa**
- Elevation term: 1000 × 9.80665 × (0 − 3) = **−29.42 kPa**
- P<sub>2</sub> = 200 − 10.5 − 29.42 = **160.08 kPa**
- Total head H = 20.394 + 0.204 + 0 = **20.598 m**

## Checking the example with heads

Bernoulli's equation can be read as energy per unit weight. At point 1 the pressure head is 200 kPa ÷ (1,000 × 9.80665) = 20.394 m and the velocity head is 2² ÷ (2 × 9.80665) = 0.204 m, so H = 20.598 m. At point 2 the pressure head is 16.324 m, the velocity head 1.275 m and the elevation 3 m, which add back to **20.598 m**. If your two totals differ, an input or unit is wrong. P<sub>2</sub> = 160.08 kPa is also 23.22 psi.

## Where the second velocity comes from

In a real pipe v<sub>2</sub> is set by continuity, v<sub>1</sub>A<sub>1</sub> = v<sub>2</sub>A<sub>2</sub>. If point 1 is a 100 mm bore carrying 15.71 L/s, a speed of 5 m/s at point 2 means a bore of 100 × √(2 ÷ 5) = **63.2 mm**. Work out the speed from the diameters first, then use this calculator for the pressure.

## Measuring the terms in practice

Static pressure is read with a gauge or transmitter on a wall tapping flush with the pipe bore, never one that pokes into the stream. Elevation differences come from drawings or a level survey of the tapping points. Velocity is found from the volumetric flow rate divided by the bore area, or with a pitot tube that senses static plus dynamic pressure. A differential pressure transmitter across two tappings measures P<sub>1</sub> − P<sub>2</sub> directly, which is how venturi and orifice meters turn this equation into a flow reading.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing gauge and absolute pressure.** Either works, but both points must use the same reference.
- **Getting the elevation sign wrong.** Point 2 above point 1 lowers P<sub>2</sub>; measure both heights from one datum.
- **Applying it across a pump or turbine.** Add the pump head or subtract the turbine head, or the result is meaningless.
- **Picking v<sub>2</sub> freely.** It is fixed by the flow rate and bore, so check it with continuity.
- **Ignoring friction on long runs.** Over many metres of pipe the head loss can be bigger than the velocity term.

## Practical notes

- Real pipe flow loses head to friction and fittings: P<sub>2</sub> is lower than this ideal value by ρg·h<sub>L</sub>. Estimate h<sub>L</sub> with the [Darcy–Weisbach calculator](/engineering/darcy-weisbach-head-loss/).
- Flow meters such as the [venturi](/engineering/venturi-flow/) and the [pitot tube](/engineering/pitot-tube-velocity/) are direct applications of Bernoulli's equation.
- If P<sub>2</sub> falls below the liquid's vapour pressure, the liquid flashes or cavitates and the equation no longer applies.
