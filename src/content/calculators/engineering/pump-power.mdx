## How to use the pump power calculator

Enter the **flow**, **total head**, **density** and **pump efficiency** to get the hydraulic and shaft power. You can also solve for the flow or head a pump can deliver with a given motor.

## Pump power formulas

<p class="formula">P<sub>h</sub> = ρ g Q H   P<sub>shaft</sub> = P<sub>h</sub> ÷ η</p>

## Worked example

50 m³/h of water against 30 m head, pump efficiency 70%:

- Q = 0.01389 m³/s
- P<sub>h</sub> = 998 × 9.81 × 0.01389 × 30 = **4.08 kW**
- P<sub>shaft</sub> = 4.08 ÷ 0.70 = **5.83 kW** → a **7.5 kW** motor is a typical choice

## Typical pump efficiencies

| Pump type and size | Efficiency |
|---|---|
| Small centrifugal (< 5 kW) | 40 – 65% |
| Medium centrifugal | 65 – 80% |
| Large centrifugal | 80 – 90% |
| Positive displacement | 70 – 90% |

## Motor selection

Select a motor larger than the shaft power, to cover end-of-curve operation and fluid density changes:

| Shaft power | Typical service factor | Motor size |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 7.5 kW | +20–25% | Next standard size up |
| 7.5–22 kW | +15% | Next standard size up |
| Above 22 kW | +10% | Next standard size up |

Standard IEC motor sizes include 0.75, 1.1, 1.5, 2.2, 3, 4, 5.5, 7.5, 11, 15, 18.5, 22, 30, 37, 45 and 55 kW.

## Electrical input power

<p class="formula">P<sub>electrical</sub> = P<sub>shaft</sub> ÷ η<sub>motor</sub></p>

A 6 kW shaft load on a 90% efficient motor draws about 6.67 kW. Over 8,000 hours a year at 0.12 per kWh, that is about **6,400** in electricity, often more than the pump's purchase price.

## Affinity laws

For a centrifugal pump at different speeds:

| Quantity | Changes with speed |
|---|---|
| Flow | ∝ n |
| Head | ∝ n² |
| Power | ∝ n³ |

Reducing speed by 20% with a VFD cuts power to about 51% — a large energy saving compared with throttling a valve.

## NPSH

To avoid cavitation, the net positive suction head available (NPSHa) must exceed the pump's required NPSH (NPSHr), with a margin of at least 0.5–1 m.

## Finding total head

Total head = static lift + pressure difference (as head) + friction losses. Use the [Darcy–Weisbach calculator](/engineering/darcy-weisbach-head-loss/) for the pipe friction part. To convert pressure to head, 1 bar ≈ 10.2 m of water.

## Default result in detail

With the defaults (50 m³/h, 30 m, 998 kg/m³, 70%), the calculator works in SI units: Q = 50 ÷ 3,600 = 0.01389 m³/s, and with g = 9.80665 m/s²:

- P<sub>h</sub> = 998 × 9.80665 × 0.01389 × 30 = **4.078 kW**
- P<sub>shaft</sub> = 4.078 ÷ 0.70 = **5.826 kW** (7.81 hp)

With a 90% efficient motor the electrical input is 6.47 kW. Running 6,000 hours a year at 8 per kWh, the pump uses 38,837 kWh and costs about **310,700** a year to run. If the same pump handles brine at 1,200 kg/m³ at the same flow and head, shaft power rises in proportion to density, to 7.00 kW, so check the motor for the densest fluid it will see.

## Checking a pump in the field

On site you rarely know the head directly; you measure it. Fit gauges on the suction and discharge flanges, read the flow from a flowmeter, and log the motor input with a power analyser. In a test with suction at −0.3 bar g, discharge at 2.6 bar g, the discharge gauge 0.5 m above the suction gauge and 45 m³/h of water:

- Head = (2.6 − (−0.3)) × 10⁵ ÷ (998 × 9.80665) + 0.5 = **30.13 m**
- Hydraulic power = 998 × 9.80665 × 0.0125 × 30.13 = **3.69 kW**
- With 5.9 kW measured at the motor terminals, wire-to-water efficiency is **62.5%**

Compare this with the curve value at the measured flow. A drop of more than 5 to 10 points usually means impeller wear, internal recirculation or a worn wear ring. One bar of pressure on water is 10.22 m of head, a useful figure when reading gauges.

## Common mistakes

- **Using pressure instead of head for a dense liquid.** Head in metres of liquid is what the pump curve shows; convert pressure with the actual density, not the water figure.
- **Leaving flow in m³/h.** The formula needs m³/s; forgetting to divide by 3,600 makes the power 3,600 times too large.
- **Sizing on the duty point only.** A centrifugal pump running further out on its curve at low system head draws more power than at duty; check the end-of-curve power.
- **Taking motor nameplate kW as power drawn.** The nameplate is the shaft output rating; a lightly loaded motor draws much less, and efficiency falls at low load.
- **Forgetting viscosity.** Oils above about 20 cSt reduce centrifugal pump head and efficiency; use the corrected efficiency, not the water curve.
