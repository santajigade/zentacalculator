## How to use the horizontal tank volume calculator

Enter the tank's **inner diameter**, **straight length** and the **liquid level** measured from the bottom. You get the liquid volume, the total tank volume and the percentage filled. To create a strapping table for a level transmitter, change the level in steps and note each volume — or watch the chart.

For more background, read [Automatic Tank Gauging Explained](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/automatic-tank-gauging-explained/).

## Horizontal tank volume formula

For a horizontal cylinder of radius r = D/2 and level h:

<p class="formula">V = L × [ r² cos⁻¹((r − h) ÷ r) − (r − h) √(2rh − h²) ]</p>

## Worked example

A tank 2 m in diameter and 5 m long with 0.5 m of liquid:

- r = 1 m, r − h = 0.5 m
- Segment area = 1 × cos⁻¹(0.5) − 0.5 × √0.75 = 1.0472 − 0.4330 = 0.6142 m²
- V = 0.6142 × 5 = **3,071 litres** — only **19.6%** full at 25% of the height

## Dished ends

Many horizontal tanks have dished (torispherical or ellipsoidal) heads that add volume beyond the cylindrical shell:

| Head type | Volume of one head (full) |
|---|---|
| 2:1 ellipsoidal | π × D³ ÷ 24 |
| Hemispherical | π × D³ ÷ 12 |
| Flat | 0 |

For a 2 m diameter tank, each 2:1 ellipsoidal head adds about **1.05 m³** when full. Partly filled heads need extra calculation; tank vendors provide strapping tables.

## Strapping tables

A strapping table lists volume at each level, usually every 10 mm or 1 cm. It is used in the DCS or tank gauge to convert level to volume. You can build one quickly with this calculator by stepping the level.

## Level transmitter range

For a level transmitter on a horizontal tank:

- **Range** = 0 to the tank inner diameter (plus any nozzle offset).
- The volume is **not** linear with level, so configure a characterisation table rather than linear scaling.
- At 50% level the tank is exactly 50% full by volume, but at 25% level it is only about 19.6% full.

## Vertical vs horizontal

In a vertical cylinder, volume is proportional to level. A horizontal tank uses floor space differently and has a non-linear level–volume curve, which is why this calculator exists.

## Using it with a level transmitter

A pressure or radar transmitter measures **level**. To display **volume**, the control system needs this non-linear curve (a strapping table). Combine with the [hydrostatic pressure calculator](/engineering/hydrostatic-pressure/) to go from pressure to level first.

## Worked example: diesel day tank

A horizontal day tank has an inner diameter of **1.5 m**, a straight length of **4 m** and a dip reading of **0.45 m**:

- Full shell volume = π × 0.75² × 4 = **7,069 L**
- Liquid volume at 0.45 m = **1,784 L**, which is 25.2% full at 30% of the diameter

The level reads 30%, but the tank is only about a quarter full. This is why a level reading must be converted with this formula or a strapping table before you order fuel.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the outside diameter.** Wall thickness on a large tank can take several percent off the volume. Use the inner diameter.
- **Measuring ullage instead of level.** A dip from the top gives the empty space. Level = diameter − ullage.
- **Ignoring the transmitter offset.** A level transmitter on a side nozzle does not see the bottom of the tank. Add the nozzle height to its reading.
- **Ignoring the tank slope.** A tank that tilts towards a drain gives a different reading at each end. Measure at the middle, or correct for the slope.
- **Forgetting temperature.** Diesel expands about 0.08% per °C, so a full tank holds about 1.6% less fuel at 35 °C than at 15 °C.
