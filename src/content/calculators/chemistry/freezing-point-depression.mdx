## How to use the freezing point depression calculator

Choose the **solvent** (which sets Kf), enter the **van 't Hoff factor** and the **molality** of the solution, and the solvent's normal freezing point. You get the freezing point depression and the new freezing point.

## Freezing point depression formula

<p class="formula">ΔT<sub>f</sub> = i × K<sub>f</sub> × m</p>

- **i** = particles per formula unit (van 't Hoff factor)
- **K<sub>f</sub>** = cryoscopic constant of the solvent (°C·kg/mol)
- **m** = molality (mol solute per kg solvent)

## Salt water freezing points (ideal estimate)

| NaCl (g per kg water) | Molality | Freezing point |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.171 | -0.64 °C |
| 20 | 0.342 | -1.27 °C |
| 35 | 0.599 | -2.23 °C |
| 50 | 0.856 | -3.18 °C |
| 100 | 1.711 | -6.37 °C |

Seawater (about 35 g/kg of salts) actually freezes at about −1.9 °C; the ideal formula slightly overestimates the effect at higher concentrations.

## Van 't Hoff factors

| Solute | Ideal i | Typical measured i (dilute) |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose, sucrose, urea | 1 | 1.0 |
| NaCl, KCl | 2 | 1.8 to 1.9 |
| CaCl₂, MgCl₂ | 3 | 2.5 to 2.7 |
| AlCl₃ | 4 | about 3.2 |

## Uses

- **Road de-icing** with NaCl or CaCl₂ (calcium chloride works to lower temperatures).
- **Antifreeze**: ethylene glycol in car radiators.
- **Ice cream making**: salt and ice reach about −10 to −20 °C to freeze the mix.
- **Molar mass determination** in the lab (cryoscopy).

## Worked example with the default values

Water (K<sub>f</sub> = 1.86 °C·kg/mol), i = 2 for NaCl and a molality of 0.5 mol/kg, which is about 29.2 g of NaCl per kg of water:

- ΔT<sub>f</sub> = 2 × 1.86 × 0.5 = **1.860 °C**
- New freezing point = 0 − 1.86 = **-1.86 °C**

Using the measured i of about 1.85 for NaCl at this concentration gives a depression nearer 1.72 °C, slightly less than the ideal figure.

## Worked example: molar mass by cryoscopy

2.50 g of an unknown non-electrolyte (i = 1) dissolved in 50.0 g of benzene lowers its freezing point by 1.28 °C:

1. Molality m = ΔT<sub>f</sub> ÷ K<sub>f</sub> = 1.28 ÷ 5.12 = **0.2500 mol/kg**
2. Moles of solute = 0.2500 × 0.0500 kg = 0.01250 mol
3. Molar mass = 2.50 ÷ 0.01250 = **200 g/mol**

Solvents with a large K<sub>f</sub>, such as camphor or cyclohexane, give bigger, easier to measure depressions, which is why they are chosen for this method.

## Ethylene glycol antifreeze (ideal estimate)

| Glycol by mass | Molality | Ideal freezing point |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 1.79 | -3.3 °C |
| 20% | 4.03 | -7.5 °C |
| 30% | 6.90 | -12.8 °C |
| 40% | 10.74 | -20.0 °C |

Real mixtures freeze lower than this at higher concentrations (a 40% mix reaches about −24 °C) because glycol solutions deviate from ideal behaviour, so use the coolant maker's chart for engine protection.

## Common mistakes

- **Using molarity instead of molality.** The formula needs moles per kilogram of solvent, not per litre of solution.
- **Forgetting the van 't Hoff factor.** Salts split into ions; NaCl gives about twice the depression of sugar at the same molality.
- **Entering the new freezing point as ΔT<sub>f</sub>.** ΔT<sub>f</sub> is a positive drop; the new freezing point is the normal value minus it.
- **Picking the wrong K<sub>f</sub>.** K<sub>f</sub> belongs to the solvent, not the solute; switching from water to benzene almost triples the effect.
- **Applying it to concentrated brines.** Ion pairing lowers the effective i, so the ideal result overstates the depression above about 1 mol/kg.
