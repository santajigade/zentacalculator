## How to use the RL time constant calculator

Enter the **inductance**, the total **resistance** in the circuit (coil resistance plus any series resistor), the **supply voltage** and a **time** after switching. You get the time constant, the final current and the current at that time for both rising and decaying cases.

## RL circuit formulas

<p class="formula">τ = L ÷ R &nbsp;·&nbsp; i(t)<sub>rise</sub> = (V/R)(1 − e<sup>−t/τ</sup>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; i(t)<sub>decay</sub> = (V/R) e<sup>−t/τ</sup></p>

## Worked example

A 100 mH coil with 50 Ω total resistance is switched onto 24 V:

- τ = 0.1 H ÷ 50 Ω = **2 ms**; final current = 24 ÷ 50 = **0.48 A**
- After t = 2 ms (one time constant) the rising current is 0.48 × (1 − e⁻¹) = **0.3034 A**
- If the coil is instead discharging from 0.48 A, after 2 ms it is 0.48 × e⁻¹ = **0.1766 A**
- It takes about 5τ = **10 ms** to reach full current

## Inductive kick

Interrupting inductor current suddenly creates a voltage spike V = L × di/dt. See the [inductor energy calculator](/electronics/inductor-energy/) for suppression methods.

## Practical notes

- Use the **total** loop resistance — coil resistance plus series resistors and the driver's on-resistance — or τ will be overestimated.
- Iron-cored coils are not linear: inductance changes with current and with the armature position in relays and solenoids, so treat τ as an estimate and measure if timing matters.
- To speed up a slow-releasing relay, add a Zener or resistor in series with the flyback diode; the larger voltage across the coil collapses the current faster.

## Current and voltages over time for the default coil

For the default 100 mH, 50 Ω coil on 24 V, the current starts rising at V ÷ L = **240 A/s** and the inductor voltage falls as the resistor voltage grows:

| Time | Current | Voltage across L | Voltage across R |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 ms | 0.1062 A | 18.69 V | 5.31 V |
| 1 ms | 0.1889 A | 14.56 V | 9.44 V |
| 2 ms | 0.3034 A | 8.83 V | 15.17 V |
| 4 ms | 0.4150 A | 3.25 V | 20.75 V |
| 6 ms | 0.4561 A | 1.19 V | 22.81 V |
| 10 ms | 0.4768 A | 0.16 V | 23.84 V |

At every instant the two voltages add to 24 V. At full current the coil stores ½LI² = ½ × 0.1 × 0.48² = **11.52 mJ**, the energy any suppression circuit must absorb at switch-off.

## Scenario 1: making the coil release faster

With a plain flyback diode the current decays through the coil's own 50 Ω with τ = 2 ms, taking about 3.14 ms to fall to 0.1 A. Adding a 200 Ω resistor in series with the diode raises the loop resistance to 250 Ω, so τ<sub>off</sub> = 0.1 ÷ 250 = **0.4 ms** and the same drop takes only 0.63 ms. The cost is a switch voltage of 24 + 0.48 × 200 = **120 V** at turn-off (plus the diode drop), so the transistor must be rated well above that.

## Scenario 2: forcing a faster rise

Stepper and solenoid drivers speed up the rise by adding series resistance and raising the voltage in proportion. Tripling the supply to 72 V with an extra 100 Ω keeps the final current at 0.48 A but cuts τ to 0.667 ms, so 90% current arrives in 1.54 ms instead of 4.61 ms. The extra resistor wastes 23.0 W continuously, which is why modern drivers use chopper current control instead.

## Measuring τ and L

Put a small sense resistor in series, switch the supply on and watch the current on an oscilloscope: τ is the time to 63.2% of the final value. Then L = τ × R. An LCR meter at 1 kHz gives a quick inductance figure, but for iron-cored coils it can differ from the value at operating current.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing units.** Millihenries divided by ohms give milliseconds directly; using henries with milliseconds gives an answer 1,000 times off.
- **Using the RC formula.** τ = L × R is wrong for inductors; doubling R halves the RL time constant.
- **Assuming a decay path exists.** If the switch simply opens with no diode or snubber, the current cannot follow the exponential curve and the stored energy appears as an arc or a destroyed transistor.
- **Expecting relay pull-in at the final current.** The armature closes at its pull-in current, often well below V/R, and the inductance rises once it closes.
- **Ignoring supply resistance.** A weak supply or long thin cable adds resistance and lowers both τ and the final current.
