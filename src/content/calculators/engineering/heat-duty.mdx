## How to use the heat duty calculator

Enter the **mass flow**, **specific heat** and the **temperature difference** between inlet and outlet. The heat duty is calculated in kW (or hp). You can also find the flow needed to carry a given load, or the temperature rise for a known heater power.

## Heat duty formula

<p class="formula">Q̇ = ṁ × c<sub>p</sub> × ΔT</p>

## Worked example with the default values

A heat exchanger cools 2 kg/s of water (c<sub>p</sub> = 4.186 kJ/kg·K) by 10 K:

1. Q̇ = ṁ × c<sub>p</sub> × ΔT = 2 × 4.186 × 10 = **83.72 kW**
2. In other units: 112.3 hp, 301,392 kJ/h, 285,665 Btu/h or 23.80 TR

The calculator multiplies c<sub>p</sub> by 1,000 to work in J/kg·K, so the raw result is 83,720 W, shown as 83.72 kW. In imperial mode the same inputs read as 15,873 lb/h and an 18 °F difference; the duty does not change, only the units on screen.

## Steam heating

When heating with steam, the steam gives up latent heat as it condenses:

<p class="formula">Steam flow = Heat duty ÷ latent heat</p>

At about 7 bar(g), saturated steam has a latent heat of roughly 2,050 kJ/kg. A 100 kW duty needs about 0.049 kg/s, or **175 kg/h** of steam.

## Log mean temperature difference

To size a heat exchanger, combine duty with the overall heat-transfer coefficient U and area A:

<p class="formula">Q = U × A × LMTD</p>

where LMTD = (ΔT₁ − ΔT₂) ÷ ln(ΔT₁ ÷ ΔT₂), using the temperature differences at each end of the exchanger.

## Cooling towers and chillers

Cooling capacity is often quoted in tons of refrigeration (TR): 1 TR = 3.517 kW. A chiller cooling 10 kg/s of water by 5 K removes 209 kW, about **59.5 TR**.

## Practical uses

- **Chiller and boiler sizing:** enter the design flow and ΔT, then convert the duty to tons with the [chiller tonnage calculator](/engineering/chiller-tonnage/).
- **Energy metering:** BTU and thermal energy meters compute exactly this product from a flow meter and two temperature sensors, usually a matched Pt100 or Pt500 pair.
- **Exchanger sizing:** once the duty is known, the [LMTD calculator](/engineering/lmtd/) gives the driving temperature difference for Q = U × A × LMTD.
- **Steam heating:** for a steam heated exchanger, the [steam consumption calculator](/engineering/steam-consumption/) turns the duty into kg/h of steam.
- **Checking performance:** a measured duty falling below the design value at the same flow points to fouling or a control valve that is not opening fully.

## Example: checking a chilled water energy meter

A chilled water branch carries 36 m³/h of water supplied at 7 °C and returned at 12 °C. At about 10 °C water has a density of 999.7 kg/m³ and c<sub>p</sub> of 4.192 kJ/kg·K:

- Mass flow = 36 × 999.7 ÷ 3,600 = **9.997 kg/s**
- Q = 9.997 × 4.192 × 5 = **209.5 kW**, or 59.6 TR

Because the ΔT is only 5 K, a 0.1 K error between the two temperature sensors changes the duty by 2%. That is why energy meters use matched sensor pairs and why low ΔT loops are hard to bill accurately.

## Example: hot and cold side balance

A thermal oil heater circuit delivers 3 kg/s of oil (c<sub>p</sub> about 2.3 kJ/kg·K) that cools from 220 °C to 180 °C in an exchanger. The oil gives up 3 × 2.3 × 40 = **276 kW**. If that heat goes into 2 kg/s of water, the water warms by 276 ÷ (2 × 4.186) = **33.0 K**. Measured duties on the two sides usually agree within a few percent; a larger gap points to a bad flow or temperature reading, or heat lost through poor insulation.

## Flow needed to carry 100 kW

Volumetric flow in m³/h for each fluid and design temperature difference, from V = Q ÷ (ρ × c<sub>p</sub> × ΔT) with the properties shown (typical values; check your fluid data sheet):

| Fluid | ΔT 5 K | ΔT 10 K | ΔT 15 K | ΔT 20 K |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Water (c<sub>p</sub> 4.186, ρ 998 kg/m³) | 17.2 | 8.6 | 5.7 | 4.3 |
| Glycol 30% (c<sub>p</sub> 3.7, ρ 1040 kg/m³) | 18.7 | 9.4 | 6.2 | 4.7 |
| Thermal oil (c<sub>p</sub> 2.2, ρ 870 kg/m³) | 37.6 | 18.8 | 12.5 | 9.4 |

Lower specific heat and density both push the flow up, so a glycol or oil system needs bigger pumps and pipes for the same load.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering volumetric flow as mass flow.** 2 m³/h is not 2 kg/s; convert with density first (about 0.554 kg/s for water).
- **Mixing kJ and kW.** With mass flow in kg/s and c<sub>p</sub> in kJ/kg·K the result is kW; with kg/h it is kJ/h, 3,600 times larger.
- **Using the water c<sub>p</sub> for glycol mixtures.** A 30% glycol solution has about 12% less heat capacity, so the duty is overstated.
- **Applying the formula across a phase change.** Boiling or condensing duty is mass flow times latent heat, which c<sub>p</sub> × ΔT does not capture.
- **Converting a °F difference like a temperature.** An 18 °F rise is 10 K, not −7.8 °C.
