## How to use the limiting reagent calculator

Balance the equation first. Enter the **mass**, **molar mass** and **coefficient** for each reactant, then the **coefficient** and **molar mass** of the product you want. The reactant with the smaller "moles ÷ coefficient" is limiting — its excess shows as 0 — and the theoretical yield is calculated from it.

## Limiting reagent method

<p class="formula">n = m / M &nbsp;·&nbsp; ξ = min(n<sub>A</sub>/a, n<sub>B</sub>/b) &nbsp;·&nbsp; m<sub>product</sub> = ξ × p × M<sub>product</sub></p>

ξ (the reaction extent) is the number of times the balanced equation can "run" before the limiting reagent is used up.

## Worked example: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

50 g N₂ (28.014 g/mol) and 10 g H₂ (2.016 g/mol):

- N₂: 1.7848 mol ÷ 1 = **1.7848**
- H₂: 4.9603 mol ÷ 3 = **1.6534** → hydrogen is the limiting reagent
- NH₃ = 1.6534 × 2 × 17.031 = **56.32 g**
- N₂ left over = (1.7848 − 1.6534) × 28.014 = **3.68 g**

## Steps by hand

1. Write and balance the chemical equation.
2. Convert every reactant mass to moles with its molar mass.
3. Divide each by its coefficient; the smallest value identifies the limiting reagent.
4. Use that value with the product's coefficient to find moles and grams of product.

Compare the theoretical yield with what you actually recovered using the [percent yield calculator](/chemistry/percent-yield/).

## Changing the hydrogen charge

With 50 g of N₂ fixed, the reactant that limits the ammonia yield depends on how much H₂ is charged. The exact stoichiometric amount is 1.7848 × 3 × 2.016 = **10.79 g** H₂:

| H₂ charged | Limiting reagent | NH₃ theoretical yield | N₂ left | H₂ left |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 g | H₂ | 33.79 g | 22.21 g | 0.00 g |
| 8 g | H₂ | 45.06 g | 12.94 g | 0.00 g |
| 10 g | H₂ | 56.32 g | 3.68 g | 0.00 g |
| 10.79 g | H₂ | 60.77 g | 0.02 g | 0.00 g |
| 12 g | N₂ | 60.79 g | 0.00 g | 1.21 g |
| 15 g | N₂ | 60.79 g | 0.00 g | 4.21 g |

Above the stoichiometric point extra hydrogen does not raise the yield; it only ends up as excess.

## Second example: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Burning 4 g of hydrogen in 40 g of oxygen:

- H₂: 4 ÷ 2.016 ÷ 2 = **0.9921**
- O₂: 40 ÷ 31.998 ÷ 1 = **1.2501**, so hydrogen is limiting even though it is the smaller mass by a factor of ten
- Water formed = 0.9921 × 2 × 18.015 = **35.74 g**
- Oxygen left = (1.2501 − 0.9921) × 31.998 = **8.26 g**

## Percent excess and percent yield

In the default ammonia example nitrogen is supplied at 1.7848 ÷ 1.6534 = 1.079 times the amount needed, which is **7.9% excess** N₂. If the run recovers 48 g of NH₃, the percent yield is 48 ÷ 56.32 × 100 = **85.2%**. Yield is always based on the limiting reagent.

## Why industry uses an excess on purpose

Plants rarely feed reactants in exact proportions. Combustion runs with 10 to 20% excess air so every bit of fuel burns. In esterification an excess of the cheaper alcohol pushes the equilibrium towards the ester. The expensive or hazardous reactant is made the limiting one, so it is used up and does not need recovering.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing grams instead of moles.** The smaller mass is often not limiting, as the hydrogen examples show.
- **Forgetting to divide by the coefficient.** Comparing 1.78 mol N₂ with 4.96 mol H₂ directly would wrongly make N₂ limiting.
- **Using an unbalanced equation.** Every coefficient feeds the result, so balance first and check atoms on both sides.
- **Using atomic instead of molecular mass.** Hydrogen gas is H₂ at 2.016 g/mol, not 1.008; oxygen gas is 31.998 g/mol.
- **Ignoring purity.** A reagent that is 95% pure supplies only 95% of its weighed mass; correct the mass before entering it.
