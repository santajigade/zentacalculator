## How to use the induction motor slip and synchronous speed calculator

Enter the **supply frequency**, choose the **number of poles** and enter the motor's **rated speed** from the nameplate. You get the synchronous speed, slip and rotor current frequency.

For more background, read [Motor Nameplate Details Explained](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/motor-nameplate-details-explained/) and [Synchronous Motor Working Principle](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/synchronous-motor-working-principle/).

## Induction motor slip and synchronous speed formulas

<p class="formula">N<sub>s</sub> = 120 × f ÷ P &nbsp;·&nbsp; s = (N<sub>s</sub> − N<sub>r</sub>) ÷ N<sub>s</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; f<sub>r</sub> = s × f</p>

## Synchronous speeds

| Poles | 50 Hz | 60 Hz | Typical full-load speed (50 Hz) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 3,000 rpm | 3,600 rpm | 2,880–2,970 rpm |
| 4 | 1,500 rpm | 1,800 rpm | 1,420–1,485 rpm |
| 6 | 1,000 rpm | 1,200 rpm | 940–990 rpm |
| 8 | 750 rpm | 900 rpm | 710–740 rpm |
| 10 | 600 rpm | 720 rpm | 570–590 rpm |
| 12 | 500 rpm | 600 rpm | 480–495 rpm |

## Example

A 4-pole motor on 50 Hz with a nameplate speed of 1,460 rpm:

- N<sub>s</sub> = 120 × 50 ÷ 4 = **1,500 rpm**
- Slip = (1,500 − 1,460) ÷ 1,500 = **2.67%**
- Rotor frequency = 0.0267 × 50 = **1.33 Hz**

## What slip tells you

- **Load**: slip rises roughly in proportion to load. A motor running at half its rated slip is lightly loaded — a quick field check of loading.
- **Size**: large, high-efficiency motors have lower slip (around 0.5–1.5%); small motors may have 4–6%.
- **Rotor faults**: broken rotor bars show up as current sidebands at (1 ± 2s) × f in motor current signature analysis.

## Speed control with a VFD

Changing frequency changes synchronous speed: at 25 Hz, a 4-pole motor runs near 750 rpm. Slip in rpm stays roughly constant at a given torque.

## Estimating motor load from slip

Between no load and full load, slip rises almost in a straight line with load. This gives a quick way to estimate how hard a motor is working with only a tachometer:

<p class="formula">Load ≈ (N<sub>s</sub> − N<sub>measured</sub>) ÷ (N<sub>s</sub> − N<sub>nameplate</sub>)</p>

Example: a 4-pole, 50 Hz motor rated at 1,460 rpm runs at **1,480 rpm**. Load ≈ (1,500 − 1,480) ÷ (1,500 − 1,460) = **50%**. Treat it as an estimate: nameplate speed has a tolerance of about ±20% of rated slip, and slip also changes with supply voltage, roughly in proportion to 1 ÷ V².

## 60 Hz motors on 50 Hz (and the reverse)

Synchronous speed scales with frequency. A 4-pole motor rated 1,750 rpm at 60 Hz runs at about 1,450 rpm on 50 Hz. Its voltage must drop in the same ratio (for example 460 V to about 380 V) to keep the flux constant. Shaft power falls with speed.

## Common mistakes

- **Using pole pairs in 120 f ÷ P.** The formula uses the number of poles. With pole pairs it becomes 60 f ÷ p.
- **Mistaking a high slip for a fault.** Small and high-torque motors (NEMA design D) are designed with 5 to 13% slip.
- **Expecting synchronous speed at no load.** Even unloaded, a motor needs a little slip to cover friction and windage losses, usually under 1%.
- **Reading speed under a VFD without the output frequency.** Use the drive's output frequency, not the 50 or 60 Hz mains, to work out N<sub>s</sub>.
- **Rounding the measured speed.** At 1,460 rpm a 5 rpm reading error changes the slip by 12.5% of its value, so use a tachometer that resolves 1 rpm.

## Slip and rotor frequency on a VFD

A drive changes the synchronous speed, but at the same load torque the motor keeps roughly the same slip in rpm. For the default motor (40 rpm slip at rated torque), the table shows why slip as a percentage climbs at low frequency while the rotor frequency stays put:

| Drive output | N<sub>s</sub> | Shaft speed | Slip | Rotor frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 Hz | 300 rpm | 260 rpm | 13.33% | 1.33 Hz |
| 20 Hz | 600 rpm | 560 rpm | 6.67% | 1.33 Hz |
| 25 Hz | 750 rpm | 710 rpm | 5.33% | 1.33 Hz |
| 35 Hz | 1,050 rpm | 1,010 rpm | 3.81% | 1.33 Hz |
| 50 Hz | 1,500 rpm | 1,460 rpm | 2.67% | 1.33 Hz |
| 60 Hz | 1,800 rpm | 1,760 rpm | 2.22% | 1.33 Hz |

At 10 Hz the same 40 rpm is a 13.3% slip. This is why sensorless drives use slip compensation: without it a conveyor set for 10 Hz runs noticeably slower than 300 rpm under load.

## Measuring slip in the field

- **Optical tachometer.** Stick reflective tape on the shaft or coupling and read the speed directly. Accuracy of 1 rpm is enough for load estimates.
- **Stroboscope.** Set it to the synchronous flash rate (1,500 per minute here); the mark appears to drift backwards at the slip speed, 40 rpm.
- **Current signature.** A spectrum of one phase current shows broken bar sidebands at (1 ± 2s) × f, which for the default motor are **47.33 Hz** and **52.67 Hz** either side of 50 Hz. The spacing itself gives the slip without touching the shaft.
- **Search coil.** A coil held near the motor end picks up the rotor frequency, here 1.33 Hz; counting its beats over a minute gives slip directly.

## Slip, torque and voltage

In the normal running range torque is nearly proportional to slip. A 7.5 kW version of the default motor delivers T = 9,549 × 7.5 ÷ 1,460 = **49.1 N·m** at 2.67% slip, so half that torque needs roughly half the slip. Slip also rises as voltage falls, roughly with 1 ÷ V². At 80% voltage the same load pushes slip to about 4.17% and speed down to 1,438 rpm, and the extra rotor current raises the winding temperature.
