## How to use the string frequency calculator

Enter the **vibrating length** (nut to bridge on an instrument), the **tension**, the string's **linear density** in grams per metre and the **harmonic number**. The calculator returns the wave speed, the frequency of that harmonic and its wavelength. To find the tension for a target note, enter the frequency and clear the tension.

## String frequency formula

<p class="formula">v = √(T ÷ μ) &nbsp;·&nbsp; f<sub>n</sub> = n v ÷ (2L) &nbsp;·&nbsp; λ<sub>n</sub> = 2L ÷ n</p>

## Example

A steel guitar string, 648 mm long, 0.4 g/m, tensioned to 70 N:

- Wave speed v = √(70 ÷ 0.0004) = **418.3 m/s**
- Fundamental f₁ = 1 ÷ (2 × 0.648) × 418.3 = **322.8 Hz** (close to the E₄ note at 329.6 Hz)

## Notes

- Stiff, thick strings are slightly inharmonic: their overtones sit a little above exact multiples of f₁.
- The same equation estimates the tension in a stay cable or wire rope from its measured natural frequency.
- Two strings tuned slightly apart produce audible beats — see the [beat frequency calculator](/physics/beat-frequency/).

## Tuning the default string to pitch

The default string gives f<sub>1</sub> = **322.8 Hz** with a wave speed of **418.3 m/s** and a wavelength of **1.296 m**. That is 36 cents flat of E<sub>4</sub> at 329.63 Hz. Solving T = μ(2Lf)² for the target note gives 0.0004 × (2 × 0.648 × 329.63)² = **73.0 N**, about 7.44 kgf, so the player needs 3.0 N more tension. A small change in tension is a large change in cents because frequency follows the square root.

## Linear density from the wire size

For a plain solid string, μ = ρ × π d² ÷ 4. A steel string with ρ = 7,850 kg/m³ and d = 0.254 mm (a 0.010 in gauge) gives μ = **0.398 g/m**, matching the default 0.4 g/m. Wound strings have a core plus a wrap, so weigh a measured length or use the maker's unit weight table instead of the diameter.

## Fret positions on a 648 mm scale

Each fret shortens the vibrating length by a factor of 2<sup>1/12</sup>, raising the pitch one semitone at the same tension:

At the 12th fret the length halves and the frequency doubles to 645.6 Hz, one octave up.

## Measuring cable tension by vibration

Rearranged as T = 4 μ L² f<sub>1</sub>², the equation turns an accelerometer reading into a tension. A stay cable with μ = 5 kg/m and a free length of 40 m vibrating at 2 Hz carries about 4 × 5 × 40² × 2² = **128 kN**. Bridge and mast inspectors use this because it needs no load cell. Sag, bending stiffness and end dampers all shift the result, so long or heavy cables need correction factors.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering μ in kg/m.** This calculator expects g/m; 0.0004 typed as kg/m is treated as 0.0004 g/m and gives a frequency 31.6 times too high.
- **Using total string length.** Only the part between the nut and the saddle vibrates.
- **Entering tension in kgf.** Convert to newtons by multiplying by 9.81.
- **Expecting harmonics to stay exact.** Thick, stiff strings run sharp in their upper partials, which is why pianos are stretch tuned.
- **Forgetting the harmonic number.** n = 2 does not give a different string; it gives the second mode of the same one at twice f<sub>1</sub>.
