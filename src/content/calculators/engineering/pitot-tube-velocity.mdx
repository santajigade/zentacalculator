## How to use the Pitot tube velocity calculator

Enter the **dynamic pressure** read from the manometer, the **fluid density** and the pitot **coefficient**. The velocity appears straight away. You can also work backwards to find the pressure you should expect for a target velocity.

## Pitot tube velocity formula

<p class="formula">v = C × √(2ΔP ÷ ρ)</p>

## Worked example

Air at 20 °C (ρ = 1.2 kg/m³), pitot reading 500 Pa (5 mbar):

v = √(2 × 500 ÷ 1.2) = **28.9 m/s**

## Air velocity quick table (ρ = 1.2 kg/m³)

| ΔP (Pa) | Velocity (m/s) |
|---|---|
| 10 | 4.1 |
| 50 | 9.1 |
| 100 | 12.9 |
| 250 | 20.4 |
| 500 | 28.9 |

## Flow from a single velocity

A pitot tube measures **point** velocity. To get flow in a duct or pipe, either:

- **Traverse** the duct: measure at many points across the area (log-Tchebycheff or equal-area method) and average the velocities.
- **Estimate**: in fully developed turbulent pipe flow, average velocity is roughly 0.8–0.85 times the centreline velocity.

Then Q = average velocity × cross-section area.

## Air density matters

Air density changes with temperature, pressure and altitude:

| Condition | Density (kg/m³) |
|---|---|
| 0 °C, sea level | 1.29 |
| 20 °C, sea level | 1.20 |
| 50 °C, sea level | 1.09 |
| 20 °C, 1,500 m altitude | 1.01 |

Using 1.2 kg/m³ at 50 °C overestimates density by 10% and underestimates velocity by about 5%.

## Averaging pitot tubes

Industrial averaging pitot tubes (such as multi-port probes) sample pressure at several points across the pipe and give a DP related to average velocity. They have low permanent pressure loss and suit large pipes and ducts.

## Aircraft airspeed

Aircraft use pitot-static systems to measure indicated airspeed from dynamic pressure. At altitude, the lower air density means true airspeed is higher than indicated airspeed.

## Measuring tips

- Point the tip straight into the flow, within about ±10°.
- Velocity varies across a duct. For flow measurement, take a **traverse** — several readings at standard positions — and average the velocities (not the pressures).
- Below about 3 m/s in air, the pressure is very small and readings become unreliable.

## Example: S-type probe in a hot stack

Stack testing uses S-type probes, which need a coefficient of about 0.84, and the gas is far lighter than room air. Flue gas close to the molar mass of air at 150 °C and 101.325 kPa has a density of 101,325 × 0.02897 ÷ (8.314 × 423.15) = **0.834 kg/m³**. With a reading of 120 Pa:

- v = 0.84 × √(2 × 120 ÷ 0.834) = **14.25 m/s**
- Stack area for a 1.2 m diameter = 1.131 m², so the flow is about **58,006 m³/h** at stack conditions

Had the default 1.2 kg/m³ been left in, the velocity would read 11.88 m/s, about 17% low.

## Average the velocities, not the pressures

Five readings across a duct of 410, 480, 520, 495 and 430 Pa give velocities that average **27.872 m/s**. Averaging the pressures first and then taking the square root gives 27.899 m/s. The gap is small here, but it grows when the profile is uneven near dampers and bends, and the pressure average always reads high.

## Inches of water to velocity

US gauges and balancing reports often use inches of water column. For standard air (1.2 kg/m³), velocity in ft/min is close to 4,005 × √(inH₂O):

## How it is measured in practice

The probe's total port faces upstream and the static ports sit on the side of the stem. Both connect to an inclined manometer or a digital micromanometer that reads the difference directly. Test points are drilled at least 7.5 duct diameters downstream of a bend or fan where possible, and the traverse positions follow the log-Tchebycheff or equal-area pattern. On wet or dusty stacks the lines are purged between readings and checked for leaks before and after the run.

## Common mistakes

- **Using room air density for hot or humid gas.** Correct density for temperature, static pressure and moisture, as in the stack example.
- **Leaving C at 1.0 for an S-type probe.** S-type probes read a larger differential than a Prandtl tube and need their calibration coefficient, usually 0.82 to 0.86.
- **Mixing pressure units.** 5 mm of water is 49.0 Pa, not 5 Pa; enter the value in the unit shown on the gauge.
- **Swapping the tubing.** Total and static lines reversed give a negative or near zero reading, and on an S-type probe a wrong orientation gives a different coefficient.
- **Reading in swirling flow.** Behind a fan or an elbow the flow is not parallel to the probe, so the tip sees only part of the dynamic pressure.
