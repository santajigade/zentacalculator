    The Ziegler-Nichols closed loop method finds the gain at which a loop just oscillates steadily (Ku) and the period of that oscillation (Pu), then sets the controller from simple ratios. This Ziegler-Nichols PID tuning calculator gives P, PI and PID settings.

    Control and commissioning engineers use it as a quick first tuning, as in the PID controller tuning guide and step-test, relay and Ziegler-Nichols auto-tuning in a DCS.

    How to use the Ziegler–Nichols PID tuning calculator

    Enter the ultimate gain Ku and the ultimate period Pu from your closed-loop test. The calculator gives the controller gain for P, PI and PID control and the integral and derivative times in seconds. Settings assume the ideal (non-interacting) PID form with integral time in seconds per repeat; check whether your controller uses gain or proportional band, and repeats per minute or minutes per repeat.

    Ziegler–Nichols closed-loop rules

    Controller Kc Ti Td
    P 0.5 Ku — —
    PI 0.45 Ku Pu ÷ 1.2 —
    PID 0.6 Ku Pu ÷ 2 Pu ÷ 8

    Example

    A loop oscillates steadily at Ku = 2 with a period Pu = 60 s:

    Rule Kc PB Ti Td
    P only 1 100% — —
    PI 0.9 111% 50 s —
    PID (classic) 1.2 83% 30 s 7.5 s
    PI (Tyreus–Luyben) 0.625 160% 132 s —
    PID (Tyreus–Luyben) 0.909 110% 132 s 9.52 s

    Cautions

    • The test drives the loop to the edge of instability — don’t do it on loops where oscillation is unsafe; use a step test and the lambda tuning calculator instead.
    • Valve stiction and sensor lag change Ku and Pu; fix mechanical problems first.
    • After tuning, test with a small set-point step and reduce gain if overshoot is excessive.

    Worked example: from a relay test to PID settings

    Driving a loop to steady oscillation with a P-only controller is risky, so many DCS auto-tuners use a relay test instead: the output is switched ±d around its working value and the PV settles into a limit cycle of amplitude a. The describing function gives the ultimate gain:

    Ku = 4d ÷ (π a)

    With a relay step of d = ±5% and a measured PV amplitude of a = 3.2% (peak), Ku = 4 × 5 ÷ (π × 3.2) = 1.989, and the period of the cycle is Pu. Taking the calculator defaults Ku = 2 and Pu = 60 s, the classic PID settings are:

    • Kc = 0.6 × 2 = 1.2 (PB 83%)
    • Ti = 60 ÷ 2 = 30 s (0.5 min, or 2 repeats per minute)
    • Td = 60 ÷ 8 = 7.5 s (0.125 min)

    Settings for a series (interacting) PID

    Many controllers use the series form. The classic rule has Ti = 4 Td exactly, which is the limit for converting ideal settings to series ones:

    Kc′ = (Kc/2)(1 + √(1 − 4Td/Ti))  ·  Ti′ = (Ti/2)(1 + √…)  ·  Td′ = (Ti/2)(1 − √…)

    For the example the root is zero, so the series settings are Kc′ = 0.6, Ti′ = 15 s and Td′ = 15 s. Typing the ideal values into a series controller gives a different, more aggressive loop.

    Variations on the closed loop rules (Ku = 2, Pu = 60 s)

    Rule Kc rule Kc PB Ti Td
    Classic PID 0.6 Ku 1.200 83% 30.0 s 7.5 s
    Pessen integral rule 0.7 Ku 1.400 71% 24.0 s 9.0 s
    Some overshoot 0.33 Ku 0.660 152% 30.0 s 20.0 s
    No overshoot 0.2 Ku 0.400 250% 30.0 s 20.0 s

    Estimating Ku and Pu from a step test model

    If a step test has already given a first order plus dead time model (gain K, time constant τ, dead time θ), the ultimate values can be calculated instead of measured. The loop is at the stability limit at the frequency ω where the process phase lag reaches 180°, atan(ωτ) + ωθ = π, and the ultimate gain is Ku = √(1 + (ωτ)²) ÷ K. For K = 1.5, τ = 120 s and θ = 20 s, solving numerically gives Ku = 6.71 and Pu = 75.2 s, so the classic PID settings would be Kc = 4.03, Ti = 37.6 s and Td = 9.4 s.

    How the ultimate values depend on the dead time ratio (for a process gain of 1; divide Ku by the real gain):

    θ ÷ τ Ku × K Pu ÷ θ
    0.05 32.06 3.92
    0.1 16.35 3.85
    0.2 8.50 3.72
    0.5 3.81 3.42
    1 2.26 3.10
    2 1.52 2.75

    Lag dominant loops (small θ ÷ τ) tolerate high gain and cycle at about four dead times or less; in dead time dominant loops Ku × K falls towards 1 and Pu towards 2θ, which is where Ziegler-Nichols settings are least robust.

    A slow temperature loop in minutes

    A jacketed reactor temperature loop cycles steadily at Ku = 8 with Pu = 12 min. The classic PID settings are Kc = 4.8 (PB 20.8%), Ti = 6 min (0.167 repeats per minute) and Td = 1.5 min. With a period this long, wait several cycles before reading Pu, because a single peak to peak time can be distorted by load changes.

    Common mistakes

    • Leaving integral action on during the test. Ku and Pu must come from a proportional-only loop or a relay test; integral action shortens the period.
    • Testing with a saturating valve. If the output hits 0 or 100% during the oscillation, the measured Ku is wrong.
    • Ignoring the controller form. Ideal, series and parallel PID forms need different numbers for the same response.
    • Using derivative on a noisy PV. Flow and liquid pressure loops are noisy; use PI there and keep derivative for slow temperature loops.
    • Keeping the settings without a check. Ziegler-Nichols aims for quarter amplitude decay; most process loops need the gain reduced after a set point test.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I find Ku and Pu?

    Put the controller in automatic with integral and derivative off, then raise the gain in small steps until the process variable oscillates with constant amplitude. That gain is Ku and the time between peaks is Pu.

    What are the Ziegler–Nichols PID settings?

    Kc = 0.6 Ku, Ti = Pu ÷ 2 and Td = Pu ÷ 8. For PI, Kc = 0.45 Ku and Ti = Pu ÷ 1.2.

    Why are Ziegler–Nichols settings so aggressive?

    They target a quarter-amplitude decay, which overshoots and oscillates. Many engineers halve the gain, or use the gentler Tyreus–Luyben rules shown in the table.

    How do I convert gain to proportional band?

    PB (%) = 100 ÷ Kc. A gain of 1.2 is a proportional band of about 83%.

    What is the relay auto-tune method?

    The controller output is switched up and down by a fixed amount d around its normal value, which makes the PV cycle at the ultimate period. The ultimate gain is Ku = 4d ÷ (π a), where a is the PV amplitude. A ±5% relay giving a 3.2% PV amplitude means Ku = 1.99.

    Do Ziegler-Nichols settings work in a series PID?

    Yes, after conversion. Because the classic rule has Ti = 4 Td, the series equivalents are Kc = 0.3 Ku, Ti = Td = Pu ÷ 4. For Ku = 2 and Pu = 60 s that is a gain of 0.6 with Ti and Td both 15 s.

    Can I get Ku and Pu without an oscillation test?

    Yes, from a step test model. Solve atan(ωτ) + ωθ = π for ω, then Ku = √(1 + (ωτ)²) ÷ K and Pu = 2π ÷ ω. A process with K = 1.5, τ = 120 s and θ = 20 s gives Ku = 6.71 and Pu = 75.2 s.
