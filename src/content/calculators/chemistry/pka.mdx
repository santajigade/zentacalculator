## How to use the pKa calculator

Enter the **acid dissociation constant Ka** (you can type it as 0.000018 or 1.8e-5). The calculator returns the pKa and the Kb and pKb of the conjugate base. To go the other way, clear Ka and enter a pKa, Kb or pKb. For a weak base, enter its Kb in the Kb field and read the pKa of its conjugate acid.

## Ka, pKa, Kb and pKb formulas

<p class="formula">pKa = −log₁₀ Ka &nbsp;·&nbsp; Ka = 10<sup>−pKa</sup></p>

<p class="formula">Ka × Kb = K<sub>w</sub> = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ &nbsp;·&nbsp; pKa + pKb = 14 (25 °C)</p>

## Example

Acetic acid, Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵:

- pKa = −log₁₀(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = **4.7447**
- Kb (acetate ion) = 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ = **5.556 × 10⁻¹⁰**
- pKb = 14 − 4.7447 = **9.2553**

## Notes

- K<sub>w</sub> changes with temperature (pK<sub>w</sub> ≈ 14.17 at 20 °C and 13.26 at 50 °C), so pKa + pKb = 14 holds only at 25 °C.
- Polyprotic acids have one pKa per proton — phosphoric acid has 2.15, 7.20 and 12.35.
- Use the [Henderson–Hasselbalch calculator](/chemistry/henderson-hasselbalch/) to find buffer pH from pKa and the acid–base ratio.

## From pKa to the pH of a solution

The default Ka of 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ also predicts the pH of a 0.10 mol/L acetic acid solution. The quick estimate [H⁺] = √(Ka C) = 1.342 × 10⁻³ mol/L gives pH 2.872. Solving the full quadratic, [H⁺] = (−Ka + √(Ka² + 4 Ka C)) ÷ 2 = 1.333 × 10⁻³ mol/L, gives pH **2.875**, so only 1.33% of the acid is ionised. For 0.10 mol/L sodium acetate the Kb of 5.556 × 10⁻¹⁰ gives [OH⁻] = √(Kb C) = 7.454 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L and pH **8.87**, which is why acetate solutions are mildly basic.

## How much is ionised at each pH

With pKa = 4.7447, the fraction present as acetate is 1 ÷ (1 + 10<sup>pKa − pH</sup>):

| pH | Acetic acid (HA) | Acetate (A⁻) |
|---|---|---|
| 3.00 | 98.2% | 1.8% |
| 4.00 | 84.7% | 15.3% |
| 4.74 | 50.3% | 49.7% |
| 5.00 | 35.7% | 64.3% |
| 6.00 | 5.3% | 94.7% |
| 7.00 | 0.6% | 99.4% |

Each pH unit away from the pKa shifts the ratio tenfold. Chromatographers use this to keep an analyte fully ionised or fully neutral, and formulators use it to predict how a drug will behave at stomach or blood pH.

## How pKa is measured

The usual method is a potentiometric titration with a calibrated pH electrode: titrate the weak acid with strong base and read the pH at the half equivalence point, where [HA] = [A⁻] and pH = pKa. Acids with a chromophore can also be measured by UV-visible spectroscopy, following the absorbance of the two forms across a series of buffers. Tabulated values usually refer to zero ionic strength. In a 0.1 mol/L salt background the Davies equation puts the apparent pKa of acetic acid near 4.53 instead of 4.74, so measured values in real media read lower.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the natural log.** −ln(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) is 10.93, not a pKa; the definition uses log₁₀.
- **Mistyping the exponent.** 1.8e5 instead of 1.8e-5 gives a negative pKa of -5.26, which would describe a strong acid.
- **Treating a base's pKb as the pKa.** For ammonia enter its Kb (1.8 × 10⁻⁵) and read the pKa of the ammonium ion, 9.26; the two numbers are not interchangeable.
- **Picking the wrong pKa of a polyprotic acid.** A phosphate buffer at pH 7.2 is governed by the second pKa (7.20), not the first (2.15).
- **Comparing values measured in different media.** Ionic strength and solvent shift pKa, so check the conditions before comparing two sources.
