    Heat duty is the rate at which heat is added to or removed from a fluid stream, set by its mass flow, specific heat and temperature change. This heat duty calculator applies Q = ṁ × cp × ΔT and gives the result in kW or hp.

    Process and HVAC engineers use it to size heat exchangers, chillers, heaters and boilers, and to check energy meter readings against the design load.

    How to use the heat duty calculator

    Enter the mass flow, specific heat and the temperature difference between inlet and outlet. The heat duty is calculated in kW (or hp). You can also find the flow needed to carry a given load, or the temperature rise for a known heater power.

    Heat duty formula

    Q̇ = ṁ × cp × ΔT

    Worked example with the default values

    A heat exchanger cools 2 kg/s of water (cp = 4.186 kJ/kg·K) by 10 K:

    1. Q̇ = ṁ × cp × ΔT = 2 × 4.186 × 10 = 83.72 kW
    2. In other units: 112.3 hp, 301,392 kJ/h, 285,665 Btu/h or 23.80 TR

    The calculator multiplies cp by 1,000 to work in J/kg·K, so the raw result is 83,720 W, shown as 83.72 kW. In imperial mode the same inputs read as 15,873 lb/h and an 18 °F difference; the duty does not change, only the units on screen.

    Steam heating

    When heating with steam, the steam gives up latent heat as it condenses:

    Steam flow = Heat duty ÷ latent heat

    At about 7 bar(g), saturated steam has a latent heat of roughly 2,050 kJ/kg. A 100 kW duty needs about 0.049 kg/s, or 175 kg/h of steam.

    Log mean temperature difference

    To size a heat exchanger, combine duty with the overall heat-transfer coefficient U and area A:

    Q = U × A × LMTD

    where LMTD = (ΔT₁ − ΔT₂) ÷ ln(ΔT₁ ÷ ΔT₂), using the temperature differences at each end of the exchanger.

    Cooling towers and chillers

    Cooling capacity is often quoted in tons of refrigeration (TR): 1 TR = 3.517 kW. A chiller cooling 10 kg/s of water by 5 K removes 209 kW, about 59.5 TR.

    Practical uses

    • Chiller and boiler sizing: enter the design flow and ΔT, then convert the duty to tons with the chiller tonnage calculator.
    • Energy metering: BTU and thermal energy meters compute exactly this product from a flow meter and two temperature sensors, usually a matched Pt100 or Pt500 pair.
    • Exchanger sizing: once the duty is known, the LMTD calculator gives the driving temperature difference for Q = U × A × LMTD.
    • Steam heating: for a steam heated exchanger, the steam consumption calculator turns the duty into kg/h of steam.
    • Checking performance: a measured duty falling below the design value at the same flow points to fouling or a control valve that is not opening fully.

    Example: checking a chilled water energy meter

    A chilled water branch carries 36 m³/h of water supplied at 7 °C and returned at 12 °C. At about 10 °C water has a density of 999.7 kg/m³ and cp of 4.192 kJ/kg·K:

    • Mass flow = 36 × 999.7 ÷ 3,600 = 9.997 kg/s
    • Q = 9.997 × 4.192 × 5 = 209.5 kW, or 59.6 TR

    Because the ΔT is only 5 K, a 0.1 K error between the two temperature sensors changes the duty by 2%. That is why energy meters use matched sensor pairs and why low ΔT loops are hard to bill accurately.

    Example: hot and cold side balance

    A thermal oil heater circuit delivers 3 kg/s of oil (cp about 2.3 kJ/kg·K) that cools from 220 °C to 180 °C in an exchanger. The oil gives up 3 × 2.3 × 40 = 276 kW. If that heat goes into 2 kg/s of water, the water warms by 276 ÷ (2 × 4.186) = 33.0 K. Measured duties on the two sides usually agree within a few percent; a larger gap points to a bad flow or temperature reading, or heat lost through poor insulation.

    Flow needed to carry 100 kW

    Volumetric flow in m³/h for each fluid and design temperature difference, from V = Q ÷ (ρ × cp × ΔT) with the properties shown (typical values; check your fluid data sheet):

    Fluid ΔT 5 K ΔT 10 K ΔT 15 K ΔT 20 K
    Water (cp 4.186, ρ 998 kg/m³) 17.2 8.6 5.7 4.3
    Glycol 30% (cp 3.7, ρ 1040 kg/m³) 18.7 9.4 6.2 4.7
    Thermal oil (cp 2.2, ρ 870 kg/m³) 37.6 18.8 12.5 9.4

    Lower specific heat and density both push the flow up, so a glycol or oil system needs bigger pumps and pipes for the same load.

    Common mistakes

    • Entering volumetric flow as mass flow. 2 m³/h is not 2 kg/s; convert with density first (about 0.554 kg/s for water).
    • Mixing kJ and kW. With mass flow in kg/s and cp in kJ/kg·K the result is kW; with kg/h it is kJ/h, 3,600 times larger.
    • Using the water cp for glycol mixtures. A 30% glycol solution has about 12% less heat capacity, so the duty is overstated.
    • Applying the formula across a phase change. Boiling or condensing duty is mass flow times latent heat, which cp × ΔT does not capture.
    • Converting a °F difference like a temperature. An 18 °F rise is 10 K, not −7.8 °C.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is heat duty?

    The rate at which heat is added to or removed from a fluid, usually in kW. For a heat exchanger, it is the same on both the hot and cold sides, ignoring losses.

    What specific heat should I use for water?

    4.186 kJ/kg·K is standard. It changes only slightly between 0 and 100 °C.

    Can I use volumetric flow?

    Convert it to mass flow first by multiplying by density. Use the mass flow rate calculator.

    What is the heat duty formula?

    Q = ṁ × cp × ΔT: mass flow × specific heat × temperature change.

    How much heat to warm 1,000 L of water by 10 °C?

    About 41,860 kJ, or 11.6 kWh.

    Why does a small temperature difference make heat duty readings inaccurate?

    The duty is directly proportional to ΔT, so a fixed sensor error is a larger share of a small difference. At 5 K, a 0.1 K mismatch between sensors is a 2% error in duty.

    How do I check that both sides of a heat exchanger agree?

    Work out ṁ × cp × ΔT for the hot stream and for the cold stream. They should match within a few percent; a bigger difference means a faulty flow or temperature reading, or heat loss to the surroundings.

    How do I convert heat duty from kW to Btu/h or tons?

    Multiply kW by 3,412 for Btu/h and divide by 3.517 for tons of refrigeration. The default 83.72 kW is about 285,665 Btu/h or 23.8 TR.
