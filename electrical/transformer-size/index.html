    Sizing a transformer means turning the connected load into a kVA rating after allowing for demand factor, power factor and future growth. This transformer size calculator gives the maximum demand and the kVA to specify.

    Electrical engineers, contractors and plant planners use it at the design stage, since an undersized unit overheats and an oversized one costs more and wastes no-load losses. See how to calculate transformer kVA rating.

    How to use the transformer size calculator

    Enter the total connected load in kW, the demand (diversity) factor, the power factor you expect after correction and a growth margin. The calculator gives maximum demand and the required kVA. Choose the next standard rating above the result.

    Transformer sizing formula

    kVA = (connected kW × demand factor) ÷ PF × (1 + growth margin)

    Worked example

    A factory has 500 kW of connected load, a demand factor of 0.7, a corrected power factor of 0.9 and allows 25% for growth:

    • Maximum demand = 500 × 0.7 = 350 kW
    • Apparent power today = 350 ÷ 0.9 = 388.9 kVA
    • Required rating = 388.9 × (1 + 0.25) = 486 kVA
    • Next standard size: 500 kVA, whose full-load current at 415 V is 500 × 1,000 ÷ (√3 × 415) = 696 A

    On day one the 500 kVA unit runs at 78% load, close to the 50 to 80% band where distribution transformers are most efficient.

    Standard distribution transformer ratings

    16, 25, 63, 100, 160, 200, 250, 315, 400, 500, 630, 800, 1,000, 1,250, 1,600, 2,000 and 2,500 kVA (IS 1180 / IEC 60076).

    Typical demand factors

    Installation Demand factor
    Continuous process plant 0.8–0.9
    General manufacturing 0.6–0.8
    Commercial buildings 0.5–0.7
    Residential complexes 0.4–0.6

    Practical checks after sizing

    • Motor starting. A large direct-on-line motor draws 6 to 7 times its full-load current. If the largest motor is more than about 20 to 30% of the transformer rating, check the voltage dip with the motor starting voltage dip calculator.
    • Harmonic loads. Drives, UPS systems and LED lighting raise eddy losses; use a K-rated unit or derate by 10 to 20% when they make up much of the load.
    • Ambient and altitude. Ratings assume about 40 °C maximum ambient and up to 1,000 m altitude; hotter or higher sites need derating.
    • Parallel or standby units. Critical plants often split the load across two transformers, each able to carry the essential load alone.

    Common mistakes

    • Adding kW and kVA together. Convert every load to the same basis before summing, and apply the power factor once.
    • Skipping the demand factor entirely or using a very low one. Without measured data, an optimistic factor is the commonest cause of undersized transformers.
    • Using the power factor before correction when a capacitor bank is planned, or after correction when it is not. The rating must match the power factor the transformer will actually see.
    • Counting standby equipment. Duty and standby pumps do not run together; include only one of each pair.
    • Rounding down to a standard size. A 486 kVA requirement needs 500 kVA, never the size below it.

    Losses and efficiency at the day one load

    The default inputs give a required rating of 486 kVA (350 kW maximum demand), so a 500 kVA unit is chosen. Before growth it carries 388.9 kVA, a loading of 77.8%. Taking illustrative losses for a 500 kVA oil-filled unit of 0.9 kW no-load (core) and 6 kW load (copper) loss at full load, with load loss scaling with the square of loading:

    Loading Output at PF 0.9 Total losses Efficiency
    25% 112 kW 1.27 kW 98.88%
    39% 174 kW 1.80 kW 98.98%
    50% 225 kW 2.40 kW 98.94%
    75% 338 kW 4.28 kW 98.75%
    100% 450 kW 6.90 kW 98.49%

    Efficiency peaks at √(0.9 ÷ 6) = 38.7% load, where copper loss equals core loss, and the curve is flat between about 30% and 80%. At the day one load the losses are 0.9 + 6 × 0.778² = 4.53 kW, an efficiency of 98.72%. Held at that load all year, the losses come to about 39,680 kWh, which is why buyers compare tender offers on capitalised losses rather than price alone. Use the maker’s tested figures, or the transformer efficiency calculator, for a real unit.

    Sizing from measured demand

    For an existing plant, measured data beats an estimated demand factor. A utility bill or a power analyser logging 15 minute demand gives the real peak in kVA, already including diversity and power factor. If the highest recorded demand is 330 kVA, adding the 25% growth margin gives 412.5 kVA, so a 500 kVA unit still fits. Log for at least a full production cycle, including any seasonal peak such as summer chiller load.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I calculate transformer size?

    Multiply connected load by the diversity factor to get maximum demand, divide by power factor for kVA, add a growth margin, then choose the next standard rating.

    What diversity factor should I use?

    It depends on how loads operate together. Continuous process plants may be 0.8–0.9; workshops and buildings 0.5–0.7.

    Why not size exactly to the load?

    Transformers last longer and are most efficient at 50–80% load, and margins allow for growth and motor starting.

    Should harmonics be considered?

    Yes. With many VFDs, UPSs or rectifiers, use a K-rated transformer or derate a standard one.

    What size transformer do I need for a 100 kW load?

    At unity diversity and 0.9 power factor, 100 kW is 111 kVA, so 160 kVA with a growth margin. With the default 0.7 demand factor and 25% growth, 100 kW connected needs 97 kVA, which is a 100 kVA unit.

    Can a transformer be loaded above its kVA rating?

    For short periods, yes: IEC 60076-7 allows cyclic overloads if the hot-spot temperature stays within limits, typically after a light-load period. Continuous overload ages the insulation quickly, roughly halving its life for every 6 to 7 °C of extra hot-spot temperature.

    Why are transformers rated in kVA rather than kW?

    Transformer heating depends on current and voltage, not on how much of that current does real work. Copper loss follows current and core loss follows voltage, so the limit is apparent power in kVA, whatever the load power factor.
