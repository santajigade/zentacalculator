## How to use the convection heat transfer calculator

Enter the **heat transfer coefficient h**, the **surface area** and the **surface and fluid temperatures**. The calculator returns the heat transfer rate and the heat flux. Clear any input and enter Q to solve for it — for example the h needed to remove a known heat load.

## Convection formula

<p class="formula">Q = h × A × (T<sub>s</sub> − T<sub>∞</sub>) &nbsp;·&nbsp; q = Q ÷ A</p>

## Worked example

A 2 m² surface at 80 °C in air at 25 °C with h = 25 W/m²·K:

- Q = 25 × 2 × (80 − 25) = **2,750 W**
- Heat flux = 2,750 ÷ 2 = **1,375 W/m²**

## Typical heat transfer coefficients (2 m², ΔT = 55 K)

| Situation | h (W/m²·K) | Heat transfer rate |
|---|---|---|
| Free convection — air | 2 – 25 | 220 – 2,750 W |
| Free convection — water | 50 – 1,000 | 5,500 – 110,000 W |
| Forced convection — air | 25 – 250 | 2,750 – 27,500 W |
| Forced convection — water | 100 – 20,000 | 11,000 – 2,200,000 W |
| Forced convection — oil | 50 – 1,500 | 5,500 – 165,000 W |
| Boiling water | 2,500 – 100,000 | 275,000 – 11,000,000 W |
| Condensing steam | 5,000 – 100,000 | 550,000 – 11,000,000 W |

## Notes

- Hot surfaces also lose heat by radiation — at 80 °C in still air it can equal the convective loss; see the [Stefan–Boltzmann calculator](/physics/stefan-boltzmann-law/).
- For heat transfer between two fluids through a wall, use an overall coefficient U and the [LMTD calculator](/engineering/lmtd/).

## Estimating the heat transfer coefficient

The hard part of any convection problem is h, which depends on the fluid, its velocity, the geometry and whether the flow is laminar or turbulent. Engineers find it from a Nusselt number correlation, Nu = hL ÷ k.

**Free convection in air.** For a vertical surface, simplified correlations give h = 1.42 (ΔT/L)<sup>0.25</sup> in laminar flow and h = 1.31 ΔT<sup>1/3</sup> in turbulent flow (W/m²·K, ΔT in K, L the height in m). For a 0.5 m high surface:

| ΔT | h, laminar (L = 0.5 m) | Q for 2 m², laminar | h, turbulent (surfaces over about 1 m tall) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 K | 3.0 W/m²·K | 60 W | 2.8 W/m²·K |
| 25 K | 3.8 W/m²·K | 189 W | 3.8 W/m²·K |
| 55 K | 4.6 W/m²·K | 506 W | 5.0 W/m²·K |
| 100 K | 5.3 W/m²·K | 1,068 W | 6.1 W/m²·K |

At ΔT = 55 K the laminar value is about 4.6 W/m²·K, so the 25 W/m²·K in the worked example implies a light forced draught, not still air.

**Forced convection inside tubes.** The Dittus-Boelter equation Nu = 0.023 Re<sup>0.8</sup> Pr<sup>0.4</sup> (heating, Re above about 10,000) gives, for water at about 40 °C (k = 0.631 W/m·K, ν = 0.658 mm²/s, Pr = 4.32) in a 25 mm tube:

| Velocity | Re | Nu | h |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 m/s | 18,997 | 109 | 2,760 W/m²·K |
| 1 m/s | 37,994 | 190 | 4,806 W/m²·K |
| 2 m/s | 75,988 | 332 | 8,368 W/m²·K |

At 1 m/s, h ≈ 4,806 W/m²·K, about 192 times the air value in the example. That is why liquid cooling moves so much more heat through the same area.

## Adding radiation as a second coefficient

Radiation can be written in the same form, Q<sub>r</sub> = h<sub>r</sub> A (T<sub>s</sub> − T<sub>∞</sub>), with h<sub>r</sub> = εσ(T<sub>s</sub>² + T<sub>∞</sub>²)(T<sub>s</sub> + T<sub>∞</sub>) in kelvin. Then the two coefficients simply add. For a painted surface (ε = 0.9) in surroundings at 25 °C and the 2 m² area of the worked example:

| Surface temperature | h<sub>r</sub> | Radiated heat from 2 m² |
|---|---|---|
| 40 °C | 5.8 W/m²·K | 175 W |
| 60 °C | 6.4 W/m²·K | 451 W |
| 80 °C | 7.1 W/m²·K | 781 W |
| 120 °C | 8.6 W/m²·K | 1,632 W |
| 200 °C | 12.3 W/m²·K | 4,309 W |

At 80 °C, h<sub>r</sub> = 7.1 W/m²·K, which adds 781 W to the convective result. Bare polished aluminium (ε near 0.05) radiates almost nothing, which is why it is used as an outer jacket on insulation.

## Second example: how hot will an enclosure run?

A sealed control panel with 0.8 m² of exposed surface dissipates 120 W in a 35 °C plant room. Solving the formula for the temperature difference, ΔT = Q ÷ (hA):

- Natural convection alone with h = 4 W/m²·K: ΔT = 120 ÷ (4 × 0.8) = **37.5 K**, a surface at 72.5 °C
- Including radiation (ε = 0.9, solved by iteration because h<sub>r</sub> depends on T<sub>s</sub>): ΔT = **14.4 K**, a surface at 49.4 °C

The air inside runs hotter than the skin, so with a skin near 49 °C this panel is already at the usual 40 to 50 °C limit for electronics and would need a filter fan or a cooler.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the wrong temperature difference.** ΔT is between the surface and the bulk fluid far from it, not between the inlet and outlet of the fluid. For a fluid that heats up along an exchanger, use the [LMTD](/engineering/lmtd/).
- **Taking h from a table without checking the flow regime.** Tables give wide ranges; a value from the wrong end can be ten times off. Calculate it from a correlation when the result matters.
- **Ignoring radiation from hot surfaces.** In still air, radiation from a painted surface at 80 °C is of the same size as natural convection, so adding only convection understates the loss by about half.
- **Mixing up h and U.** h is a single film coefficient; U combines both films and the wall. Using a film h where an overall U belongs overstates heat transfer.
- **Forgetting fouling.** Scale and deposits add thermal resistance over time, so design values are often 10 to 30% below clean values.
