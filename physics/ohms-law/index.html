    Ohm’s law formulas

    V = I × R P = V × I

    Combining these gives every other form you may need:

    Find From V and I From I and R From V and R From P and …
    Voltage — I × R — √(P × R)
    Current — — V ÷ R P ÷ V
    Resistance V ÷ I — — V² ÷ P
    Power V × I I² × R V² ÷ R —

    Worked example: 4–20 mA loop

    A 4–20 mA transmitter drives a 250 Ω sense resistor. At full scale (20 mA):

    • V = 0.020 A × 250 Ω = 5 V
    • P = 5 V × 0.020 A = 0.1 W

    That is why 250 Ω resistors convert a 4–20 mA signal into a 1–5 V input. The default values above show the 24 V supply case; set Current to 20 mA to try it.

    Reading the graph

    The chart plots one quantity against another while holding your other inputs fixed. With voltage fixed, current falls as resistance rises — the inverse curve I = V ÷ R. Switch the axes to plot power, or change the range.

    When Ohm’s law does not apply

    Ohm’s law holds for resistors and metal wires at constant temperature. It does not apply directly to diodes, LEDs, transistors or gas-discharge lamps, whose resistance changes with current. For LEDs, use the LED resistor calculator.

    Practical tips

    • Resistor wattage: choose a resistor rated at least 2× the calculated power.
    • Wire and fuse sizing depend on current, so calculate I first.
    • Remember to convert mA to A and kΩ to Ω when working by hand — the calculator handles this for you.

    Worked example with the default values

    The calculator opens with 24 V across 250 Ω:

    • I = V ÷ R = 24 ÷ 250 = 0.096 A (96 mA)
    • P = V × I = 24 × 0.096 = 2.304 W

    A standard 1/4 W resistor would burn out here; it needs at least a 5 W part to keep a 2× margin. This is the case of a 250 Ω resistor connected straight across a 24 V supply by mistake, instead of in series with a transmitter.

    Loop load check for a 4 to 20 mA circuit

    A 24 V loop must leave enough voltage for the transmitter at 20 mA. With 200 m of 1 mm² copper (7.0 Ω for both cores) and a transmitter that needs 12 V:

    Load resistance Drop at 20 mA Left for transmitter Result
    0 Ω 0.14 V 23.86 V OK
    250 Ω 5.14 V 18.86 V OK
    500 Ω 10.14 V 13.86 V OK
    600 Ω 12.14 V 11.86 V Too low
    750 Ω 15.14 V 8.86 V Too low

    The largest total loop resistance is (24 − 12) ÷ 0.020 = 600 Ω, including cable.

    Measuring to confirm the numbers

    Measure resistance only with the circuit de-energised, because a meter on the ohms range in a live circuit reads wrong and can be damaged. Measure current by breaking the circuit and putting the meter in series, or by reading the voltage across a known resistor and dividing. Comparing a measured voltage drop with V = I × R is the quickest way to spot a corroded terminal or a wrong resistor.

    Common mistakes

    • Forgetting to convert milliamps. 96 mA is 0.096 A; using 96 gives a resistance a thousand times too small.
    • Using cold resistance for a lamp or heater. Filament resistance rises many times when hot, so a cold ohmmeter reading overstates the current.
    • Applying V = IR to an LED or diode. Their current is not proportional to voltage; use the forward voltage and a series resistor.
    • Using RMS and peak values together. In AC work, keep voltage and current both in RMS when calculating power.
    • Ignoring resistor tolerance. A 5% resistor can be 237.5 to 262.5 Ω, which moves the current by the same 5%.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is Ohm's law?

    Ohm's law states that the current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it. V = I × R, where V is volts, I is amperes and R is ohms.

    How do I find resistance from power and voltage?

    Use R = V² ÷ P. A 230 V, 60 W lamp has a hot resistance of 230² ÷ 60 ≈ 882 Ω. Enter V and P above and the calculator does this for you.

    Does Ohm's law apply to every component?

    No. It applies to ohmic (linear) components like resistors. Diodes, transistors and lamp filaments change resistance with current or temperature.

    What is the formula for current?

    I = V ÷ R. 24 V across 250 Ω gives 0.096 A (96 mA).

    How do I calculate power from voltage and resistance?

    P = V² ÷ R. 12 V across 6 Ω dissipates 24 W.

    What size resistor should carry 24 V and 96 mA?

    It dissipates 2.30 W, so choose a 5 W resistor to keep at least a 2× power margin. A 1/4 W or 1 W part would overheat.
