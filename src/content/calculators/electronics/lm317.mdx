## How to use the LM317 calculator

Enter **R1** (between the output and adjust pins) and **R2** (between adjust and ground), plus the **input voltage** and **load current**. The calculator returns the output voltage, the headroom between input and output and the power the regulator turns into heat. To design for a target voltage, clear R2 and enter the output voltage you want.

## LM317 formula

<p class="formula">V<sub>out</sub> = 1.25 V × (1 + R2 ÷ R1) + I<sub>ADJ</sub> × R2</p>

<p class="formula">P<sub>D</sub> = (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>out</sub>) × I<sub>load</sub></p>

V<sub>REF</sub> = 1.25 V (1.20–1.30 V between parts) and I<sub>ADJ</sub> ≈ 50 µA typical.

## Example

R1 = 240 Ω, R2 = 715 Ω, 12 V input, 0.5 A load:

- V<sub>out</sub> = 1.25 × (1 + 715 ÷ 240) + 50 µA × 715 = **5.010 V**
- Headroom = 12 − 5.010 = **6.99 V** — comfortably above 3 V
- P<sub>D</sub> = 6.99 × 0.5 = **3.50 W** — needs a heat sink

## Design tips

- Fit a 0.1 µF input capacitor near the regulator and at least 1 µF on the output; 10 µF from adjust to ground improves ripple rejection.
- Dropping a large voltage wastes power — a 24 V to 5 V regulator at 1 A turns 19 W into heat. Consider a [buck converter](/electronics/buck-converter/) instead.
- Size the heat sink with the [heat sink calculator](/electronics/heat-sink/) using the P<sub>D</sub> above.

## Heat sink check for the default design

The default circuit (R1 = 240 Ω, R2 = 715 Ω, 12 V in, 0.5 A) gives V<sub>out</sub> = **5.010 V**, headroom **6.990 V** and P<sub>D</sub> = **3.495 W**. Using the classic TO-220 figures of about 50 °C/W junction to air and 4 °C/W junction to case:

- Without a heat sink at 25 °C: T<sub>J</sub> = 25 + 3.495 × 50 = **200 °C**, far above the 125 °C limit, so the thermal shutdown will cycle
- Allowed total thermal resistance at 40 °C ambient: (125 − 40) ÷ 3.495 = 24.3 °C/W
- Heat sink needed: 24.3 − 4 (junction to case) − 0.5 (pad) = **19.8 °C/W or lower**

A small clip-on TO-220 heat sink of about 20 °C/W is therefore right at the limit; a larger extruded one with some margin is the safer choice.

## Effect of input voltage on dissipation

For the same 5.01 V output at 0.5 A, every extra volt of input is burned in the regulator:

| Input | Headroom | Dissipation | Efficiency | Headroom check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 V | 2.99 V | 1.50 W | 63% | Marginal |
| 9 V | 3.99 V | 2.00 W | 56% | OK |
| 12 V | 6.99 V | 3.50 W | 42% | OK |
| 15 V | 9.99 V | 5.00 W | 33% | OK |
| 24 V | 18.99 V | 9.50 W | 21% | OK |

Feeding a 5 V LM317 rail from 24 V wastes more power than it delivers. Pick the lowest input that still keeps 3 V of headroom at the bottom of the ripple and at minimum mains voltage.

## Using the LM317 as a current source

With a single resistor between OUT and ADJ and the load from ADJ to ground, the LM317 regulates current instead of voltage: I = 1.25 V ÷ R. A 350 mA LED driver needs R = 1.25 ÷ 0.35 = **3.57 Ω** (use 3.6 Ω), and that resistor dissipates 0.438 W, so a 1 W part is appropriate. Add about 3 V of overhead plus the LED string voltage to find the minimum input.

## Common mistakes

- **Making R1 too large.** The divider current must cover the minimum load current (up to 10 mA on some datasheets). With R1 = 240 Ω it is 5.2 mA; with 1 kΩ only 1.25 mA, and the output can drift up at no load.
- **Putting R1 at the far end of a long wire.** Connect R1 directly at the output and adjust pins; resistance in the output lead adds to the 1.25 V reference error.
- **Leaving out protection diodes.** With a large output capacitor or a 10 µF adjust capacitor, a diode from output to input and another from adjust to output stop the capacitors discharging through the chip when input is shorted.
- **Forgetting ripple valleys.** Headroom must be met at the lowest point of the rectifier ripple, not at the average DC input.
- **Using the reference as exact.** V<sub>REF</sub> varies from 1.20 to 1.30 V between parts, so a fixed divider can be 4% off; trim if the rail must be precise.
