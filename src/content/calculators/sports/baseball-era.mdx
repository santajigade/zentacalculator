## How to use the ERA calculator

Enter **earned runs**, **innings pitched** (decimal), **hits** and **walks**. ERA and WHIP are calculated.

## ERA formulas

<p class="formula">ERA = 9 × ER ÷ IP   WHIP = (H + BB) ÷ IP</p>

## Worked example

A pitcher has allowed 45 earned runs, 130 hits and 40 walks in 150 innings:

- ERA = 9 × 45 ÷ 150 = 405 ÷ 150 = **2.70**
- WHIP = (130 + 40) ÷ 150 = 170 ÷ 150 = **1.13**

So the pitcher allows under three earned runs per nine innings and a little more than one base runner per inning, both well above league average.

## ERA and WHIP benchmarks

| Rating | ERA | WHIP |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | under 3.00 | under 1.10 |
| Above average | 3.00 to 3.75 | 1.10 to 1.25 |
| Average | 3.75 to 4.50 | 1.25 to 1.35 |
| Below average | 4.50 to 5.25 | 1.35 to 1.45 |
| Poor | over 5.25 | over 1.45 |

Rough guides for modern MLB; league scoring levels vary by year.

## Innings notation

Innings are recorded in thirds, one for each out: "6.1" means 6⅓ innings and "6.2" means 6⅔. Convert to decimals before calculating, so 6.1 becomes 6.333 and 6.2 becomes 6.667. For 3 earned runs in 6⅔ innings the correct ERA is 27 ÷ 6.667 = **4.05**; typing 6.2 instead gives 4.35, an error of 0.30 runs from a single start.

## Earned vs unearned runs

Runs that score because of fielding errors or passed balls are **unearned** and do not count towards ERA. Only earned runs reflect the pitcher's performance.

## Shorter games

For leagues with 7-inning games, ERA is sometimes scaled to 7 innings: ERA = 7 × ER ÷ IP. State which version you use.

## FIP

Fielding Independent Pitching focuses on strikeouts, walks, hit batters and home runs, which the pitcher controls most directly. It is scaled to look like ERA and often predicts future ERA better.

## How one start moves the ERA

Starting from the 2.70 ERA over 150 innings above, this is what different next starts would do:

| Next start | ERA for that start | Season ERA after | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.0 IP, 0 ER | 0.00 | 2.58 | -0.12 |
| 6.0 IP, 2 ER | 3.00 | 2.71 | +0.01 |
| 5.1 IP, 4 ER | 6.75 | 2.84 | +0.14 |
| 3.0 IP, 6 ER | 18.00 | 3.00 | +0.30 |
| 1.2 IP, 8 ER | 43.20 | 3.15 | +0.45 |

A bad short outing does far more damage than a long good one repairs, because the earned runs are spread over very few innings. Early in a season, when a pitcher has only 20 or 30 innings, a single start can move ERA by more than a run.

## FIP example

Fielding Independent Pitching uses only the outcomes a pitcher controls: FIP = (13 × HR + 3 × (BB + HBP) - 2 × K) ÷ IP + constant. With 15 home runs, 40 walks, 5 hit batters and 160 strikeouts in 150 innings, and a league constant of about 3.10, FIP = (195 + 135 - 320) ÷ 150 + 3.10 = **3.17**. An ERA well below FIP often means good defence or luck on balls in play.

## Seven inning games

High school and many youth leagues play seven innings, so ERA is often scaled by 7: the same 45 earned runs in 150 innings gives 7 × 45 ÷ 150 = **2.10**. Compare pitchers only on the same scale.

## Common mistakes

- **Typing scorebook innings as decimals.** 6.2 innings is 6.667, not 6.2, so the ERA comes out too high.
- **Counting unearned runs.** Runs that score after an error that should have ended the inning are left out of ERA, though they still count in runs allowed.
- **Including hit batters in WHIP.** WHIP counts only walks and hits; hit by pitch and errors are not added.
- **Judging small samples.** A reliever with 10 innings and 6 earned runs has a 5.40 ERA that can drop to 3.00 within a few clean outings.
- **Comparing across eras and parks.** League average ERA changes with the era and the ballpark, so adjusted figures such as ERA+ are used for fair comparisons.
