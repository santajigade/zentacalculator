## How to use the Bayes' theorem calculator

Enter the **prior probability** of A, the probability of the evidence B when A is true (**true-positive rate** or sensitivity) and when A is false (**false-positive rate**, 1 − specificity). The calculator returns the overall probability of seeing B, the posterior probability of A given B, and the probability of A despite a negative result.

## Bayes' theorem formula

<p class="formula">P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) ÷ P(B)</p>

<p class="formula">P(B) = P(B|A) × P(A) + P(B|¬A) × (1 − P(A))</p>

## Example

A condition affects 1% of people. A test detects it 95% of the time and gives a false positive 5% of the time:

- P(B) = 0.95 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = **5.90%**
- P(A|B) = 0.95 × 0.01 ÷ 0.0590 = **16.10%**
- P(A|not B) = 0.05 × 0.01 ÷ 0.9410 = **0.0531%**

Out of 10,000 people: 95 true positives and 495 false positives — so only about 1 in 6 positives has the condition.

## Posterior probability after one positive result

| Prior P(A) | Test 95% / 5% | Test 99% / 1% |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1% | 1.9% | 9.0% |
| 0.5% | 8.7% | 33.2% |
| 1% | 16.1% | 50.0% |
| 2% | 27.9% | 66.9% |
| 5% | 50.0% | 83.9% |
| 10% | 67.9% | 91.7% |
| 20% | 82.6% | 96.1% |
| 50% | 95.0% | 99.0% |

## Notes

- A second, independent positive test uses the first posterior as the new prior and raises the probability sharply.
- The same reasoning applies to alarm systems: a sensitive alarm for a rare fault produces mostly nuisance trips.

## The default case as counts

Probabilities are easier to read as people. With the default 1% prior, 95% sensitivity and 5% false-positive rate, out of 10,000 people:

| | Test positive | Test negative | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Has A | 95 (true positive) | 5 (false negative) | 100 |
| Does not have A | 495 (false positive) | 9,405 (true negative) | 9,900 |
| Total | 590 | 9,410 | 10,000 |

The posterior is the first column read downwards: 95 ÷ 590 = **16.10%**, matching the calculator. A negative result is very reliable here: 9,405 of 9,410 negatives are correct, a negative predictive value of 99.95%.

## Odds and likelihood ratios

Clinicians often use the odds form, posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. The positive likelihood ratio is 0.95 ÷ 0.05 = **19**. Prior odds of 1 : 99 = 0.0101 become 0.1919, and converting back, 0.1919 ÷ (1 + 0.1919) = 16.10%. The negative likelihood ratio is 0.05 ÷ 0.95 = 0.0526, so a negative result cuts the odds about nineteenfold. Likelihood ratios are handy because they do not depend on prevalence and can be quoted for a test once.

## Repeated positive results

If a second test is truly independent, the first posterior becomes the new prior:

| Positive tests in a row | Prior before the test | Posterior after it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.00% | 16.10% |
| 2 | 16.10% | 78.48% |
| 3 | 78.48% | 98.58% |
| 4 | 98.58% | 99.92% |

Two positives take the probability from 1% to about 78%, which is why screening programmes confirm a positive with a second, different test.

## Common mistakes

- **Swapping P(A|B) and P(B|A).** A 95% chance of a positive given the condition is not a 95% chance of the condition given a positive. This is the prosecutor's fallacy.
- **Entering specificity as the false-positive rate.** A 95% specific test has a false-positive rate of 5%, not 95%.
- **Repeating the same test and treating it as independent.** A sample that cross-reacts once will usually cross-react again, so the second result adds little.
- **Using population prevalence for a selected group.** Someone sent for testing because of symptoms has a much higher prior than the general population.
- **Entering percentages as fractions or the reverse.** Check that 1% is entered as 1% and not as 0.01%.
