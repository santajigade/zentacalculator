    A combination counts the ways to choose items from a set when order does not matter, such as lottery numbers, a committee or a sample of parts. This combination calculator works out nCr for any n and r and also gives the chance of picking one particular combination.

    Students use it for probability homework, players use it to understand lottery odds, and quality inspectors and researchers use it to work out how likely a random sample is to catch a problem.

    How to use the combination calculator

    Enter the total items and how many are chosen. You get the number of possible combinations and the chance of picking one specific combination.

    Combination formula

    nCr = n! ÷ ( r! × (n − r)! )

    Worked examples

    • 6/49 lottery: 49C6 = 13,983,816
    • Pizza: choose 3 toppings from 10 → 120 combinations
    • Teams: pick 5 players from 12 → 792 teams

    Combinations with repetition

    When items can be chosen more than once (such as scoops of ice cream from flavours):

    C(n + r − 1, r)

    3 scoops from 10 flavours, repeats allowed: C(12, 3) = 220.

    Total subsets

    The number of all possible subsets of n items (choosing any number, including none) is 2n. With 10 toppings, there are 1,024 possible pizzas.

    Symmetry

    C(n, r) = C(n, n − r). Choosing 3 items to include from 10 is the same as choosing 7 to leave out — both give 120.

    Pascal’s triangle

    nCr values form Pascal’s triangle — each number is the sum of the two above it. They are also the coefficients in (a + b)ⁿ.

    Combinations in probability

    Many probability questions are a ratio of two combination counts: the number of ways to get the result you want, divided by the total number of ways.

    Example: the chance of exactly two aces in a 5-card hand:

    • Ways to choose 2 of the 4 aces: C(4,2) = 6
    • Ways to choose the other 3 cards from the 48 non-aces: C(48,3) = 17,296
    • All possible hands: C(52,5) = 2,598,960
    • Probability = 6 × 17,296 ÷ 2,598,960 ≈ 3.99%

    Worked example: the default 6 from 49

    The defaults are n = 49 and r = 6. Writing out the factorials is not needed, because most of 49! cancels against 43!:

    • 49C6 = (49 × 48 × 47 × 46 × 45 × 44) ÷ (6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1) = 10,068,347,520 ÷ 720 = 13,983,816
    • Chance of one ticket matching all six = 1 ÷ 13,983,816 = 0.00000715%, the value the calculator shows in percent

    Odds of each prize tier in a 6/49 lottery

    To match exactly k of the 6 drawn numbers, choose k from the 6 winners and the rest from the 43 losers:

    Numbers matched Calculation Winning tickets Odds
    6 C(6,6) × C(43,0) 1 1 in 13,983,816
    5 C(6,5) × C(43,1) 258 1 in 54,201
    4 C(6,4) × C(43,2) 13,545 1 in 1,032
    3 C(6,3) × C(43,3) 246,820 1 in 57
    2 C(6,2) × C(43,4) 1,851,150 1 in 8

    Matching 3 numbers happens about once in 57 tickets, which is why the smallest prizes are paid so often while the jackpot rarely is.

    Using combinations in acceptance sampling

    An inspector draws 5 parts at random from a lot of 50 that, unknown to them, contains 3 defective parts. The chance that the sample contains no defects is the number of all-good samples divided by all samples:

    • P(no defect found) = C(47,5) ÷ C(50,5) = 1,533,939 ÷ 2,118,760 = 72.4%
    • P(exactly one defect found) = C(3,1) × C(47,4) ÷ C(50,5) = 25.3%
    • Doubling the sample to 10 parts lowers the miss chance to 50.4%

    This is the hypergeometric distribution, and the same counting answers questions about card hands, jury selection and drawing coloured balls without replacement.

    Common mistakes

    • Using permutations when order does not matter. Picking 3 people for a committee is C(n,3). Electing a president, secretary and treasurer is P(n,3).
    • Forgetting repetition. If an item can be chosen more than once (scoops of the same flavour), use combinations with repetition.
    • Double counting in “at least one” problems. It is usually easier to work out 1 − P(none).
    • Computing huge factorials on a basic calculator. 49! has 63 digits and overflows many calculators; cancel the common factors first, as in the worked example.
    • Treating r larger than n as valid. You cannot choose 7 items from 5 without repetition, so nCr is 0; use combinations with repetition if items can be reused.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is a combination?

    A selection where order doesn't matter, such as choosing 3 toppings from 10.

    What is the formula?

    nCr = n! ÷ (r! (n − r)!).

    What are the odds of winning a 6/49 lottery?

    1 in 13,983,816 for the jackpot.

    How many combinations of 3 from 10 are there?

    120.

    How many 5-card poker hands are there?

    2,598,960.

    What is 5 choose 2?

    10. There are 10 ways to choose 2 items from 5.

    How many combinations of 2 cards from a 52-card deck?

    1,326.

    How many combinations does a 4-digit code have?

    10,000 (0000 to 9999). Order matters and digits repeat, so it is 10⁴, not a combination.

    What is the probability of getting exactly two aces in a 5-card hand?

    C(4,2) × C(48,3) ÷ C(52,5) = 6 × 17,296 ÷ 2,598,960 ≈ 3.99%, or about 1 in 25 hands.

    What are the odds of matching exactly 3 numbers in a 6/49 lottery?

    C(6,3) × C(43,3) = 246,820 winning tickets out of 13,983,816, so about 1 in 57.

    What is the chance a sample of 5 misses all 3 defects in a lot of 50?

    C(47,5) ÷ C(50,5) = 72.4%. A sample that small passes a lot with 6% defective most of the time.
