## How to use the acceleration calculator

Enter the **initial speed**, **final speed** and **time** to get the acceleration in m/s² and in g. Or enter the acceleration to find the time or speed.

## Acceleration formula

<p class="formula">a = (v − u) ÷ t</p>

## Worked example

A car goes from 0 to 100 km/h in 8 seconds:

- Change in speed = 100 km/h = 27.78 m/s
- a = 27.78 ÷ 8 = **3.47 m/s²** = **0.354 g**

## Typical accelerations

| Situation | Acceleration |
|---|---|
| Family car, 0–100 km/h in 12 s | 2.3 m/s² |
| Sports car, 0–100 km/h in 3.5 s | 7.9 m/s² |
| Free fall | 9.81 m/s² (1 g) |
| Emergency braking (dry road) | about −8 m/s² |

## Deceleration and braking

Deceleration is negative acceleration. A car braking from 100 km/h to 0 in 3 seconds decelerates at about −9.26 m/s², close to 1 g — the limit for good tyres on dry roads. Distance travelled while braking is s = v² ÷ (2a); see the [SUVAT calculator](/physics/suvat-equations/).

## Acceleration in two or three dimensions

Acceleration is a vector. If an object speeds up and turns at the same time, its total acceleration combines the forward part (change in speed) and the sideways part (centripetal). Use the [centripetal force calculator](/physics/centripetal-force/) for the turning part.

## How much g can people take?

- Everyday driving: under 0.3 g.
- Roller coasters: up to about 4–5 g briefly.
- Fighter pilots with g-suits: about 9 g for short periods.

## Distance and force during the default run

With constant acceleration the average speed is (0 + 27.78) ÷ 2 = 13.89 m/s, so the car covers 13.89 × 8 = **111.1 m** while reaching 100 km/h. For a 1,500 kg car, F = m a = 1,500 × 3.472 = **5,208 N** of net forward force, and at the moment it reaches 100 km/h that force needs F × v = 145 kW at the wheels. Real cars accelerate harder at low speed and less near the top, so 3.47 m/s² is an average.

## Overtaking and braking examples

Going from 50 to 80 km/h in 4 s is (80 − 50) ÷ 3.6 ÷ 4 = **2.08 m/s²**. Braking from 100 km/h at 0.8 g (7.85 m/s²) takes 3.54 s and 49.2 m, before adding the distance covered during the driver's reaction time.

## How acceleration is measured

Phones, car data loggers and crash test dummies use MEMS accelerometers, tiny silicon masses on springs whose deflection is read capacitively. Vibration engineers use piezoelectric accelerometers that respond to thousands of hertz. Performance testers more often log GPS speed many times a second and differentiate it, which avoids the tilt error an accelerometer picks up on a sloping road. In a school lab, two light gates and a timer give the speed at two points and the time between them.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving speeds in km/h.** Divide by 3.6 first; 100 ÷ 8 = 12.5 is in km/h per second, not m/s².
- **Dropping the sign.** Slowing from 100 to 50 km/h gives a negative a; keep it so later calculations stay consistent.
- **Treating the average as the peak.** A car that averages 0.35 g may pull 0.6 g in first gear.
- **Using total time with a reaction delay.** If the timer includes a pause before moving, the acceleration comes out too low.
- **Quoting g as a force.** g is an acceleration ratio; multiply by mass and 9.80665 to get newtons.
