## How to use the Gay-Lussac's law calculator

Enter the **initial absolute pressure** and **temperature**, and the **final temperature**. The calculator gives the final absolute pressure. Enter any three of the four values to solve for the fourth — for example, the temperature at which a vessel reaches its design pressure.

## Gay-Lussac's law formula

<p class="formula">P<sub>1</sub> ÷ T<sub>1</sub> = P<sub>2</sub> ÷ T<sub>2</sub> &nbsp;⇒&nbsp; P<sub>2</sub> = P<sub>1</sub> × T<sub>2</sub> ÷ T<sub>1</sub></p>

Temperatures are absolute (K = °C + 273.15) and pressures absolute.

## Example

A sealed container holds gas at 200 kPa absolute and 20 °C. It is heated to 80 °C:

- T<sub>1</sub> = 293.15 K, T<sub>2</sub> = 353.15 K
- P<sub>2</sub> = 200 × 353.15 ÷ 293.15 = **240.93 kPa absolute** (139.6 kPa gauge)

## Pressure of a gas sealed at 200 kPa(a) and 20 °C

| Temperature | P ÷ P₁ | Absolute pressure | Gauge pressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| -20 °C | 0.864 | 172.7 kPa | 71.4 kPa |
| 0 °C | 0.932 | 186.4 kPa | 85.0 kPa |
| 20 °C | 1.000 | 200.0 kPa | 98.7 kPa |
| 40 °C | 1.068 | 213.6 kPa | 112.3 kPa |
| 60 °C | 1.136 | 227.3 kPa | 126.0 kPa |
| 80 °C | 1.205 | 240.9 kPa | 139.6 kPa |
| 100 °C | 1.273 | 254.6 kPa | 153.3 kPa |
| 150 °C | 1.443 | 288.7 kPa | 187.4 kPa |

## Solving for temperature

Leave the final temperature blank and enter a final pressure to find when a vessel reaches a limit. If the default container (200 kPa absolute at 20 °C) has a relief valve set at 300 kPa absolute:

- T<sub>2</sub> = T<sub>1</sub> × P<sub>2</sub> ÷ P<sub>1</sub> = 293.15 × 300 ÷ 200 = **439.73 K**
- In Celsius: 439.73 − 273.15 = **166.6 °C**

## The gauge pressure trap

Suppose the 200 kPa in the example was really read on a gauge. The absolute pressure is 200 + 101.325 = 301.325 kPa, and at 80 °C it becomes 301.325 × 353.15 ÷ 293.15 = 363.0 kPa absolute, or **261.7 kPa gauge**. Entering the gauge value as if it were absolute gives 240.9 kPa, an underestimate of 20.7 kPa. When the gas is heated, this error is always on the unsafe side.

## Pressure rise per degree

The pressure changes by P<sub>1</sub> ÷ T<sub>1</sub> for every kelvin, so the rise per degree grows with the fill pressure. For gases sealed at 20 °C:

| Sealed system | Absolute pressure at 20 °C | Rise per °C | At 50 °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jar sealed at atmosphere | 101.3 kPa | 0.346 kPa | 111.7 kPa |
| Default example | 200.0 kPa | 0.682 kPa | 220.5 kPa |
| Small receiver | 500.0 kPa | 1.706 kPa | 551.2 kPa |
| Air receiver, 10 bar(a) | 1,000.0 kPa | 3.411 kPa | 1,102.3 kPa |
| Gas cylinder, 200 bar(a) | 20,000.0 kPa | 68.224 kPa | 22,046.7 kPa |

A cylinder left in the sun gains about 68 kPa (0.68 bar) for every degree, which is why cylinders are stored away from heat and their filling pressure is always quoted at a reference temperature, usually 15 °C.

## Absolute zero from two readings

Gay-Lussac's law is the basis of the constant volume gas thermometer. Plot the two default points against Celsius temperature: the slope is (240.93 − 200) ÷ (80 − 20) = 0.6822 kPa per °C, and the line reaches zero pressure at 20 − 200 ÷ 0.6822 = **-273.15 °C**. Guillaume Amontons used the same idea around 1700 to estimate that an absolute zero of temperature must exist.

## Common mistakes

- **Using Celsius directly.** 200 × 80 ÷ 20 gives 800 kPa, a fourfold rise instead of the real 20.5%.
- **Using gauge pressure.** Add atmospheric pressure first, then subtract it again from the answer if a gauge value is needed.
- **Applying it to a flexible container.** A tyre or balloon expands as it warms, so its pressure rises less than the law predicts; use the combined gas law when volume changes.
- **Applying it to a liquefied gas.** A propane or CO₂ cylinder holding liquid follows the vapour pressure curve, which climbs much faster than P ∝ T.
- **Forgetting temperature gradients.** If only part of a vessel is heated, the gas is not at one temperature, so use the average gas temperature, not the hottest surface.

## Notes

- Real gases follow the law well at moderate pressures; near condensation or at very high pressure use a compressibility factor.
- Liquids trapped in a closed pipe behave very differently — thermal expansion of a liquid can raise pressure by several bar per °C, which is why thermal relief valves are fitted.
- To change pressure, volume and temperature together, use the [combined gas law calculator](/chemistry/combined-gas-law/).
