faq:
  - q: What is the geometric distribution formula?
    a: P(X = k) = (1 − p)^(k − 1) × p — k − 1 failures followed by one success. The probability of success within k trials is P(X ≤ k) = 1 − (1 − p)^k.
  - q: What is the mean of a geometric distribution?
    a: The expected number of trials up to and including the first success is 1 ÷ p, and the variance is (1 − p) ÷ p². With p = 20% you expect 5 trials on average.
  - q: What is the difference between the two definitions of the geometric distribution?
    a: This calculator counts trials up to and including the first success (k = 1, 2, 3 …). Some textbooks count only the failures before it (k = 0, 1, 2 …); then P = (1 − p)^k × p and the mean is (1 − p) ÷ p.
  - q: Is the geometric distribution memoryless?
    a: Yes — having already failed several times does not change the chance of success on the next trial. It is the discrete counterpart of the exponential distribution.
  - q: "How many trials do I need to be 95% sure of at least one success?"
    a: "Use n = ln(0.05) ÷ ln(1 − p) and round up. With p = 20% that is 14 trials, and with p = 5% it is 59 trials."
  - q: "How is the geometric distribution related to the negative binomial?"
    a: "The geometric distribution is the negative binomial with r = 1. The negative binomial counts the trials needed for r successes, so it is the sum of r independent geometric variables."

## How to use the geometric distribution calculator

Enter the **probability of success** on each trial and the **trial number k**. The calculator returns the probability that the first success happens exactly on trial k, by trial k at the latest, and not within k trials, plus the mean and standard deviation of the number of trials. Clear p and enter a target P(X ≤ k) to find the success rate needed.

## Geometric distribution formulas

<p class="formula">P(X = k) = (1 − p)<sup>k−1</sup> p &nbsp;·&nbsp; P(X ≤ k) = 1 − (1 − p)<sup>k</sup></p>

<p class="formula">μ = 1 ÷ p &nbsp;·&nbsp; σ = √(1 − p) ÷ p</p>

## Notes

- The trials must be independent with a constant success probability.
- To count successes in a fixed number of trials instead, use the [binomial probability calculator](/statistics/binomial-probability/).

## Worked example with the default inputs

A sales call succeeds 20% of the time (p = 20%). For the first sale to come on the third call (k = 3), two failures must be followed by a success:

- P(X = 3) = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.2 = **12.8%**
- P(X ≤ 3) = 1 − 0.8³ = 1 − 0.512 = **48.8%**
- P(X > 3) = 0.8³ = **51.2%**
- Mean = 1 ÷ 0.2 = **5 trials**, standard deviation = √0.8 ÷ 0.2 = **4.4721**

The median is only **4 trials**, lower than the mean of 5, because the distribution has a long right tail: a few long runs of failures pull the average up.

## How many trials for a given confidence

Solving 1 − (1 − p)<sup>n</sup> ≥ c for n gives the number of attempts needed to be c sure of at least one success:

<p class="formula">n = ⌈ ln(1 − c) ÷ ln(1 − p) ⌉</p>

For p = 20% and 95% confidence, n = ln(0.05) ÷ ln(0.8) rounded up = **14** trials, giving 95.6%.

## Where it is used

- **Inspection and testing.** If 2% of parts are defective, the chance the first defect turns up within 50 parts is 1 − 0.98⁵⁰ = 63.6%.
- **Retries in communications.** The number of transmissions until a packet gets through, when each attempt fails independently, is geometric. The mean is the expected retry cost.
- **Games and draws.** The number of rolls until a six (p = 1/6) has a mean of 6, but there is still a 16.2% chance of no six in 10 rolls.

## The memoryless property in numbers

Suppose the first five calls all failed. The chance of a sale in the next three is still 1 − 0.8³ = **48.8%**, exactly the same as P(X ≤ 3) from a fresh start. Past failures carry no information when trials are independent, so there is no such thing as being "due" a success.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up the two definitions.** Counting failures before the first success shifts k by one and changes the mean from 1/p to (1 − p)/p. Check which one a textbook or exam uses.
- **Using it when p changes.** Learning effects, fatigue or sampling without replacement from a small batch break the constant p assumption. A small batch needs the hypergeometric approach instead.
- **Reading the mean as a typical value.** With p = 20% the mean is 5 trials but the median is 4; one run in ten needs 11 or more trials.
- **Expecting a success by trial 1/p.** P(X ≤ 5) is only 67.2%, not 100%.
- **Confusing P(X = k) with P(X ≤ k).** "First success on the third try" and "a success within three tries" are different questions with different answers.
