    Storage spheres for LPG, ammonia and other pressurised liquids hold more or less than a simple level percentage suggests, because the cross-section changes with height. This spherical tank volume calculator uses the spherical-cap formula to turn a liquid level into a volume.

    Terminal operators, instrument engineers and inventory accountants use it to build level-to-volume tables, set high-level alarms and check fill limits. See LPG level measurement in bullets and spheres.

    How to use the spherical tank volume calculator

    Enter the inside diameter of the sphere and the liquid level measured from the bottom. The calculator returns the liquid volume, the full capacity and the percent filled. To find the level for a given volume, clear the level and enter the volume.

    Spherical tank volume formula

    V = π h² (3R − h) ÷ 3  ·  Vtotal = π D³ ÷ 6

    Worked example

    A 10 m sphere (R = 5 m) with 4 m of liquid:

    • V = π × 4² × (15 − 4) ÷ 3 = 184.31 m³
    • Capacity = π × 10³ ÷ 6 = 523.60 m³
    • Filled = 184.31 ÷ 523.60 = 35.2% — at 40% of the level

    Level-to-volume table for a 10 m sphere

    Level Volume % of capacity

    20% (2.0 m) 54.45 m³ 10.4%

    Notes

    • Level and volume are only equal (in percent) at 0, 50% and 100%.
    • For mass, multiply the volume by the liquid density at the measured temperature.

    From volume to mass

    Inventory is usually reported in tonnes. In the worked example, if the 4 m of liquid is propane at about 500 kg/m³ (its density near 20 °C), the stock is 184.31 × 500 = 92.2 t. Use the density at the measured liquid temperature: liquid propane gets noticeably lighter as it warms, so the same volume holds less mass on a hot afternoon.

    How level is measured on spheres

    • Radar or servo gauges on a still pipe on the top of the sphere measure the liquid surface directly. Because the vessel is under pressure, the gauge sits on a pressure-rated nozzle or behind a ball valve.
    • Differential pressure transmitters need the vapour pressure on the low side, and the vapour density matters at high pressure. See the DP level range calculator.
    • Strapping tables from a calibration survey replace the ideal formula for custody transfer, because real spheres are slightly out of round and have legs, nozzles and internal pipes.

    The fraction of the diameter needed for a given percent of volume is the same for every sphere, so this table can be scaled to any size (the last column is for the 10 m example):

    25% 32.6% of D 3.26 m
    50% 50.0% of D 5.00 m
    75% 67.4% of D 6.74 m
    90% 80.4% of D 8.04 m
    95% 86.5% of D 8.65 m

    For example, to hold 300 m³ in the 10 m sphere the level must reach 5.49 m. The calculator solves the same cubic when you clear the level and enter a volume.

    How much volume one millimetre of level represents

    The liquid surface is a circle of area π h (D − h), so a small level change Δh changes the volume by that area times Δh. It is largest at the equator (78.5 m² for a 10 m sphere) and shrinks to nothing at the top and bottom:

    Level Surface area Volume per mm of level
    0.5 m 14.9 m² 14.9 L
    2.0 m 50.3 m² 50.3 L
    4.0 m 75.4 m² 75.4 L
    5.0 m 78.5 m² 78.5 L
    8.0 m 50.3 m² 50.3 L
    9.5 m 14.9 m² 14.9 L

    At the 4 m example level a radar gauge with ±3 mm accuracy leaves an uncertainty of 75.4 × 0.003 = 226 L, or about 113 kg of propane. Near the top the same gauge error is worth far less volume, which helps when setting fill limits.

    Common mistakes

    • Treating level percent as volume percent. At 40% level a sphere is only 35.2% full; at 90% level it is already 97.2% full.
    • Using the outside diameter. Shell plates on large spheres can be 40 to 60 mm thick, which changes the capacity by around 2 to 4% on a 10 m sphere.
    • Measuring level from the wrong datum. The formula needs the depth from the lowest inside point. Gauges are often referenced to a nozzle or flange, so add the offset first.
    • Forgetting the bottom fittings. Liquid trapped in the drain sump and outlet piping below the sphere is not part of the cap volume.
    • Setting alarms by level instead of volume. A high-level alarm for 85% fill must be set at about 76% of the height on any sphere, not at 85%.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the formula for a partially filled sphere?

    V = π h² (3R − h) ÷ 3, where R is the radius and h the liquid depth. It is the volume of a spherical cap and holds for any level from 0 to 2R.

    Why is 50% level exactly 50% volume?

    A sphere is symmetrical about its equator, so half the height holds half the volume. Below and above the middle, volume changes much more slowly per unit level than in a vertical cylinder.

    How full should an LPG sphere be filled?

    Typically to no more than 85% of volume to leave vapour space for thermal expansion — check the local regulation and the design.

    Does the calculator use inside or outside diameter?

    Inside diameter. Subtract twice the wall thickness from the outside diameter for an accurate capacity.

    At what level is a sphere 85% full?

    At 75.6% of its inside diameter, whatever the size. For a 10 m sphere that is 7.56 m of liquid. The ratio is the same for every sphere because the shape scales.

    How much volume does one millimetre of level represent in a sphere?

    It equals the liquid surface area π h (D − h) times 1 mm. In a 10 m sphere that is 75.4 L at 4 m of level, 78.5 L at the 5 m equator and only 14.9 L at 9.5 m.

    What level gives 25% of the volume in a spherical tank?

    32.6% of the inside diameter for any sphere, which is 3.26 m in a 10 m sphere. 75% of the volume is reached at 67.4% of the diameter.
