## How to use the de Broglie wavelength calculator

Choose the **particle** and enter its **velocity**. The calculator returns the momentum, the de Broglie wavelength in nanometres and the kinetic energy in electronvolts. To find the velocity needed for a given wavelength, clear the velocity and enter the wavelength.

## de Broglie wavelength formula

<p class="formula">λ = h ÷ p = h ÷ (m v)</p>

<p class="formula">KE = ½ m v² &nbsp;·&nbsp; λ = h ÷ √(2 m KE)</p>

## Worked example with the default values

An electron (m = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg) moving at 1 × 10⁶ m/s:

1. Momentum p = m v = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ × 1 × 10⁶ = **9.1094 × 10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s**
2. Wavelength λ = h ÷ p = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ 9.1094 × 10⁻²⁵ = 7.2739 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = **0.7274 nm**
3. Kinetic energy = ½ m v² = 4.5547 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, and dividing by 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J/eV gives **2.843 eV**

Check: λ = h ÷ √(2 m KE) with KE in joules gives 0.7274 nm again. The speed is only 0.33% of the speed of light, so the relativistic factor γ = 1.0000056 changes the momentum by about 0.0006%, far below the precision shown.

## Wavelength of electrons accelerated through a voltage

| Accelerating voltage | Non-relativistic λ | Relativistic λ |
|---|---|---|
| 10 V | 387.830 pm | 387.828 pm |
| 100 V | 122.643 pm | 122.637 pm |
| 1,000 V | 38.783 pm | 38.764 pm |
| 10,000 V | 12.264 pm | 12.205 pm |
| 100,000 V | 3.878 pm | 3.701 pm |
| 300,000 V | 2.239 pm | 1.969 pm |

Transmission electron microscopes run at 100–300 kV, giving wavelengths of a few picometres — far below the 400–700 nm of visible light.

## Notes

- For an electron accelerated from rest through V volts, λ (nm) ≈ 1.226 ÷ √V (non-relativistic).
- Thermal neutrons (about 2,200 m/s) have λ ≈ 0.18 nm, ideal for neutron diffraction.
- The [photon energy calculator](/physics/photon-energy/) does the equivalent conversion for light, where λ = h c ÷ E.

## Same speed, different particles

At the default 1 × 10⁶ m/s, the heavier particles have far shorter wavelengths and far more energy:

| Particle | Wavelength | Kinetic energy |
|---|---|---|
| Electron | 727.4 pm | 2.843 eV |
| Proton | 0.3961 pm | 5,220 eV |
| Neutron | 0.3956 pm | 5,227 eV |
| Alpha particle | 0.09972 pm | 20,736 eV |

A proton and a neutron at the same speed have almost the same wavelength because only momentum matters, not charge.

## How matter waves are measured

The first direct proof came from the Davisson and Germer experiment in 1927. Electrons accelerated through 54 V have λ = h ÷ √(2 m e V) = **0.1669 nm**. Scattered from a nickel crystal with a surface row spacing of 0.215 nm, they showed a strong peak at 50°, and the grating condition d sin θ = λ gives 0.215 × sin 50° = 0.1647 nm, within about 1% of the prediction.

Today the same idea is used every day. Electron diffraction in a transmission electron microscope reveals crystal structure, low energy electron diffraction (LEED) maps surfaces, and neutron diffraction uses reactor neutrons near 2,200 m/s (0.180 nm) to locate light atoms such as hydrogen that X-rays barely see. To get a 0.1 nm electron, roughly one atomic spacing, the electron needs a speed of 7.274e+06 m/s, which you can check by clearing the velocity box and entering 0.1 nm.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the photon formula.** λ = h c ÷ E is only for light. For a particle with mass, use λ = h ÷ p or h ÷ √(2 m KE).
- **Putting energy in eV straight into the formula.** Convert eV to joules first by multiplying by 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹, or the answer is out by a factor of about 2.5 × 10⁹.
- **Ignoring relativity at high voltage.** Above about 10 kV for electrons the non-relativistic λ is noticeably too long, as the voltage table shows.
- **Mixing up nm and pm.** 0.7274 nm is 727.4 pm; electron microscope wavelengths are normally quoted in picometres.
- **Using grams.** Masses must be in kilograms; the particle menu already supplies them in kg.
