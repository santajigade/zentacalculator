    The 4–20 mA current loop is the standard analog signal in process instrumentation: 4 mA means 0% of the range and 20 mA means 100%. Converting between current and process value is daily work for instrument engineers — see the 4 to 20 mA current loop explained and PLC analog scaling from raw counts. This free 4–20 mA scaling calculator solves for whichever value you leave blank and shows the formula and steps.

    How to use the 4–20 mA scaling calculator

    1. Enter the transmitter’s range — the value at 4 mA (low) and at 20 mA (high), in any engineering unit such as bar, °C, m or m³/h.
    2. Enter the loop current to get the process value and percent of span.

    To check a loop the other way round, press × on Loop current and type the process value — the calculator shows the current the transmitter should send. Reverse-acting ranges (for example 100 at 4 mA and 0 at 20 mA) work too.

    For more background, read 4 to 20 mA Signal Conversion Calculator.

    4–20 mA scaling formula

    % span = (I − 4 mA) ÷ 16 mA
    value = low + % span × (high − low)

    Worked examples

    Pressure transmitter, 0–10 bar, reading 12 mA

    • % span = (12 − 4) ÷ 16 = 50%
    • Value = 0 + 0.5 × 10 = 5 bar

    Temperature transmitter, 0–250 °C, reading 16.8 mA

    • % span = (16.8 − 4) ÷ 16 = 80%
    • Value = 0.8 × 250 = 200 °C

    Quick reference

    Current % of span
    4 mA 0%
    8 mA 25%
    12 mA 50%
    16 mA 75%
    20 mA 100%

    NAMUR NE 43 fault levels

    Current Meaning
    ≤ 3.6 mA Fault (downscale failure)
    3.8 – 4.0 mA Under-range, still valid
    20.0 – 20.5 mA Over-range, still valid
    ≥ 21 mA Fault (upscale failure)

    Check your transmitter’s manual — some manufacturers use slightly different limits.

    Reverse scaling: process value to mA

    To find the current a transmitter should output for a known process value:

    I = 4 + 16 × (V − L) ÷ (H − L)

    For a 0–10 bar transmitter reading 6.5 bar: I = 4 + 16 × 0.65 = 14.4 mA. In the calculator, press × on Loop current and type the process value.

    Why 4–20 mA?

    • Live zero: 4 mA at 0% lets you tell a real zero from a broken wire (0 mA).
    • Current is immune to wire resistance: the same current flows through the whole loop, so long cables do not change the reading, within the supply’s limits.
    • Loop-powered devices: 4 mA is enough to power two-wire transmitters.

    Loop power budget

    The supply must cover every voltage drop in the loop:

    Vsupply ≥ Vtransmitter min + Imax × (Rload + Rcable)

    With a 24 V supply, a transmitter needing 12 V and a 250 Ω input card, the maximum cable resistance at 20 mA (or 22 mA for alarms) is (24 − 12) ÷ 0.022 − 250 ≈ 295 Ω.

    Reversed and split ranges

    • Reverse acting: 20 mA at 0% and 4 mA at 100%. Enter the range high as the value at 4 mA and the range low as the value at 20 mA.
    • Split range: two valves share one signal, e.g. valve A over 4–12 mA and valve B over 12–20 mA. Scale each half separately.

    HART on top of 4–20 mA

    HART adds a digital signal on top of the analog current, letting you read the tag, range and diagnostics without disturbing the 4–20 mA value. Check the transmitter’s LRV and URV via HART when commissioning to make sure they match the DCS scaling.

    Converting mA to volts

    Many PLC inputs read the loop as a voltage across a 250 Ω resistor, giving 1–5 V. Use the Ohm’s law calculator to check other resistor values.

    Troubleshooting a 4 to 20 mA loop

    Symptom Likely cause
    0 mA Open loop, blown fuse, no supply or reversed polarity
    3.6 mA or below, steady Transmitter reports a fault (NAMUR NE 43 low alarm)
    21 mA or above Fault high alarm, or process far above range
    Stuck at 4 mA Wrong range, sensor damaged, or the process really is at its low limit
    Reading off by a fixed ratio Range set in the DCS differs from the transmitter LRV and URV
    Noisy or jumping reading Shield grounded at both ends, cable run next to VFD or power cables

    To measure the loop current without breaking it, use a clamp meter for mA, or put a meter across the transmitter’s test terminals if it has them.

    Common mistakes

    • Different ranges in the transmitter and the DCS. Both ends must use the same LRV and URV, or every reading is scaled wrongly.
    • Double square-root extraction on differential pressure flow. Apply it in the transmitter or in the DCS, never both.
    • Too little loop voltage. Supply voltage minus the drop across the loop resistance must stay above the transmitter’s minimum, or it cannot reach 20 mA.
    • Treating 0 mA as zero. In a live-zero loop, 0 mA always means a broken loop, not an empty tank or zero flow.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I convert 4–20 mA to a process value?

    Value = low + (mA − 4) ÷ 16 × (high − low). For 12 mA on a 0–10 bar transmitter, (12 − 4) ÷ 16 × 10 = 5 bar.

    Why does the signal start at 4 mA instead of 0?

    The 4 mA "live zero" powers two-wire transmitters and lets the system tell a real zero reading (4 mA) from a broken wire (0 mA).

    What do currents below 4 mA or above 20 mA mean?

    Many devices follow NAMUR NE 43. Readings below about 3.6 mA or above about 21 mA signal a fault, such as a sensor failure or open circuit.

    Can I calculate the mA output for a given value?

    Yes. Press × on Loop current, then type the process value. The calculator gives the expected current, useful for loop checks.

    What is 12 mA in percent?

    50% of span. 4 mA is 0% and 20 mA is 100%, so each 1.6 mA is 10%.

    What does each mA represent in 4 to 20 mA?

    Each 1 mA is 6.25% of the measuring span, because the 16 mA span covers 100%.

    How do I convert 4 to 20 mA to 1 to 5 V?

    Pass the loop current through a 250 Ω resistor. 4 mA gives 1 V and 20 mA gives 5 V.

    Why is my flow reading wrong after scaling a DP transmitter?

    Check where square-root extraction is done. If both the transmitter and the DCS apply it, the reading is badly wrong. Use it in one place only.
