## How to use the generator power angle calculator

Enter the generator's **internal EMF** and the **terminal (bus) voltage**, both line-to-line, the **synchronous reactance per phase** and the **power angle δ**. The calculator returns the three-phase active power, the maximum (pull-out) power and the reactive power delivered — negative Q means the machine is absorbing reactive power. To find the load angle for a given output, enter P and clear δ.

## Power angle equations

<p class="formula">P = (E × V ÷ X<sub>s</sub>) × sin δ &nbsp;·&nbsp; P<sub>max</sub> = E × V ÷ X<sub>s</sub></p>

<p class="formula">Q = (E × V × cos δ − V²) ÷ X<sub>s</sub></p>

## Example

An 11 kV generator with E = 12 kV, X<sub>s</sub> = 10 Ω per phase, running at δ = 20°:

- P<sub>max</sub> = 12,000 × 11,000 ÷ 10 = **13.2 MW**
- P = 13.2 × sin 20° = **4.515 MW**
- Q = (12,000 × 11,000 × cos 20° − 11,000²) ÷ 10 = **0.304 MVAr** (exporting)

## Power and reactive power vs load angle

Same machine (E = 12 kV, V = 11 kV, X<sub>s</sub> = 10 Ω):

| δ | sin δ | Active power | Reactive power |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0° | 0.000 | 0.00 MW | +1.10 MVAr |
| 10° | 0.174 | 2.29 MW | +0.90 MVAr |
| 20° | 0.342 | 4.51 MW | +0.30 MVAr |
| 30° | 0.500 | 6.60 MW | -0.67 MVAr |
| 45° | 0.707 | 9.33 MW | -2.77 MVAr |
| 60° | 0.866 | 11.43 MW | -5.50 MVAr |
| 75° | 0.966 | 12.75 MW | -8.68 MVAr |
| 90° | 1.000 | 13.20 MW | -12.10 MVAr |

## Notes

- Armature resistance is neglected — typically less than 1% of X<sub>s</sub> for large machines.
- In per-unit, P = E V sin δ ÷ X<sub>s</sub> with E, V and X<sub>s</sub> in pu; see the [per-unit calculator](/electrical/per-unit/).
- During a fault the transient reactance X′<sub>d</sub> replaces X<sub>s</sub> in transient-stability studies.

## Finding the load angle for a set output

Run the equation backwards to see how hard the machine is working. For the default generator to deliver 9 MW:

- sin δ = 9 ÷ 13.2 = 0.6818, so δ = **43.0°**
- Q = (12,000 × 11,000 × cos 43.0° − 11,000²) ÷ 10 = **-2.44 MVAr**

At this point the machine is absorbing reactive power, because 12 kV of EMF is no longer enough to export MVAr at that angle. The operator would raise the excitation, which lowers δ and restores a lagging power factor.

## Raising excitation at constant power

Holding the default 4.515 MW output while the AVR changes the EMF:

| Internal EMF E | Load angle δ | Pull-out power | Reactive power |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 kV | 24.2° | 11.00 MW | -2.07 MVAr |
| 11 kV | 21.9° | 12.10 MW | -0.87 MVAr |
| 12 kV | 20.0° | 13.20 MW | +0.30 MVAr |
| 13 kV | 18.4° | 14.30 MW | +1.47 MVAr |
| 14 kV | 17.0° | 15.40 MW | +2.62 MVAr |

Higher excitation gives a smaller load angle and a larger pull-out power, so an over-excited machine is more stable. Running under-excited to absorb MVAr pushes δ up and erodes the margin, which is why under-excitation limiters exist.

## In practice

The synchronous reactance of 10 Ω is 0.826 pu on a 10 MVA, 11 kV base, typical of a round-rotor machine. The angle itself is rarely measured directly; the AVR, the capability chart and the pole-slip relay work from terminal voltage, current and power. A power system stabiliser adds damping so that δ does not swing after a disturbance. Real machines also have a step-up transformer and line reactance between them and the grid, which add to X<sub>s</sub> and lower the pull-out power.

## Common mistakes

- **Using phase voltages without multiplying by 3.** With E and V entered as 6.93 kV and 6.35 kV the formula gives 1.505 MW, one third of the real 4.515 MW.
- **Leaving a spreadsheet in radians.** SIN(20) in Excel treats 20 as radians and returns 12.05 MW; use SIN(RADIANS(20)).
- **Using sub-transient reactance for steady state.** X″<sub>d</sub> is for fault currents; steady-state power uses X<sub>s</sub>, and transient stability uses X′<sub>d</sub>.
- **Treating P<sub>max</sub> as an operating point.** At 90° any small disturbance causes pole slipping; continuous operation stays well below it.
- **Reading E from the terminals under load.** E is the open-circuit voltage for the present field current, not the measured terminal voltage.
