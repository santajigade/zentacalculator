## How to use the kVA to kW converter

Enter the **apparent power in kVA** and the **power factor**. The calculator gives real power (kW) and reactive power (kVAr). To go from kW to kVA, press × on kVA and type the kW.

## KVA to kW formulas

<p class="formula">kW = kVA × PF &nbsp;·&nbsp; kVA = kW ÷ PF &nbsp;·&nbsp; kVAr = √(kVA² − kW²)</p>

These come from the **power triangle**: real power (kW) and reactive power (kVAr) are at right angles, and apparent power (kVA) is the hypotenuse.

## Generator (DG set) sizing

1. Add up the kW of all loads that may run together.
2. Divide by the generator's rated power factor (usually 0.8) to get kVA.
3. Allow for motor starting currents — direct-on-line motors draw 6–8 times their running current briefly.
4. Add a margin of 20–25% so the generator does not run continuously near full load.

**Example**: 60 kW of loads ÷ 0.8 = 75 kVA; with a 25% margin, choose about **100 kVA**.

## UPS sizing

UPS units list both VA and W. A 1,000 VA UPS rated at 0.6 PF supports only 600 W; modern units often have 0.9–1.0 PF. Size by **watts** for the connected load.

## Power factor of common loads

| Load | Typical PF |
|---|---|
| Resistive heaters, incandescent lamps | 1.0 |
| Induction motor, full load | 0.80–0.90 |
| Induction motor, light load | 0.3–0.6 |
| LED drivers and SMPS (with PFC) | 0.9–0.99 |
| Older fluorescent lighting | 0.5–0.9 |

## Improving power factor

Capacitor banks supply reactive power locally so less has to come from the grid. Raising PF from 0.75 to 0.95 on a 100 kW load cuts apparent power from 133 kVA to 105 kVA, reducing current, cable losses and transformer loading. The capacitor bank needed is the difference in kVAr: 55 kVAr.

## Worked example

With the default inputs, 100 kVA at a power factor of 0.8:

- Real power P = 100 × 0.8 = **80 kW**
- Reactive power Q = √(100² − 80²) = **60 kVAr**

The 3-4-5 triangle is a handy check: at PF 0.8 the kW, kVAr and kVA are always in the ratio 4 : 3 : 5.

Below about 0.9 the reactive part grows quickly, which is why a poor power factor loads a transformer far more than its kW suggests.

## From kVA to amps

The current that sets cable and breaker sizes follows from kVA alone, with no power factor: I = S ÷ (√3 × V). For 100 kVA that is **139 A** at 415 V and **144 A** at 400 V.

## Checking transformer loading

A plant drawing 720 kW at PF 0.82 from a 1,000 kVA transformer loads it to 878 kVA, or **88%**. If the power factor were corrected to 0.95, the same 878 kVA would carry 834 kW, so correction frees capacity without a new transformer. Read kW, kVA and PF together on the main incomer's multifunction meter or a power analyser; the kVA maximum demand on the electricity bill shows the same thing averaged over the billing period.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding kVA of loads with different power factors directly.** A 50 kVA load at 0.8 PF and a 30 kVA heater at 1.0 PF do not total 80 kVA. Add kW (70) and kVAr (30) separately: the total is √(70² + 30²) = **76.2 kVA** at PF 0.92.
- **Treating a UPS or inverter VA rating as watts.** A 10 kVA UPS with a 0.9 output PF delivers at most 9 kW.
- **Loading a 0.8 PF generator with unity PF loads up to its kVA.** A 100 kVA set has an engine sized for about 80 kW; heaters totalling 100 kW would overload it.
- **Using displacement PF where harmonics are high.** With drives and rectifiers, true PF is lower than cos φ, so kVA is higher than kW ÷ cos φ suggests.
- **Using the kVAr formula with a PF above 1 or kW above kVA.** Power factor cannot exceed 1; a reading that implies it usually means a wiring or CT polarity error on the meter.
