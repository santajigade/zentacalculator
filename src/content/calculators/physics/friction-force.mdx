## How to use the friction force calculator

Enter the **coefficient of friction** and the **mass** of an object on a level surface — the normal force N = mg is calculated for you — and you get the friction force, the angle of repose and the deceleration of the object if it slides freely to a stop. For a sloped surface or an extra pressing force, clear the mass and enter the **normal force** directly, or use the [inclined plane calculator](/physics/inclined-plane/). To find μ, enter the measured friction force and the mass and clear the coefficient.

## Friction formula

<p class="formula">F<sub>f</sub> = μ × N &nbsp;·&nbsp; N = m × g (level surface) &nbsp;·&nbsp; θ<sub>repose</sub> = arctan μ<sub>s</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; a = μ<sub>k</sub> × g</p>

Use the static coefficient μ<sub>s</sub> for the force needed to start an object moving and the kinetic coefficient μ<sub>k</sub> for the force needed to keep it moving. g = 9.80665 m/s².

## Worked example

A 10 kg crate on a floor with μ = 0.4:

- Normal force N = 10 × 9.80665 = **98.07 N**
- Friction force F<sub>f</sub> = 0.4 × 98.07 = **39.23 N** — the force needed to keep it sliding
- Angle of repose θ = arctan 0.4 = **21.80°** — a ramp steeper than this lets the crate slide on its own
- If pushed and released, it decelerates at 0.4 × 9.80665 = **3.923 m/s²**

**Finding μ:** a force gauge reads 147.1 N while dragging a 25 kg box at a steady speed, so μ<sub>k</sub> = 147.1 ÷ (25 × 9.80665) = **0.600**.

## Pulling or pushing at an angle

The default crate needs 39.23 N when the force is horizontal, but tilting the force changes the normal force. Pulling upward at angle α lifts some of the weight, so P = μ m g ÷ (cos α + μ sin α); pushing downward at α presses the crate harder, so P = μ m g ÷ (cos α − μ sin α). For the 10 kg crate with μ = 0.4:

| Angle of the force | Pulling upward | Pushing downward |
|---|---|---|
| 0° | 39.23 N | 39.23 N |
| 10° | 37.21 N | 42.85 N |
| 20° | 36.44 N | 48.86 N |
| 30° | 36.80 N | 58.90 N |
| 45° | 39.62 N | 92.46 N |

The easiest pull is at α = arctan μ = 21.80°, the same as the angle of repose, where only **36.42 N** is needed. Pushing down at 30° takes 58.90 N against 36.80 N for pulling up at 30°, which is why a sled or a heavy box is easier to pull with a rope than to push. Beyond arctan(1 ÷ μ) = 68.2° a downward push can never move the crate, however hard you press.

## Sliding to a stop

If the crate is shoved to 2 m/s and released, it decelerates at 3.923 m/s² and stops after v² ÷ (2 μ g) = **0.510 m** in 0.510 s. Friction does 39.23 N × 0.510 m = 20 J of work, exactly the kinetic energy ½ m v² the crate started with, and that energy ends up as heat in the two surfaces.

## Measuring friction in practice

Testing standards such as ASTM D1894 for plastic films drag a weighted sled across a sample at a fixed speed and record the force: the first peak gives the static coefficient and the steady plateau the kinetic one. On site, a spring balance on a loaded pallet does the same job. Repeat each test a few times, because dust, moisture and surface wear easily change μ by 20% or more.

## Common mistakes

- **Multiplying μ by the mass instead of the weight.** 0.4 × 10 kg gives 4, which is not a force; the correct friction is 0.4 × 98.07 N = 39.23 N.
- **Treating static friction as always equal to μ<sub>s</sub> N.** μ<sub>s</sub> N is only the maximum. A crate pushed with 20 N that does not move feels exactly 20 N of friction.
- **Using the weight as the normal force on a slope or with an angled push.** The normal force changes with the angle, as the table above shows.
- **Assuming μ cannot exceed 1.** Rubber on dry concrete, silicone and clean metals in vacuum can reach 1 or more; it simply means the friction force is larger than the normal force.
- **Using sliding friction for a rolling wheel.** Rolling resistance coefficients are ten to a hundred times smaller than sliding ones.

## Practical notes

- **Rolling is much easier than sliding:** the rolling resistance coefficient of a steel wheel on a rail is about 0.001 and a car tyre on asphalt about 0.01, compared with 0.3–0.8 for sliding.
- **Stopping distance:** an object sliding at speed v stops in v² ÷ (2 μ g) — a car at 50 km/h (13.9 m/s) with locked wheels on dry concrete (μ ≈ 0.8) needs about 12.3 m.
- **Bolted joints:** friction under the nut and in the threads absorbs about 90% of the tightening torque, which is why the friction factor matters so much in the [bolt torque calculator](/engineering/bolt-torque/).
- To include the work done against friction over a distance, use the [work and power calculator](/physics/work-and-power/).
