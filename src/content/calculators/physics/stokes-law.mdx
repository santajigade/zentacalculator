## How to use the Stokes' law calculator

Enter the **particle diameter**, the **particle density**, the **fluid density** and the fluid's **dynamic viscosity**. The calculator returns the terminal settling velocity (negative means the particle rises), the drag force at that speed and the **particle Reynolds number** — keep it below about 1 for Stokes' law to apply. To find viscosity from a falling-ball test, clear the viscosity and enter the measured velocity.

## Stokes' law formula

<p class="formula">v<sub>t</sub> = (ρ<sub>p</sub> − ρ<sub>f</sub>) g d² ÷ (18 μ)</p>

<p class="formula">F<sub>d</sub> = 3 π μ d v &nbsp;·&nbsp; Re<sub>p</sub> = ρ<sub>f</sub> v d ÷ μ</p>

## Example

A 50 µm quartz sand grain (2,650 kg/m³) settling in water at 20 °C (998 kg/m³, 1.0 mPa·s):

- v = (2650 − 998) × 9.807 × (50 × 10⁻⁶)² ÷ (18 × 0.001) = **0.002250 m/s** (2.25 mm/s)
- Re = 998 × 0.002250 × 50 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.001 = **0.112** — below 1, so Stokes' law applies
- Drag force = 3π × 0.001 × 50 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.002250 = **1.060 × 10⁻⁹ N**, equal to the grain's submerged weight

## Settling of quartz particles in water at 20 °C

| Diameter | Settling velocity | Reynolds number | Time to settle 1 m |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 µm | 0.9 µm/s | 9.0 × 10⁻⁷ | 309 h |
| 5 µm | 22.5 µm/s | 1.1 × 10⁻⁴ | 12.3 h |
| 10 µm | 90 µm/s | 9.0 × 10⁻⁴ | 3.09 h |
| 20 µm | 360 µm/s | 7.2 × 10⁻³ | 46.3 min |
| 50 µm | 2.25 mm/s | 0.11 | 7.41 min |
| 100 µm | 9 mm/s | 0.9 | 1.85 min |

At 100 µm the Reynolds number approaches 1, so Stokes' law starts to overpredict the velocity of larger grains.

## Notes

- Viscosity changes strongly with temperature — water at 5 °C is about 1.5 mPa·s, slowing settling by a third.
- Non-spherical particles settle more slowly than the equivalent sphere.
- For larger objects, use the [drag force calculator](/physics/drag-force/) with a drag coefficient instead.

## Sizing a settling tank from the result

The default 50 µm sand grain settles at 2.250 mm/s. In a clarifier 3 m deep it needs 3 ÷ 0.002250 = 1333 s, about **22 minutes**, to reach the floor from the surface. Designers turn that velocity into a surface overflow rate: any particle that settles faster than the flow rate divided by the tank plan area is captured. For 2.25 mm/s that is 8.10 m³ per m² of tank area per hour, so a flow of 50 m³/h needs at least 6.2 m² of surface, before a safety factor of 1.5 to 2 for turbulence and short circuiting.

## Temperature changes the answer

Viscosity of water falls quickly as it warms, and settling speed rises in proportion. The same 50 µm quartz grain:

| Water temperature | Viscosity | Settling velocity | Time to fall 1 m |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 °C | 1.519 mPa·s | 1.48 mm/s | 11.3 min |
| 10 °C | 1.306 mPa·s | 1.72 mm/s | 9.7 min |
| 20 °C | 1.002 mPa·s | 2.25 mm/s | 7.4 min |
| 30 °C | 0.797 mPa·s | 2.83 mm/s | 5.9 min |
| 40 °C | 0.653 mPa·s | 3.46 mm/s | 4.8 min |

A plant that settles well in summer can carry fines over the weir in winter for this reason alone.

## Measuring viscosity with a falling ball

A 2 mm steel ball (7,800 kg/m³) dropped in glycerol (1,260 kg/m³) takes 9.89 s to fall between two marks 100 mm apart, so v = 10.11 mm/s. Rearranged, μ = (ρp − ρf) g d² ÷ (18 v) = **1.41 Pa·s**, and Re = 0.018, safely in the Stokes range. Start timing only after the ball has fallen a few centimetres so it is at terminal speed, and use a tube at least 10 times the ball diameter, or apply a wall correction, because nearby walls slow the ball.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up mPa·s and Pa·s.** Water is 1.0 mPa·s, which is 0.001 Pa·s; entering 1 Pa·s makes the velocity 1,000 times too small.
- **Using radius in the 18 μ form.** The equation here uses diameter. The radius form is v = 2 (ρp − ρf) g r² ÷ (9 μ); swapping one for the other gives a factor of 4 error.
- **Skipping the Reynolds check.** Above Re ≈ 1 the true drag is higher than 3πμdv, so Stokes' law overstates the speed of coarse sand and gravel.
- **Treating flocs as solid grains.** A floc of fine particles holds a lot of water, so its effective density may be only 1,010 to 1,100 kg/m³.
- **Forgetting that air is a fluid too.** A 10 µm dust particle (2,000 kg/m³) in air settles at only 6.0 mm/s, taking about 7 minutes to fall 2.5 m, which is why fine dust hangs in a room.
