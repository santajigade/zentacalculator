## How to use the buck converter calculator

Enter the **input** and **output voltage**, **output current**, **switching frequency**, the **inductor ripple** as a percentage of output current, and the allowed **output voltage ripple**. You get the duty cycle, ripple current, inductance, peak inductor current and minimum output capacitance.

For more background, read [Synchronous Rectification in Buck Converters](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/synchronous-rectification-buck-converter/).

## The formulas (continuous conduction)

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Duty cycle | D = V<sub>o</sub> ÷ V<sub>i</sub> |
| Inductance | L = V<sub>o</sub>(V<sub>i</sub> − V<sub>o</sub>) ÷ (V<sub>i</sub> f ΔI) |
| Peak current | I<sub>pk</sub> = I<sub>o</sub> + ΔI ÷ 2 |
| Output capacitance | C = ΔI ÷ (8 f ΔV) |

## Example

24 V to 5 V at 2 A, 500 kHz, 30% ripple (0.6 A):

- D = **20.8%**
- L = 5 × 19 ÷ (24 × 500,000 × 0.6) = **13.2 µH** → use 15 µH
- Peak current = **2.3 A** — choose an inductor rated above this for saturation
- For 20 mV ripple, C ≥ 0.6 ÷ (8 × 500,000 × 0.02) = **7.5 µF** → use 22 µF ceramic

## Inductance vs frequency (24 V → 5 V, 0.6 A ripple)

| Frequency | Inductance |
|---|---|
| 100 kHz | 66.0 µH |
| 250 kHz | 26.4 µH |
| 500 kHz | 13.2 µH |
| 1 MHz | 6.6 µH |
| 2 MHz | 3.3 µH |

Higher frequency allows smaller inductors and capacitors but increases switching losses.

## Design checks

- Inductor saturation current above I<sub>pk</sub> with margin; RMS rating above I<sub>o</sub>.
- Input capacitor sized for RMS ripple current ≈ I<sub>o</sub>√(D(1 − D)).
- Use the controller datasheet's compensation and layout guidance — tight loops around the switch, diode and input capacitor matter most.

## After choosing real parts

The calculation asks for 13.2 µH, so a standard **15 µH** inductor is fitted. Larger inductance means less ripple: ΔI = 5 × 19 ÷ (24 × 500,000 × 15 µH) = **0.528 A**, and the peak current falls to **2.264 A**. The converter stays in continuous conduction down to a load of about ΔI ÷ 2, roughly 0.26 A with 15 µH (0.3 A with the calculated value); below that it enters discontinuous mode and the simple duty cycle formula no longer holds.

The capacitance formula ignores ESR. To keep 20 mV with 0.6 A of ripple the ESR must be below 0.020 ÷ 0.6 = **33 mΩ**. A ceramic capacitor near 5 mΩ adds only 3 mV, while an electrolytic of 50 mΩ adds 30 mV on its own, which is why ceramics dominate at 500 kHz. The input capacitor must carry about 2 × √(0.208 × 0.792) = **0.81 A RMS**.

With a non-synchronous design and a 0.5 V Schottky diode, the duty cycle rises to (5 + 0.5) ÷ (24 + 0.5) = **22.4%**. The on-time at 500 kHz is 417 ns, comfortably above the minimum on-time of typical controllers.

## Designing across an input range

The inductance needed rises with input voltage, so design at the highest input. For 5 V out, 0.6 A ripple and 500 kHz:

| Input voltage | Duty cycle | Inductance | On-time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 V | 62.5% | 6.2 µH | 1250 ns |
| 12 V | 41.7% | 9.7 µH | 833 ns |
| 18 V | 27.8% | 12.0 µH | 556 ns |
| 24 V | 20.8% | 13.2 µH | 417 ns |
| 36 V | 13.9% | 14.4 µH | 278 ns |

A converter fed from 8 to 36 V needs about 14.4 µH, not the 13.2 µH found at 24 V.

## Measuring ripple properly

Measure output ripple with the scope probe's ground spring touching the capacitor ground pad, not a long ground clip, and set 20 MHz bandwidth limiting. A long lead picks up switching spikes that look like ripple. Check the switch node for the expected duty cycle, and use a current probe on the inductor to confirm the ripple and that the waveform stays triangular with no sharp rise near the peak, which would mean saturation.

## Common mistakes

- **Designing at nominal input voltage.** At 36 V the same ripple needs 14.4 µH; a part sized at 24 V gives more ripple and higher peak current at maximum input.
- **Ignoring DC bias on ceramic capacitors.** X5R and X7R parts can lose half or more of their capacitance at rated voltage, so check the derating curve at 5 V.
- **Rating the inductor at the load current.** The saturation rating must exceed the peak current and ideally the controller's current limit.
- **Asking for too short an on-time.** Converting 24 V to 1 V at 2 MHz needs only 20.8 ns, below the 50 to 100 ns minimum of many controllers.
- **Probing with a long ground lead,** which turns switching edges into false ripple readings.
