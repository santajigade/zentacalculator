## How to use the wire resistance calculator

Choose the **material**, enter the **total conductor length**, the **cross-section** and the **operating temperature**. You get the resistance at 20 °C, at your temperature, and per kilometre.

## Wire resistance formula

<p class="formula">R = ρ × L ÷ A &nbsp;·&nbsp; R<sub>T</sub> = R₂₀ × [1 + α(T − 20)]</p>

The calculator uses α = 0.00393 /°C, the temperature coefficient of copper; aluminium (0.00403 /°C) is very close.

## Uses

- Voltage drop — see the [cable voltage drop calculator](/electrical/cable-voltage-drop/).
- 4–20 mA loop budgets — see the [loop resistance calculator](/engineering/loop-resistance/).
- RTD lead-wire error: 2-wire RTDs read high by the lead resistance (about 0.39 °C per 0.15 Ω for a Pt100).

## Worked example with the default values

The defaults describe **100 m of 1.5 mm² annealed copper** at 20 °C. Converting the area to SI, 1.5 mm² is 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ m², so:

- R₂₀ = 1.724 × 10⁻⁸ × 100 ÷ 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ = **1.1493 Ω**
- Resistance per km = 1.1493 ÷ 100 × 1,000 = **11.49 Ω/km**

If the same cable runs fully loaded at 70 °C, the resistance becomes 1.1493 × (1 + 0.00393 × 50) = **1.3752 Ω**, about 20% higher. That is why voltage drop and loss figures for power cables are usually stated at the maximum conductor temperature, not at 20 °C.

## Loop example: a field transmitter 500 m away

A 4 to 20 mA transmitter 500 m from the control room on a 1.5 mm² copper pair has 1,000 m of conductor in the loop. At 20 °C that is 11.49 Ω, and on a hot cable tray at 40 °C it is 12.40 Ω. At the full 20 mA the cable drops 0.248 V, which must fit in the supply budget along with the transmitter minimum voltage and the input resistor.

## Resistance of 2.5 mm² conductor at different temperatures

| Temperature | Copper | Aluminium |
|---|---|---|
| 0 °C | 6.354 Ω/km | 10.393 Ω/km |
| 20 °C | 6.896 Ω/km | 11.304 Ω/km |
| 40 °C | 7.438 Ω/km | 12.215 Ω/km |
| 70 °C | 8.251 Ω/km | 13.582 Ω/km |
| 90 °C | 8.793 Ω/km | 14.493 Ω/km |

To match a 16 mm² copper conductor with aluminium you need 16 × 2.826e-08 ÷ 1.724e-08 = **26.2 mm²**, so the next standard size, 35 mm², is the usual like-for-like swap once current rating is also checked.

## How conductor resistance is measured

Cable makers test conductor resistance with a DC micro-ohmmeter using a four-wire (Kelvin) connection, so the test lead resistance does not add to the reading. The sample is left to settle at room temperature, and the reading is corrected to 20 °C before comparing it with the IEC 60228 maximum. On site, a low-resistance ohmmeter across a looped pair at the far end gives the round-trip value; an ordinary multimeter is not accurate below about 1 Ω.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the one-way length.** A two-wire circuit has twice the route length of conductor; a three-phase balanced circuit uses the one-way length per phase.
- **Forgetting the mm² to m² conversion.** Leaving the area in mm² while ρ is in Ω·m gives a result a million times too large.
- **Applying the copper coefficient to nichrome.** The calculator uses 0.00393 /°C, which suits copper and aluminium; nichrome changes only about 0.0001 /°C, so its temperature correction here overstates the rise.
- **Ignoring AC effects on large cables.** Skin and proximity effect raise AC resistance noticeably above about 150 mm² at 50 Hz; datasheets list a separate AC value.
- **Comparing a calculated value with the standard limit.** IEC 60228 figures are maximums for stranded conductors, so a real cable can sit a few percent above the solid-wire calculation and still pass.
