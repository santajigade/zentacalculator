## How to use the CAGR calculator

Enter a **starting value**, **ending value** and the **number of years** to get the CAGR. You can also enter a growth rate to project a future value, or find how many years a target will take.

The currency symbol follows your region; CAGR works the same for revenue, users, population or any other quantity.

## CAGR formula

<p class="formula">CAGR = (ending ÷ starting)<sup>1/t</sup> − 1</p>

## Worked example

An investment grew from 10,000 to 18,000 over 5 years.

CAGR = (18,000 ÷ 10,000)<sup>1/5</sup> − 1 = 1.8<sup>0.2</sup> − 1 = **12.47% per year**

The total growth was 80%, but the yearly compound rate is 12.47% — not 80 ÷ 5 = 16%.

## Why CAGR beats simple averages

Suppose an investment rises 50% one year and falls 50% the next. The simple average return is 0%, yet 100 → 150 → 75 is a real loss. CAGR correctly shows −13.4% per year.

## CAGR examples

| Start → End | Period | CAGR |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 → 20,000 | 5 years | 14.87% |
| 10,000 → 20,000 | 10 years | 7.18% |
| 50,000 → 150,000 | 8 years | 14.72% |
| 100 → 80 | 3 years | -7.17% |
| 1,000 → 5,000 | 15 years | 11.33% |

A negative CAGR means the value shrank on average each year.

## Where CAGR is used

- **Investments**: comparing mutual funds, stocks or property bought at different times.
- **Business growth**: revenue, users or profit growth over several years in annual reports.
- **Economics**: GDP and population growth rates.
- **Planning**: projecting a value forward at a steady growth rate — press × on Ending value and enter a CAGR.

## CAGR vs absolute return vs XIRR

| Measure | What it tells you | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute return | Total % gain, ignoring time | Holdings under a year |
| CAGR | Steady yearly rate from start to end | One lump sum, held for years |
| XIRR | Yearly rate with many cash flows | SIPs, top-ups, withdrawals |

If you invested through a SIP, CAGR on the total invested understates or overstates the true return. Use XIRR instead.

## Common mistakes

- **Using months as years.** Enter 18 months as 1.5 years, or switch the unit to months.
- **Dividing the total return by years.** A 100% gain over 10 years is a 7.18% CAGR, not 10% a year.
- **Ignoring dividends.** For stocks, include reinvested dividends in the ending value to get total-return CAGR.
- **Counting the wrong number of years.** Five yearly figures from 2021 to 2025 span 4 years of growth, not 5.
- **Computing CAGR across a sign change.** If the start is zero or the values go from a loss to a profit, CAGR has no meaning; report the absolute change instead.

## Limits of CAGR

- It hides volatility: two investments with the same CAGR can have very different ups and downs.
- It ignores money added or withdrawn during the period.
- Past growth rates do not guarantee future results.

## Year-by-year path of the default example

At the default CAGR of 12.47%, the 10,000 grows like this. The final year lands exactly on 18,000, which is the definition of CAGR:

| Year | Value | Gain so far |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 10,000.00 | 0.00 |
| 1 | 11,247.46 | 1,247.46 |
| 2 | 12,650.54 | 2,650.54 |
| 3 | 14,228.64 | 4,228.64 |
| 4 | 16,003.61 | 6,003.61 |
| 5 | 18,000.00 | 8,000.00 |

The real investment almost certainly took a bumpier route; CAGR only fixes the two ends. At this rate money doubles in ln 2 ÷ ln(1.1247) = **5.90 years**, close to the rule of 72 estimate of 72 ÷ 12.47 = 5.77 years.

## Business example: revenue over four years

A company reports revenue of 120, 150, 138, 171 and 210 (in lakh or millions) for five consecutive years. That is 4 years of growth, so CAGR = (210 ÷ 120)<sup>1/4</sup> − 1 = **15.02%**. The four year-on-year changes are +25.0%, -8.0%, +23.9%, +22.8%, whose simple average is 15.93%, higher than the CAGR because the dip and recovery inflate the average.

## Years to multiply your money

Set the CAGR and leave the period blank to find how long a target takes. The table shows years needed to double, triple and five-fold a value:

| CAGR | 2× | 3× | 5× |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4% | 17.7 | 28.0 | 41.0 |
| 6% | 11.9 | 18.9 | 27.6 |
| 8% | 9.0 | 14.3 | 20.9 |
| 10% | 7.3 | 11.5 | 16.9 |
| 12% | 6.1 | 9.7 | 14.2 |
| 15% | 5.0 | 7.9 | 11.5 |
| 20% | 3.8 | 6.0 | 8.8 |

For example, growing 10,000 to 50,000 at 12% takes ln 5 ÷ ln 1.12 = **14.2 years**.

## Periods shorter than a year

CAGR annualises, so a short period is scaled up. A gain from 10,000 to 11,500 in 18 months is a 15% total return but a CAGR of 9.77%. Fund factsheets in India show absolute returns for periods under one year for exactly this reason: annualising a few weeks of movement gives misleading figures.
