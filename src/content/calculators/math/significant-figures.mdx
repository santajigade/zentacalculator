  - q: How do you round to significant figures?
    a: Find the first non-zero digit, count n digits from it, and look at the next digit — if it is 5 or more round up, otherwise round down, then replace the dropped digits in front of the decimal point with zeros. 1,234.5678 to 3 sig figs is 1,230; 0.0045678 to 3 sig figs is 0.00457.

    a: Three. Leading zeros only locate the decimal point and never count; the 4, the 5 and the trailing zero after the decimal point do. In scientific notation it is 4.50 × 10⁻³, which makes the count obvious.
  - q: How many significant figures should an answer have?
    a: For multiplication and division, keep as many significant figures as the least precise input. For addition and subtraction, keep as many decimal places as the input with the fewest decimal places. Round only the final answer, not intermediate steps.

## How to use the significant figures calculator

Enter the **number**, the number of **significant figures** you want and, separately, a number of **decimal places**. The calculator returns the number rounded to that many significant figures, its power-of-ten exponent and mantissa (so r = mantissa × 10<sup>exponent</sup> in scientific notation) and the number rounded to the chosen decimal places. Halves are rounded away from zero (2.5 → 3, −2.5 → −3). Trailing zeros that are significant (such as 2.50) cannot be shown in a number box, so write the result with the number of figures you asked for.

## Rounding formula

<p class="formula">e = ⌊log<sub>10</sub>|x|⌋ &nbsp;·&nbsp; r = sign(x) × ⌊|x| ÷ 10<sup>e − n + 1</sup> + ½⌋ × 10<sup>e − n + 1</sup></p>

<p class="formula">rounded to d decimal places = sign(x) × ⌊|x| × 10<sup>d</sup> + ½⌋ ÷ 10<sup>d</sup></p>

e is the position of the first significant digit (the exponent in scientific notation) and n the number of significant figures.

## Worked example

Round 1234.5678 to 3 significant figures and to 2 decimal places:

- First significant digit: e = ⌊log<sub>10</sub> 1234.5678⌋ = **3** (the number is 1.2345678 × 10<sup>3</sup>)
- Rounding unit = 10<sup>3 − 3 + 1</sup> = 10; 1234.5678 ÷ 10 = 123.45678 → 123
- Rounded to 3 sig figs = 123 × 10 = **1,230** = 1.23 × 10<sup>3</sup>
- Rounded to 2 decimal places = **1,234.57**

## Rules for counting significant figures

| Number | Significant figures | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 4,508 | 4 | Zeros between non-zero digits count |
| 0.00450 | 3 | Leading zeros never count; the trailing zero after the decimal point does |
| 45.00 | 4 | Trailing zeros after a decimal point count |
| 4500 | 2 (ambiguous) | Trailing zeros in a whole number are ambiguous — write 4.5 × 10³ or 4.500 × 10³ |
| 4500. | 4 | A decimal point after the number makes the zeros count |
| 6.022 × 10²³ | 4 | In scientific notation every digit of the mantissa counts |

## Practical notes

- **Measurements:** quote a result to the precision of the instrument — a ruler read to 1 mm gives 23.4 cm (3 s.f.), not 23.4000 cm. Combine uncertainties with the [measurement uncertainty calculator](/statistics/measurement-uncertainty/).
- **Rounding 5s:** this calculator rounds halves away from zero. Some scientific software and IEEE 754 arithmetic use "round half to even" (banker's rounding), where 2.5 → 2 and 3.5 → 4; this avoids a small upward bias when many values are rounded.
- **Binary floating point:** a decimal like 2.675 is stored as 2.67499999…, so any computer may round it to 2.67 instead of 2.68. If an exact half matters, add a tiny amount or use more figures.
- Use the [scientific notation calculator](/math/scientific-notation/) to convert the result to and from E-notation.

## Second example: a small negative number

Round −0.0045678 to 3 significant figures and to 2 decimal places:

1. e = ⌊log<sub>10</sub> 0.0045678⌋ = **−3**, because the first significant digit (4) is in the thousandths place
2. Rounding unit = 10<sup>−3 − 3 + 1</sup> = 10<sup>−5</sup>, and 0.0045678 ÷ 10<sup>−5</sup> = 456.78, which rounds to 457
3. Rounded to 3 sig figs = **−0.00457** = −4.57 × 10<sup>−3</sup>
4. Rounded to 2 decimal places = **0.00**

The sign is kept aside and put back at the end, so negative numbers round the same way as positive ones. The decimal place result shows why fixed decimals are a poor choice for very small values: every digit is lost.

## How much precision each figure claims

Rounding 1,234.5678 to more figures shrinks the rounding unit, and the largest error a rounded value can carry is half that unit:

| Sig figs | Rounded | Scientific notation | Rounding unit | Max rounding error | Relative error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 | 1 × 10³ | 1000 | ±500 | 50% |
| 2 | 1,200 | 1.2 × 10³ | 100 | ±50 | 4.2% |
| 3 | 1,230 | 1.23 × 10³ | 10 | ±5 | 0.41% |
| 4 | 1,235 | 1.235 × 10³ | 1 | ±0.5 | 0.040% |
| 5 | 1,234.6 | 1.2346 × 10³ | 0.1 | ±0.05 | 0.0040% |
| 6 | 1,234.57 | 1.23457 × 10³ | 0.01 | ±0.005 | 0.00040% |
| 7 | 1,234.568 | 1.234568 × 10³ | 0.001 | ±0.0005 | 0.000040% |

Each extra figure makes the claim ten times tighter. Three figures already pins this value to within about 0.4%, which is better than most everyday measurements.

## Significant figures in calculations

- **Multiplying:** a plate measured as 12.5 cm by 3.2 cm has an area of 12.5 × 3.2 = 40.0 cm² on a calculator. 3.2 has only 2 significant figures, so report **40 cm²** (better written 4.0 × 10¹ cm² to show both figures).
- **Adding:** 12.52 g + 3.1 g = 15.62 g, but 3.1 is known only to one decimal place, so report **15.6 g**.
- **Exact factors:** the diameter of a circle with radius 4.37 cm is 2 × 4.37 = **8.74 cm**. The 2 is exact and does not limit the answer to one figure.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting leading zeros.** 0.0045678 has five significant figures, not seven; zeros in front of the first non-zero digit only place the decimal point.
- **Rounding in stages.** 1.2449 to 2 significant figures is 1.2. Rounding first to 1.245 and then to 1.25 and 1.3 gives a wrong answer; always round the original value once.
- **Rounding intermediate results.** Keep all digits through a multi step calculation and round only the final answer, or small errors build up.
- **Dropping significant trailing zeros.** A reading of 2.50 V has three figures; writing 2.5 V throws away the information that the last digit was measured.
- **Using the wrong rule for sums.** Addition and subtraction follow decimal places, not significant figures, so 100.2 + 0.0345 is 100.2, not 100.2345 or 100.
- **Entering zero.** Zero has no first significant digit (log<sub>10</sub> 0 is undefined), so the calculator cannot round it; 0 to any number of figures is simply 0.
