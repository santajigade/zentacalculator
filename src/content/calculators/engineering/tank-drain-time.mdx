## How to use the tank drain time calculator

Enter the **inside diameter** of the vertical tank, the **diameter of the outlet** and its type (which sets the **discharge coefficient**), and the **starting and final levels** measured above the outlet. The calculator gives the **drain time**, the outflow at the start and the end, the volume drained and the average flow. Enter a **fill flow rate** to see how long it takes to fill the same volume back up with the outlet closed. To find the outlet size for a required drain time, enter the time and press × on the outlet diameter.

## Tank drain time formula (Torricelli's law)

<p class="formula">Q = C<sub>d</sub> a √(2 g h) &nbsp;·&nbsp; t = (A ÷ C<sub>d</sub> a) × √(2 ÷ g) × (√h<sub>1</sub> − √h<sub>2</sub>)</p>

<p class="formula">Fill time = A (h<sub>1</sub> − h<sub>2</sub>) ÷ Q<sub>fill</sub></p>

A = π D² ÷ 4 is the tank cross-section, a = π d² ÷ 4 the outlet area and g = 9.80665 m/s². The formula assumes a constant cross-section, a tank open to the atmosphere (or vented) and an outlet discharging freely to atmosphere.

## Worked example

A 2 m diameter tank with a 50 mm sharp-edged bottom outlet (C<sub>d</sub> = 0.61) drains from 3 m to 0.2 m:

- Tank area A = π × 2² ÷ 4 = 3.1416 m²; outlet area a = π × 0.05² ÷ 4 = 19.635 cm²
- Drain time = (3.1416 ÷ (0.61 × 0.0019635)) × √(2 ÷ 9.80665) × (√3 − √0.2) = 1,522 s = **25.37 min**
- Outflow at the start = 0.61 × 0.0019635 × √(2 × 9.80665 × 3) × 3600 = **33.07 m³/h**, falling to **8.54 m³/h** at 0.2 m
- Volume drained = 3.1416 × (3 − 0.2) = **8,796 L**, an average of **20.81 m³/h**
- Filling it back at 10 m³/h takes 8.7965 ÷ 10 = **52.78 min**

## Practical notes

- **Drain lines and valves:** if the tank empties through a pipe, the friction and fittings reduce the flow. Use the [orifice flow calculator](/engineering/orifice-flow/) or a pipe pressure-drop calculation for the real outflow; a pipe that drops below the tank adds head and can speed up draining.
- **Closed tanks** need a vent sized for the outflow, otherwise the vacuum above the liquid slows or stops draining.
- **Cone or dished bottoms** hold extra liquid below the straight side; for a [cone bottom tank](/engineering/cone-bottom-tank-volume/) the last part drains faster than this formula predicts because the area shrinks.
- Vortexing near the end of draining can draw air into the outlet and reduce the flow further.

## Outlet type and drain time

The discharge coefficient changes the time in direct proportion. For the default 2 m tank and 50 mm outlet:

| Outlet | C<sub>d</sub> | 3 m to 0.2 m | 3 m to empty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Re-entrant tube | 0.50 | 30.9 min | 41.7 min |
| Sharp-edged hole | 0.61 | 25.4 min | 34.2 min |
| Short nozzle | 0.82 | 18.9 min | 25.4 min |
| Bell-mouth | 0.97 | 16.0 min | 21.5 min |

Swapping a sharp-edged hole for a short nozzle of the same bore cuts the drain time from 25.4 to 18.9 minutes without any change to the tank.

## Sizing an outlet for a target time

Drain time is inversely proportional to the outlet area, so the bore scales with the square root of the time ratio. To bring the default tank from 3 m to 0.2 m in 15 minutes instead of 25.37, the outlet must be 50 × √(25.37 ÷ 15) = **65.0 mm**, so a standard DN65 nozzle does the job.

## Checking the outlet on site

Timing a real drain is a quick check of the outlet. If the default tank takes 30 minutes instead of the calculated 25.37, the effective coefficient is 0.61 × 25.37 ÷ 30 = **0.516**. A value well below the table suggests a partly closed valve, a strainer, debris in the nozzle or a drain line long enough for friction to control the flow. Level can be read from a sight glass, a dip tape or the level transmitter trend, using two readings well apart so the timing error stays small.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming the flow stays at its starting value.** Dividing the volume by the initial outflow gives 8.80 m³ ÷ 33.07 m³/h × 60 = 16.0 min, far short of the real 25.4 min.
- **Measuring levels from the tank floor.** The head is measured from the outlet centre; for a side nozzle 300 mm above the floor, the liquid below the nozzle never drains and the levels entered must be reduced by 0.3 m.
- **Using C<sub>d</sub> 0.61 for a drain line.** Once the liquid passes through a pipe, valve and bends, friction controls the flow and the effective coefficient may be 0.2 to 0.4.
- **Applying water figures to viscous liquids.** Oils, syrups and slurries have lower coefficients, especially at low head near the end of draining.
- **Leaving an inflow running.** Any feed still entering the tank extends the drain time, and if it exceeds the outflow at the final head, the level never reaches it.
