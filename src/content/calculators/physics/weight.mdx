## How to use the weight calculator

Choose a **location**, then enter a **mass**. You get the weight in newtons (or lbf) and what an Earth-calibrated bathroom scale would show there.

## Weight formula

<p class="formula">W = m × g</p>

## Worked example (70 kg person)

With the default 70 kg mass on Earth:

1. W = m × g = 70 × 9.80665 = **686.47 N**, which is 154.3 lbf
2. Equivalent scale reading = W ÷ 9.80665 = **70 kg**, as expected on Earth

Choose the Moon instead and the weight drops to 70 × 1.62 = **113.4 N**. The same Earth calibrated scale divides by 9.80665 and shows **11.56 kg**, although the person still has 70 kg of mass.

## Mass vs weight

| | Mass | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Amount of matter | Force of gravity on that matter |
| Unit | kilogram (kg) | newton (N) |
| Changes with location? | No | Yes |
| Measured by | Balance | Spring scale |

Bathroom scales measure force but display it as kilograms by assuming Earth's gravity.

## Weightlessness

Astronauts on the International Space Station are not beyond gravity — at about 400 km up, gravity is still around 90% of Earth's. They feel weightless because they are in continuous free fall around the planet.

## Gravity on Earth varies slightly

Standard gravity is 9.80665 m/s², but the real value ranges from about 9.78 at the equator to 9.83 at the poles — a difference of about 0.5% in your weight.

## Weight at altitude

Gravity falls with the square of the distance from Earth's centre, g = g₀ × (R ÷ (R + h))², with R = 6,371 km. For the same 70 kg person:

| Location | Height | g (m/s²) | Weight (N) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sea level | 0 m | 9.807 | 686.5 |
| Everest summit | 8,849 m | 9.779 | 684.6 |
| Airliner cruise | 11,000 m | 9.773 | 684.1 |
| Edge of space (100 km) | 100 km | 9.506 | 665.4 |
| ISS orbit | 400 km | 8.682 | 607.8 |
| Geostationary orbit | 35,786 km | 0.224 | 15.7 |

Even at geostationary height the weight is not zero; satellites stay up because they are falling around the Earth, not because gravity has vanished.

## How weight is measured in practice

A spring scale or electronic scale measures force. Most electronic platforms use a strain gauge [load cell](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/load-cell-working-principle/) whose output is proportional to the force on it, and the display divides by an assumed g to show kilograms. That is why commercial scales are calibrated with test masses at their place of use. A 70 kg person weighs 684.6 N at the equator (g ≈ 9.78) and 688.2 N near the poles (g ≈ 9.832). A scale set up with standard gravity would read 69.81 kg at the equator. A beam balance compares two masses directly, so it reads the same anywhere, even on the Moon.

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting weight in kilograms.** In physics, weight is a force in newtons; 70 kg is the mass, 686 N is the weight.
- **Confusing lb and lbf.** A 70 kg mass is 154.3 lb of mass, and on Earth it weighs 154.3 lbf; the two numbers only match under standard gravity.
- **Multiplying a scale reading by g twice.** The scale reading is already a mass equivalent; multiply it by 9.80665 once to get newtons.
- **Using Earth's g on another planet.** The Moon's 1.62 m/s² gives a weight about one sixth of Earth's; picking the right location matters more than the decimals.
- **Expecting zero weight in orbit.** At 400 km, gravity is still about 89% of the surface value.
