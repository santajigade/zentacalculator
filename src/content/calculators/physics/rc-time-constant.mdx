## How to use the RC time constant calculator

Enter the **resistance** and **capacitance** to get the time constant, approximate full-charge time and filter cut-off frequency. You can also solve for the capacitor needed for a target time.

## RC time constant formulas

<p class="formula">τ = R C   t(≈99%) = 5τ   f<sub>c</sub> = 1 ÷ (2π R C)</p>

## Worked example

10 kΩ and 100 µF:

- τ = 10,000 × 0.0001 = **1 s**
- Full charge ≈ **5 s**
- Cut-off frequency = **0.159 Hz**

## Charge and discharge levels

| Time | Charging (% of supply) | Discharging (% remaining) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5τ | 39.3% | 60.7% |
| 1τ | 63.2% | 36.8% |
| 2τ | 86.5% | 13.5% |
| 3τ | 95.0% | 5.0% |
| 4τ | 98.2% | 1.8% |
| 5τ | 99.3% | 0.7% |

## Voltage at any time

- Charging: V(t) = V<sub>s</sub> × (1 − e<sup>−t/τ</sup>)
- Discharging: V(t) = V<sub>0</sub> × e<sup>−t/τ</sup>

To find the time to reach a given voltage while charging: t = −τ × ln(1 − V ÷ V<sub>s</sub>).

## Applications

- **Timers and delays**: the classic 555 timer uses RC charging to set its period.
- **Filters**: RC low-pass filters remove high-frequency noise from sensor signals; the cut-off frequency is 1 ÷ (2πRC).
- **Debouncing switches**: a small RC network smooths mechanical switch bounce.
- **Power supply smoothing**: capacitors hold up the voltage between rectifier peaks.

## Safety with large capacitors

Large capacitors in power supplies can stay charged after power is removed. Always discharge them through a suitable resistor before touching, and allow at least 5τ.

## Time constants for common part values

Kilohms multiplied by microfarads give milliseconds, which makes quick estimates easy:

| Resistance | 1 nF | 100 nF | 1 µF | 100 µF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 kΩ | 1 µs | 100 µs | 1 ms | 100 ms |
| 10 kΩ | 10 µs | 1 ms | 10 ms | 1 s |
| 100 kΩ | 100 µs | 10 ms | 100 ms | 10 s |
| 1 MΩ | 1 ms | 100 ms | 1 s | 100 s |

## Voltages for the default circuit on a 12 V supply

With 10 kΩ and 100 µF (τ = 1 s, cut-off 0.1592 Hz) connected to a 12 V source:

| Time after switching | Charging voltage | Discharging voltage (from 12 V) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 s | 4.72 V | 7.28 V |
| 1 s | 7.59 V | 4.41 V |
| 2 s | 10.38 V | 1.62 V |
| 3 s | 11.40 V | 0.60 V |
| 5 s | 11.92 V | 0.08 V |

To reach 9 V while charging takes t = −1 s × ln(1 − 9 ÷ 12) = **1.386 s**. If the 12 V source itself has 1 kΩ of internal resistance, the real time constant is (10 kΩ + 1 kΩ) × 100 µF = 1.1 s, so every time in the table stretches by 10%.

## Measuring τ in practice

Drive the circuit with a slow square wave and watch the capacitor on an oscilloscope. Measure the time from the step to 63.2% of the final value, or for a discharge the time to fall to 36.8%. A quicker way is to measure the 10% to 90% rise time, which equals 2.197τ for a single RC stage. A general purpose electrolytic with ±20% tolerance means the default network can measure anywhere from 0.8 s to 1.2 s, so check the real part before trimming resistor values.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing unit prefixes.** 10 kΩ × 100 µF is 1 s, but 10 kΩ × 100 nF is 1 ms; a slip of a thousand is the most common error.
- **Treating 5τ as exactly full.** At 5τ the capacitor is at 99.3%; precision sample and hold circuits wait 7τ to 9τ for 12 bit accuracy.
- **Forgetting the source and load resistance.** Driver output resistance adds to R, and a load across the capacitor forms a divider that lowers the final voltage and shortens τ.
- **Assuming the cut-off frequency removes the signal.** A 1 kΩ and 100 nF low pass filter cuts off at 1592 Hz but only attenuates by 3 dB there and by 20 dB per decade above.
- **Ignoring leakage in large electrolytics.** With megohm resistors, leakage current can stop the capacitor ever reaching the expected voltage.
