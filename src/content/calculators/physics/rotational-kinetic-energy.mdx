## How to use the rotational kinetic energy calculator

Choose the **shape**, then enter the **mass**, the **radius** and the **speed in rpm**. You get the moment of inertia, the angular velocity and the stored rotational kinetic energy. For other shapes, clear the mass and enter a known moment of inertia directly.

## Rotational kinetic energy formula

<p class="formula">E = ½ I ω² &nbsp;·&nbsp; I = k m r² &nbsp;·&nbsp; ω = 2π N ÷ 60</p>

## Worked example

A 20 kg solid steel disc of radius 250 mm spinning at 1,000 rpm:

- I = ½ × 20 × 0.25² = **0.6250 kg·m²**
- ω = 2π × 1000 ÷ 60 = **104.72 rad/s**
- E = ½ × 0.625 × 104.72² = **3,427 J** (0.952 Wh)

## Moments of inertia of common shapes

| Shape (about central axis) | I |
|---|---|
| Solid disc or cylinder | ½ m r² |
| Thin ring or hoop | m r² |
| Thick ring, radii r₁ and r₂ | ½ m (r₁² + r₂²) |
| Solid sphere | ⅖ m r² |
| Thin hollow sphere | ⅔ m r² |
| Rod about its centre, length L | m L² ÷ 12 |
| Rod about one end | m L² ÷ 3 |

## Energy vs speed for the example disc

| Speed | Energy |
|---|---|
| 500 rpm | 857 J |
| 1,000 rpm | 3,427 J |
| 1,500 rpm | 7,711 J |
| 3,000 rpm | 30,843 J |
| 6,000 rpm | 123,370 J |

The rim of this disc moves at ω r = 104.72 × 0.25 = **26.18 m/s**. If the same disc rolled along the floor at that speed, its straight line energy ½ m v² would be 6,854 J on top of the 3,427 J of spin.

## Same mass, different shape

At 1,000 rpm with 20 kg and a 250 mm radius:

| Shape | Moment of inertia | Stored energy |
|---|---|---|
| Solid disc | 0.6250 kg·m² | 3,427 J |
| Thin ring | 1.2500 kg·m² | 6,854 J |
| Solid sphere | 0.5000 kg·m² | 2,742 J |
| Thin hollow sphere | 0.8333 kg·m² | 4,569 J |

Moving the mass from a solid disc to a thin rim doubles the energy (6,854 J against 3,427 J) without adding a kilogram.

## Using the energy in practice

**Coast down.** If the example disc slows from 1,000 to 600 rpm, it releases 2,193 J. Delivered over 2 s that is an average of 1,097 W, which is how a press flywheel supplies a short punching stroke from a small motor.

**Braking.** A constant friction torque of 5 N·m gives an angular deceleration of 5 ÷ 0.625 = 8 rad/s², so the disc stops in 13.1 s after about 109 revolutions. All 3,427 J end up as heat in the brake or bearings.

**Measuring the moment of inertia.** For irregular parts, hang the part on a thin wire (torsion pendulum) or let a known falling mass spin it up and time the acceleration, then enter I directly instead of shape, mass and radius.

## Common mistakes

- **Using rpm instead of rad/s.** E = ½ I ω² needs ω in rad/s; putting 1,000 in directly gives an answer about 91 times too large.
- **Diameter for radius.** Entering 500 mm for a 500 mm diameter disc makes I and E four times too large.
- **Treating a spoked or webbed flywheel as a solid disc.** Most of its mass sits in the rim, so it is closer to a thick ring.
- **Ignoring the shaft, hub and coupling.** On small rotors they can add a noticeable share of the total inertia.
- **Forgetting the stress limit.** Stored energy rises with speed squared, but so does rim stress; check the rated maximum speed before raising rpm to store more energy.
