    A Pt100 is a platinum resistance thermometer with 100 Ω at 0 °C, and its resistance rises in a known way as it gets hotter. This Pt100 resistance calculator converts temperature to ohms and back for Pt100, Pt500 and Pt1000 sensors using the Callendar Van Dusen equation.

    Instrument technicians use it to check a sensor with a multimeter, as in how to test an RTD, and to simulate a temperature with a decade box. The Pt100 vs Pt1000 differences matter when choosing one.

    How to use the pt100 / pt1000 resistance calculator

    Choose the sensor type, then type either a temperature to get the expected resistance, or a resistance reading from your multimeter to get the temperature. Temperatures can be entered in °C, °F or K.

    For more background, read RTD Self-Heating Error.

    The formula (IEC 60751)

    For 0 °C ≤ T ≤ 850 °C, platinum RTDs follow the Callendar–Van Dusen equation:

    R = R₀ × (1 + A·T + B·T²)

    • R₀ — resistance at 0 °C (100 Ω for Pt100)
    • A = 3.9083 × 10⁻³ °C⁻¹
    • B = −5.775 × 10⁻⁷ °C⁻²
    • T — temperature in °C

    Worked example

    A Pt100 at 100 °C:

    R = 100 × (1 + 0.39083 − 0.005775) = 138.51 Ω

    Going the other way, a reading of 138.50 Ω means about 99.99 °C.

    A ±(0.15 + 0.002 abs(t)) ±0.15 °C ±0.35 °C

    C ±(0.6 + 0.01 abs(t)) ±0.60 °C ±1.60 °C

    2-, 3- and 4-wire connections

    Connection Lead-resistance error Typical use
    2-wire Full lead resistance added Short runs, low accuracy
    3-wire Mostly cancelled if leads are equal Most industrial installations
    4-wire Eliminated Laboratory and high-accuracy work

    With 2-wire, 10 m of 0.5 mm² copper (about 0.36 Ω each way, 0.72 Ω total) adds roughly 1.8 °C of error, since a Pt100 changes about 0.385 Ω/°C.

    Below 0 °C

    For temperatures below 0 °C, IEC 60751 uses the full Callendar–Van Dusen equation with an extra C term:

    R = R₀ × [1 + A·t + B·t² + C·(t − 100)·t³]

    where C = −4.183 × 10−12. The calculator’s formula is exact from 0 to 850 °C and very close down to about −50 °C.

    Self-heating

    The measuring current heats the sensor slightly. Transmitters typically use 0.5–1 mA to keep self-heating below a few hundredths of a degree.

    Field tips

    • Check for lead resistance. On a 2-wire Pt100, every 1 Ω of cable resistance reads as about 2.6 °C too high.
    • Self-heating. Too much measuring current warms the sensor. Typical transmitters use 1 mA or less.
    • Tolerance classes. Class A is ±(0.15 + 0.002·|T|) °C; Class B is ±(0.3 + 0.005·|T|) °C.

    Worked example: resistance reading to temperature

    The default case is a Pt100 at 100 °C, which the calculator gives as 138.51 Ω (a Pt1000 at the same temperature reads 1385.1 Ω). Going the other way needs the quadratic solved for t:

    t = [−A + √(A² − 4B(1 − R/R₀))] ÷ (2B)

    Suppose a 4-wire meter reads 175.00 Ω across a Pt100. Then t = 197.67 °C. If the same sensor is wired 2-wire through cable with 1.4 Ω of loop resistance, the instrument sees the extra ohms as heat; correcting for them gives 193.87 °C, so the uncorrected reading is about 3.8 °C high.

    Sensitivity and tolerance in ohms

    The slope dR/dt = R₀(A + 2Bt) falls slowly with temperature because B is negative, so one ohm means a little more temperature at the hot end. Class A tolerance converted to ohms is what you compare with a resistance check on the bench.

    Temperature Pt100 resistance Pt100 slope Pt1000 slope Class A tolerance
    0 °C 100.00 Ω 0.3908 Ω/°C 3.908 Ω/°C ±0.15 °C = ±0.059 Ω
    100 °C 138.51 Ω 0.3793 Ω/°C 3.793 Ω/°C ±0.35 °C = ±0.133 Ω

    300 °C 212.05 Ω 0.3562 Ω/°C 3.562 Ω/°C ±0.75 °C = ±0.267 Ω

    At 0 °C a Pt100 changes 0.3908 Ω per degree; at 400 °C only 0.3446 Ω, about 12% less.

    How the resistance is measured in practice

    A transmitter or PLC RTD card pushes a small constant current through the element and reads the voltage across it. At 1 mA a Pt100 at 100 °C develops only 138.5 mV, so the input must resolve microvolts, and thermocouple effects at dissimilar terminals can matter on low-level signals. For a field check, disconnect the sensor, measure the element with a 4-wire ohmmeter or a calibrator, and compare with the table. To test the transmitter instead, connect a decade box or RTD simulator in place of the sensor and dial in the resistance for each test point; a Pt100 calibration guide covers the full procedure.

    Common mistakes

    • Setting a Pt1000 input and connecting a Pt100. The transmitter reads about one tenth of the expected resistance and shows a temperature near −200 °C or an underrange fault.
    • Using the 0.385 Ω/°C average for precise work. It is the mean slope from 0 to 100 °C; at 400 °C the real slope is 0.345 Ω/°C, so linear estimates drift by several degrees at high temperature.
    • Measuring with a cheap 2-wire multimeter. The probe leads and contact resistance add a few tenths of an ohm, which is already close to 1 °C on a Pt100.
    • Forgetting the alpha of the sensor. Some older or US sensors use α = 0.003916 or 0.003920 instead of the IEC 0.00385, and their resistance at 100 °C differs by about 0.6 Ω.
    • Checking a hot sensor while it is still in the process. Remove it or read it at a known reference such as an ice bath; otherwise you cannot tell sensor drift from a real temperature change.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the resistance of a Pt100 at 100 °C?

    About 138.51 Ω. A Pt100 is 100 Ω at 0 °C and rises by roughly 0.385 Ω per °C.

    What is the difference between Pt100 and Pt1000?

    A Pt1000 has ten times the resistance (1000 Ω at 0 °C). It is less affected by lead-wire resistance, so it suits 2-wire connections and battery-powered devices.

    Does this work below 0 °C?

    This calculator uses the IEC 60751 equation for 0 to 850 °C. Below 0 °C an extra term is needed; support for negative temperatures will be added.

    What do 2-wire, 3-wire and 4-wire mean?

    They are ways of connecting the RTD. 3-wire and 4-wire connections cancel the resistance of the cable, which otherwise reads as extra temperature.

    What is the resistance of a Pt100 at 0 °C?

    Exactly 100.00 Ω. A Pt1000 is 1,000 Ω at 0 °C.

    How much does a Pt100 change per degree?

    About 0.385 Ω per °C near room temperature.

    How do I convert a Pt100 resistance reading to temperature?

    Solve the IEC 60751 quadratic: t = [−A + √(A² − 4B(1 − R/100))] ÷ 2B, with A = 3.9083 × 10⁻³ and B = −5.775 × 10⁻⁷. A reading of 175.00 Ω gives 197.67 °C. Enter the resistance and press × on Temperature to let the calculator do it.

    What voltage does a Pt100 give at 1 mA?

    V = I × R, so 138.5 mV at 100 °C and 100 mV at 0 °C. The change is only about 0.39 mV per °C, which is why RTD inputs need high resolution and good shielding.
