## How to use the inductor energy calculator

Enter the **inductance** and the **current** to get the stored energy. Add a **switch-off time** to estimate the average voltage induced if the current falls to zero in that time without suppression, and the average power the stored energy is released at. To find the current that stores a given energy, enter the energy and press × on the current.

For more background, read [Lenz’s Law and Electromagnetic Induction](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/lenzs-law-electromagnetic-induction/).

## Inductor energy formulas

<p class="formula">E = ½ × L × I² &nbsp;·&nbsp; V = L × di/dt ≈ L × I ÷ Δt &nbsp;·&nbsp; P<sub>avg</sub> = E ÷ Δt</p>

The voltage is the average over a linear fall in current; an abrupt interruption is faster still, so the real spike is limited only by the coil's stray capacitance or by arcing across the switch.

## Worked example

A 10 mH choke carrying 2 A is switched off in 1 ms:

- Stored energy E = ½ × 0.01 × 2² = **0.02 J** (20 mJ)
- Induced voltage V = 0.01 × 2 ÷ 0.001 = **20 V**
- Average power released = 0.02 ÷ 0.001 = **20 W**

Interrupting the same current in 10 µs would induce about 2,000 V — why a suppression diode is essential.

## Practical notes

- **Saturation and core choice:** ferrite cores saturate at about 0.3–0.5 T, iron powder and silicon steel at higher flux. Gapped cores store most of their energy in the air gap, which is why SMPS inductors and flyback transformers use them.
- **Energy per cycle:** a converter that stores E each switching period at frequency f transfers up to P = E × f — 20 µJ at 100 kHz is 2 W.
- **Decay time:** with a flyback diode the current dies away with the coil's L/R time constant; see the [RL time constant calculator](/electronics/rl-time-constant/).
- To compare with energy stored in an electric field, use the [capacitor energy calculator](/electronics/capacitor-energy/).

## Sizing a clamp for the default coil

The default 10 mH coil at 2 A holds 20 mJ, and every bit of it must go somewhere when the switch opens. Three common ways to absorb it give very different results:

1. **Plain flyback diode.** The coil voltage is held at about 0.7 V and the current decays through the coil's own resistance. If the coil runs from 12 V at 2 A its resistance is 6 Ω, so τ = L ÷ R = 1.67 ms and the current takes about 5τ = **8.3 ms** to die away. Most of the 20 mJ heats the winding.
2. **Diode plus 24 V Zener.** The coil voltage is clamped at 24 V, so the current falls linearly to zero in L × I ÷ V = **0.833 ms**. The Zener absorbs the full 20 mJ at an average of 24 V × 1 A = 24 W during that time.
3. **No suppression.** The voltage rises until something breaks down: the transistor, the contacts or the insulation.

If the coil is switched 50 times a second, the clamp must dissipate 20 mJ × 50 = **1.0 W** continuously, which rules out a small signal Zener.

The energy is the same in every row; only the rate changes. Faster interruption means a higher voltage and a shorter, more intense pulse.

## Measuring inductance under current

An LCR meter measures inductance at a small test signal, which tells you nothing about saturation. To check a choke at its working current, apply a known DC voltage through a switch and capture the current on a scope with a current probe: di/dt = V ÷ L. At 12 V the default 10 mH coil ramps at 1,200 A/s and should reach 2 A in about 1.67 ms (before resistance flattens the curve). If the slope steepens as the current rises, the core is saturating and the stored energy is less than ½LI² suggests.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering henries as millihenries.** 10 H instead of 10 mH gives 20 J, a thousand times too much.
- **Forgetting that energy goes with I².** Running the coil at 1.4 times the current roughly doubles the energy the clamp must take.
- **Rating the clamp only for peak voltage.** The Zener or TVS must also absorb the energy per pulse and the average power at the switching rate.
- **Using a plain diode on a fast-release relay or valve.** The slow decay delays drop-out by several milliseconds; a Zener clamp releases far faster.
- **Using small-signal inductance near saturation.** The real inductance at full current can be much lower than the datasheet value.
