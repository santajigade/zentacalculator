  - q: How do you calculate autotransformer current?
    a: I_high = S ÷ V_high and I_low = S ÷ V_low, as for any transformer. The common part of the winding carries only the difference, I_low − I_high.
  - q: How much power is transformed in an autotransformer?
    a: Only S × (1 − V_low ÷ V_high). The rest, S × V_low ÷ V_high, is conducted straight through the shared winding. A 240/200 V, 10 kVA unit transforms just 1.67 kVA.
  - q: How much copper does an autotransformer save?
    a: Copper needed = (1 − K) × that of a two-winding transformer, with K = V_low ÷ V_high, so the saving is K. The closer the two voltages, the bigger the saving.
  - q: What is the main disadvantage of an autotransformer?
    a: There is no electrical isolation between primary and secondary, and if the common winding opens, the full input voltage can appear at the output. It also has lower impedance, so fault currents are higher.

## How to use the autotransformer calculator

Enter the **load rating** in kVA and the **high-** and **low-voltage** sides of the autotransformer (it works the same for step-up and step-down). The calculator returns the current on each side, the current in the common section of the winding, how much of the power is transformed magnetically versus conducted directly, and the copper saved compared with a two-winding transformer of the same rating.

## Autotransformer formulas

<p class="formula">I<sub>H</sub> = S ÷ V<sub>H</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; I<sub>L</sub> = S ÷ V<sub>L</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; I<sub>common</sub> = I<sub>L</sub> − I<sub>H</sub></p>

<p class="formula">S<sub>transformed</sub> = S (1 − K) &nbsp;·&nbsp; S<sub>conducted</sub> = S K &nbsp;·&nbsp; K = V<sub>L</sub> ÷ V<sub>H</sub></p>

## Example

A 10 kVA, 240 V to 200 V step-down autotransformer:

- I<sub>H</sub> = 10,000 ÷ 240 = **41.67 A**, I<sub>L</sub> = 10,000 ÷ 200 = **50.00 A**
- Common winding current = 50 − 41.67 = **8.33 A**
- Transformed = 10 × (1 − 200/240) = **1.667 kVA**; conducted = **8.333 kVA**
- Copper saved ≈ **83.3%** of a 10 kVA two-winding transformer

## Where autotransformers are used

- **Motor starting**: autotransformer starters apply 50–80% voltage taps, cutting line current to K² of the direct-on-line value.
- **Grid interconnection**: large 400/220 kV and 220/132 kV interbus transformers, where the ratio is close to 1.
- **Variacs**: variable autotransformers for test benches.
- Not suitable where isolation is required, or for large ratios such as 11 kV/415 V.

## Checking the two winding sections

An autotransformer is really two windings in series. In the default 10 kVA, 240/200 V unit:

- The **series section** spans 40 V and carries the high-side current: 41.67 A × 40 V = **1.667 kVA**
- The **common section** spans 200 V and carries the difference current: 8.33 A × 200 V = **1.667 kVA**

Both equal the transformed power of 1.667 kVA, which is why the core and copper are sized for about a sixth of the load. The common section can use wire rated for only 8.33 A, while the series section must carry 41.67 A.

## Autotransformer motor starter taps

On a starter, the motor sees the tap voltage, its current falls in proportion and the line current falls by the square, as does torque:

| Tap | Motor current | Line current | Starting torque |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 50% of DOL | 25.0% of DOL | 25.0% of DOL |
| 65% | 65% of DOL | 42.3% of DOL | 42.3% of DOL |
| 80% | 80% of DOL | 64.0% of DOL | 64.0% of DOL |

The 65% tap is the usual compromise when the driven load needs at least 40% of full-voltage torque to break away.

## Fault level

Because only the series section separates the two systems, the per-unit impedance on the throughput rating is (1 − K) times that of the same core connected as a two-winding transformer. A 4% impedance becomes about **0.67%**, so downstream fault current can be six times higher. Breakers and fuses on the low side must be rated for it.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying on transformed kVA.** The nameplate rating is the throughput load, 10 kVA here, not the 1.667 kVA transformed.
- **Expecting isolation.** The output is bonded to the input, so it cannot be used to create a separately derived system or a safe low-voltage supply.
- **Using a large ratio.** At 400/100 V the copper saving is only 25% and an open common winding would put 400 V on 100 V equipment.
- **Leaving the neutral unconnected in a three-phase star unit.** The star point must be tied to the supply neutral or the phase voltages float.
