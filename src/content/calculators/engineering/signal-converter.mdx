## How to use the signal converter calculator

Enter a value in **any one** field — a 4–20 mA current, a percent of range or one of the voltage signals — and the calculator fills in the equivalent value for every other standard. All conversions are linear by percent of range.

## Signal conversion formulas

<p class="formula">% = (I<sub>4–20</sub> − 4 mA) ÷ 16 mA</p>

<p class="formula">I<sub>0–20</sub> = 20 mA × % &nbsp;·&nbsp; V<sub>0–10</sub> = 10 V × % &nbsp;·&nbsp; V<sub>1–5</sub> = 1 V + 4 V × % &nbsp;·&nbsp; V<sub>0–5</sub> = 5 V × %</p>

## Example

12 mA on a 4–20 mA loop:

- Percent = (12 − 4) ÷ 16 = **50%**
- 0–20 mA = **10 mA**, 0–10 V = **5 V**, 1–5 V = **3 V**, 0–5 V = **2.5 V**

## Worked example: from a 1 to 5 V reading

A controller input reads **3.8 V** across its 250 Ω resistor. Enter it in the 1 to 5 V field:

- Percent of range = (3.8 − 1) ÷ 4 = **70%**
- 4 to 20 mA loop current = 4 + 16 × 0.70 = **15.2 mA**
- 0 to 10 V equivalent = **7.0 V**, 0 to 20 mA = **14.0 mA**, 0 to 5 V = **3.5 V**

Multiply the percent by the transmitter range to get engineering units: on a 0 to 10 bar transmitter 70% is 7.0 bar.

## NAMUR NE 43 signal levels

Most modern transmitters follow NAMUR NE 43, which keeps the measurement between 3.8 and 20.5 mA and reserves the values beyond for fault signalling:

| Signal | Current | Percent of range | 1 to 5 V equivalent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Failure (low) | 3.6 mA | −2.50% | 0.900 V |
| Under-range limit | 3.8 mA | −1.25% | 0.950 V |
| 0% (live zero) | 4.0 mA | 0.00% | 1.000 V |
| 100% | 20.0 mA | 100.00% | 5.000 V |
| Over-range limit | 20.5 mA | 103.12% | 5.125 V |
| Failure (high) | 21.0 mA | 106.25% | 5.250 V |

A receiver that clips at 0% and 100% hides these states, so set its alarm limits to catch them.

## Current to voltage with a resistor

A current loop becomes a voltage across a precision resistor, V = I × R:

| Resistor | At 4 mA | At 12 mA | At 20 mA |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 Ω | 0.20 V | 0.60 V | 1.00 V |
| 100 Ω | 0.40 V | 1.20 V | 2.00 V |
| 250 Ω | 1.00 V | 3.00 V | 5.00 V |
| 500 Ω | 2.00 V | 6.00 V | 10.00 V |

The resistor's tolerance goes straight into the reading, so use 0.1% resistors. Each one also adds to the loop load: with a 24 V supply and a transmitter that needs 12 V, the loop can drive at most (24 − 12) ÷ 0.020 = **600 Ω** in total.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the live zero.** 4 mA is 0%, so it maps to 0 V on a 0 to 10 V output, not 2 V. Mapping mA to volts by simple proportion (V = mA ÷ 2) gives a 2 V offset at zero.
- **Converter configured for the wrong input range.** If an isolator is set to 0 to 20 mA but fed 4 to 20 mA, 4 mA shows up as 20% and the span is wrong too.
- **Ground loops.** Non-isolated converters tie the field and control system grounds together, and the circulating current shows up as an offset or noise. Use an isolator when the two sides are grounded at different points.
- **Exceeding the loop load.** Adding a recorder, an indicator and a 250 Ω input in series can push the transmitter below its minimum voltage, and the signal flattens near 20 mA.
- **Reading 0 to 10 V over long cables.** Voltage signals lose accuracy to cable resistance and pick up noise. For long runs, convert to 4 to 20 mA at the field end.

## Choosing a signal

- **4–20 mA** is immune to voltage drop along the cable, detects a broken wire and can power 2-wire transmitters — the plant standard.
- **0–10 V** is common for drives, dampers and building automation over short distances.
- **1–5 V** is the voltage across a 250 Ω resistor in a 4–20 mA loop and is how many controllers read current inputs.
