## How to use the torque calculator

Enter the **force**, the **lever arm length** (from the pivot or bolt centre to where the force is applied) and the **angle** between the force and the lever — 90° for a push at right angles. The calculator gives the torque and the effective (perpendicular) lever arm. To find the force needed for a specified torque, enter the torque and press × on Force.

## Torque formula

<p class="formula">τ = F × r × sin θ &nbsp;·&nbsp; τ = F × r<sub>⊥</sub></p>

Torque is greatest when the force is perpendicular to the lever (θ = 90°) and zero when pushing along the lever (θ = 0°).

## Worked example

A force of 200 N on a 0.3 m spanner at 90°:

- τ = 200 × 0.3 × sin 90° = **60 N·m** = 44.3 lbf·ft = 6.12 kgf·m
- Pushing at 45° instead gives only 42.4 N·m — the perpendicular lever arm shrinks to 0.212 m

## Levers and balance

A lever balances when the torques on both sides are equal: F₁ × r₁ = F₂ × r₂. A 50 kg child 2 m from a see-saw pivot balances a 100 kg adult 1 m from it.

## Typical torques

| Task | Typical torque |
|---|---|
| Opening a jar lid | 1–3 N·m |
| M8 steel bolt (property class 8.8) | about 25 N·m |
| Bicycle pedal (hard effort) | 50–100 N·m |
| Car wheel nuts | 80–140 N·m |
| Small car engine (peak) | 100–250 N·m |

## Second scenario: a weight on a tilted bar

The angle in the formula is between the force and the lever, not between the lever and the ground. Hang a 10 kg mass (98.07 N) at the end of a 0.3 m bar. With the bar horizontal the angle is 90°, so τ = 98.07 × 0.3 = **29.42 N·m**. Raise the bar to 30° above horizontal and gravity, which still points straight down, now meets the bar at 60°: τ = 98.07 × 0.3 × sin 60° = **25.48 N·m**. Entering 30° here would give half the torque, which is the most common error with this formula.

## Torque wrench extensions

A crowfoot or adapter that sticks out 5 cm in line with a 40 cm torque wrench lengthens the lever. Set to 60 N·m, the bolt actually receives 60 × (0.40 + 0.05) ÷ 0.40 = **67.5 N·m**. To deliver 60 N·m, set the wrench to 60 × 0.40 ÷ 0.45 = **53.3 N·m**. An adapter turned at 90° to the handle adds no length and needs no correction. Without any adapter, 60 N·m on this wrench needs 150 N at the grip.

## How torque is measured

Click type torque wrenches release at a preset value, beam and dial wrenches show the torque as it rises, and electronic wrenches read a strain gauge in the shaft. Workshop wrenches are usually certified to within about 4% of reading and should be checked on a calibrator every year or after a drop. On test benches, rotating torque is measured with an in-line torque transducer between motor and load, and static torque with a reaction sensor or with a known weight hung on a measured arm, exactly as in the scenario above.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the angle to the horizontal** instead of the angle between the force and the lever, as in the tilted bar example.
- **Measuring the lever from the wrong point.** The arm runs from the centre of the bolt or axle to the line of the force, not from the end of the jaw.
- **Confusing lbf·ft with lbf·in.** One lbf·ft is 12 lbf·in, so a spec of 60 lbf·in is only 6.78 N·m.
- **Treating kgf as a mass.** A kgf·m is 9.80665 N·m; entering a mass in kg as the force gives a torque about ten times too small.
- **Forgetting the friction share in bolting.** Most of a tightening torque is spent on thread and head friction, so the same torque gives very different clamp loads with dry and oiled threads.

## Practical notes

- Use a calibrated **torque wrench** where a bolt torque is specified, and hold it at the marked grip — pulling elsewhere changes the lever length.
- An **in-line extension** on a torque wrench increases the applied torque: actual torque = reading × (L + extension) ÷ L, where L is the wrench length.
- For the torque needed to reach a given bolt preload, use the [bolt torque calculator](/engineering/bolt-torque/); for torque from power and speed, the [torque–power–speed calculator](/engineering/torque-power-speed/).
