## How to use the vortex shedding frequency calculator

Enter the **flow velocity**, the **width** of the bluff body (or the diameter of a cylinder such as a thermowell) and the **Strouhal number**. You get the shedding frequency. Add the **pipe internal diameter** to see the volume flow and an approximate K-factor in pulses per litre.

## Vortex shedding formula

<p class="formula">f = St × V ÷ d</p>

<p class="formula">Q = V × π D² ÷ 4 &nbsp;·&nbsp; K = f ÷ Q</p>

## Example

Water at 3 m/s past a 20 mm bluff body (St = 0.2) in a 100 mm pipe:

- f = 0.2 × 3 ÷ 0.020 = **30 Hz**
- Q = 3 × π × 0.1² ÷ 4 = **84.82 m³/h**
- K ≈ 30 ÷ 23.56 L/s = **1.273 pulses per litre**

## Practical notes

- Vortex meters need a minimum Reynolds number (typically about 10,000–20,000) to shed regularly — below that they cut off.
- Typical sizing velocities: liquids 0.3–7 m/s, gases and steam about 2–80 m/s.
- Pipe vibration at frequencies near the shedding frequency can cause false counts; see the [K-factor calculator](/engineering/flow-meter-k-factor/) for pulse-to-flow scaling.

## Checking the Reynolds number and low flow limit

The Strouhal number only stays near 0.2 when the flow is turbulent. For water (ν ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m²/s) at 3 m/s in the 100 mm pipe, Re = 3 × 0.1 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ = **300,000**, well inside the linear range. If the meter needs Re ≥ 20,000 to shed reliably, the lowest usable velocity is 20,000 × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.1 = **0.20 m/s**, which is 5.65 m³/h and a shedding frequency of only 2.0 Hz. Below that the meter cuts off to zero. A viscous liquid raises this limit in direct proportion to its kinematic viscosity.

The K-factor falls roughly with the cube of the bore, so large meters give few pulses per litre and need a longer gate time for a stable reading.

## Thermowells and other immersed bodies

The same formula gives the forcing frequency on a thermowell, with d equal to the tip diameter. ASME PTC 19.3 TW compares that frequency with the well's natural frequency and requires the ratio to stay below set limits, so a short, stiff well or a smaller tip may be needed at high gas or steam velocities. A velocity collar is no longer recommended as a fix.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the pipe velocity at low Reynolds number.** Below the meter's minimum Reynolds number, shedding becomes irregular and stops, so a viscous fluid can read zero at flows the frequency formula says are measurable.
- **Ignoring blockage.** The bluff body narrows the flow area, so the velocity at the body is higher than the pipe velocity and the real K-factor differs from this estimate.
- **Mounting on a vibrating pipe.** Pump or compressor vibration near the shedding frequency can be counted as flow, even with no flow at all.
- **Too little straight pipe.** Elbows and valves upstream distort the vortex street; follow the maker's upstream and downstream run lengths.
- **Measuring two-phase flow.** Gas in liquid or liquid droplets in steam change the shedding pattern and give unreliable readings.
