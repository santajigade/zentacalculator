  - q: What is the Arrhenius acceleration factor formula?
    a: AF = exp[(Ea ÷ k) × (1/T_use − 1/T_stress)], with temperatures in kelvin and Boltzmann's constant k = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K. It is the ratio of the failure rate at the stress temperature to that at the use temperature.
  - q: What activation energy should I use?
    a: Use the value for the failure mechanism if it is known — for example around 0.3–0.5 eV for oxide defects and 0.5–0.9 eV for electromigration. 0.7 eV is the common default for semiconductor HTOL reports; a lower Ea gives a smaller, more conservative AF.
  - q: How is the FIT rate calculated with zero failures?
    a: With no failures, the upper confidence bound on the failure rate is λ = −ln(1 − CL) ÷ (device-hours × AF). At 60% confidence −ln(0.4) = 0.916, at 90% it is 2.303. This equals the chi-square method χ²(CL, 2) ÷ (2 × device-hours × AF).
  - q: Why is MTTF from a life test so large?
    a: Because it is an MTTF for random failures during the useful life, not a wear-out lifetime. An MTTF of 1,000 years means that in a population of 1,000 devices about one fails per year — not that a single device lasts 1,000 years.

## How to use the acceleration factor calculator

Enter the **activation energy**, the **use temperature** (junction or component temperature in the field) and the **stress temperature** of the test. That gives the acceleration factor. To turn a life test into a failure rate, also enter the **number of devices**, the **test hours** and the **confidence level**; the calculator assumes the test finished with **zero failures** and returns the equivalent device-hours, the upper-bound FIT rate and the lower-bound MTTF.

## Acceleration factor formulas

<p class="formula">AF = exp[(E<sub>a</sub> ÷ k)(1/T<sub>use</sub> − 1/T<sub>stress</sub>)] &nbsp;·&nbsp; k = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K</p>

<p class="formula">λ<sub>upper</sub> = −ln(1 − CL) ÷ (N × t × AF) &nbsp;·&nbsp; FIT = λ × 10⁹ h &nbsp;·&nbsp; MTTF = 1/λ</p>

## Worked example

A JEDEC-style HTOL test of 77 devices for 1,000 h at 125 °C with no failures, field use at 55 °C, E<sub>a</sub> = 0.7 eV, 60% confidence:

- AF = exp[(0.7 ÷ 8.617 × 10⁻⁵) × (1/328.15 − 1/398.15)] = **77.65**
- Equivalent device-hours = 77 × 1,000 × 77.65 = **5,978,694 h**
- Failure rate ≤ 0.916 ÷ 5,978,694 = **153 FIT**
- MTTF ≥ **6,524,888 h** (about 744 years)

## Practical notes

- Use the **junction temperature** (ambient + power × θ<sub>JA</sub>) for both test and use, not the oven or room temperature.
- The Arrhenius model covers temperature-driven mechanisms only. Humidity (Peck model, 85 °C/85% RH), voltage and temperature cycling (Coffin–Manson) need their own acceleration factors.
- With r failures, replace −ln(1 − CL) by χ²(CL; 2r + 2) ÷ 2 — for example 2.02 at 60% confidence and 1 failure.
- Don't push the stress temperature past the point where new failure mechanisms appear (package glass transition, solder melting), or the acceleration factor is meaningless.

## Planning a test to prove a FIT target

The calculator can be run backwards to size a test. Suppose a customer asks for no more than **50 FIT** at 60% confidence, with the same 55 °C use temperature, 125 °C stress and E<sub>a</sub> = 0.7 eV:

- Required equivalent device-hours = 0.916 ÷ (50 × 10⁻⁹) = **18,325,815 h**
- Real device-hours in the oven = 18,325,815 ÷ 77.65 = **236,019 h**
- With 77 devices that is about **3,065 h** of testing, or about **237 devices** for a standard 1,000 h run

The default test of 77 devices for 1,000 h only reaches 153 FIT, so either the sample, the duration or the stress temperature has to rise.

## How confidence level changes the result

For the default 77 × 1,000 h test with zero failures:

| Confidence | −ln(1 − CL) | FIT upper bound | MTTF lower bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50% | 0.693 | 116 FIT | 984 years |
| 60% | 0.916 | 153 FIT | 744 years |
| 90% | 2.303 | 385 FIT | 296 years |
| 95% | 2.996 | 501 FIT | 228 years |

Reports often quote 60% because it gives a smaller number; customers in automotive and medical work usually ask for 90%.

## How the test is run in practice

HTOL (high temperature operating life, JEDEC JESD22-A108) parts are biased and exercised inside a chamber, normally at 125 °C ambient, and taken out at read points such as 168, 500 and 1,000 hours for electrical testing at room and hot temperature. The acceleration factor must use the **junction** temperature. If each device dissipates 0.5 W with θ<sub>JA</sub> = 40 K/W, the junction sits at 145 °C, the acceleration factor rises to **206** and the same test demonstrates **58 FIT**. Ignoring that self heating understates the stress, while claiming it without measuring it overstates the result.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering temperatures in °C inside the formula.** The Arrhenius term needs kelvin; the calculator converts, but hand calculations with 55 and 125 give nonsense.
- **Assuming a cool field temperature.** If the product actually runs at 65 °C instead of 55 °C, AF falls from 77.6 to 37.3 and the same test only supports 319 FIT.
- **Picking a high E<sub>a</sub> to flatter the numbers.** Moving from 0.7 eV to 1.0 eV multiplies AF several times, so the value must come from the failure mechanism, not from the target.
- **Pooling tests with different conditions.** Device-hours from 125 °C and 150 °C runs must each be multiplied by their own AF before adding.
- **Reading MTTF as product life.** A demonstrated MTTF of hundreds of years describes the random failure rate, not how long a single part lasts before wear-out.
