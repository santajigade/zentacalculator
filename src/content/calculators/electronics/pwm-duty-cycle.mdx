## How to use the PWM duty cycle calculator

Enter the **PWM frequency**, the **on time** and the signal **amplitude**. You get the period, duty cycle, off time, and the average and RMS output voltages. To find the on time for a target duty cycle, enter the duty cycle and press × on On time.

## PWM duty cycle formulas

<p class="formula">T = 1 ÷ f &nbsp;·&nbsp; D = t<sub>on</sub> ÷ T &nbsp;·&nbsp; V<sub>avg</sub> = D × V &nbsp;·&nbsp; V<sub>rms</sub> = √D × V</p>

## Worked example

A 1 kHz PWM signal with a 0.25 ms pulse from a 12 V supply:

- Period T = 1 ÷ 1000 Hz = **1 ms**, so the off time is 1 − 0.25 = **0.75 ms**
- Duty cycle = 0.25 ÷ 1 = **25%**
- Average voltage = 0.25 × 12 = **3 V**; RMS voltage = √0.25 × 12 = **6 V**

## 8-bit and 10-bit PWM values

| Duty cycle | 8-bit value (0–255) | 10-bit value (0–1023) | Average of 12 V |
|---|---|---|---|

| 10% | 26 | 102 | 1.20 V |

## PWM resolution vs frequency

A timer counting at f<sub>clk</sub> with N-bit resolution can produce at most f<sub>clk</sub> ÷ 2<sup>N</sup> (fast PWM); phase-correct PWM halves it.

| Resolution | Max. frequency at 16 MHz | Max. frequency at 72 MHz | Duty step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8-bit (256 steps) | 62.500 kHz | 281.250 kHz | 0.3906% |
| 10-bit (1,024 steps) | 15.625 kHz | 70.312 kHz | 0.0977% |
| 12-bit (4,096 steps) | 3.906 kHz | 17.578 kHz | 0.0244% |
| 16-bit (65,536 steps) | 0.244 kHz | 1.099 kHz | 0.0015% |

The Arduino Uno's default analogWrite frequencies come from the 16 MHz clock with a ÷64 prescaler: 16 MHz ÷ 64 ÷ 256 = **976.56 Hz** on pins 5 and 6 (fast PWM) and 16 MHz ÷ 64 ÷ 510 = **490.20 Hz** on the others (phase-correct).

## Average vs RMS

A resistive heater cares about RMS (its power is V²/R), while a DC motor's speed follows the average voltage. At 25% duty, 12 V gives 3 V average but 6 V RMS — the heater gets 25% of full power.

## Practical notes

- To turn PWM into a steady analogue voltage, add an [RC low-pass filter](/electronics/rc-filter/) with a cutoff at least 10× below the PWM frequency.
- Inductive loads (motors, solenoids) need a flyback diode across them; the current keeps flowing during the off time.
- At very low or very high duty cycles, the driver's rise and fall times become a large part of the pulse and the real average differs from D × V.

## Filtering the default signal into a DC level

Passing the default 1 kHz, 25%, 12 V signal through a 10 kΩ and 1 µF low-pass filter (τ = 10 ms, cutoff 15.9 Hz) gives a 3 V average with a steady-state ripple of

V<sub>pp</sub> = V (1 − e<sup>−t<sub>on</sub>/τ</sup>)(1 − e<sup>−t<sub>off</sub>/τ</sup>) ÷ (1 − e<sup>−T/τ</sup>) = **225 mV**

and takes about 5τ = **50 ms** to settle after a duty change. A bigger time constant lowers the ripple but slows the response:

| Time constant | Cutoff | Ripple (p-p) | Settling (5τ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 ms | 159.2 Hz | 2,216 mV | 5 ms |
| 2.2 ms | 72.3 Hz | 1,019 mV | 11 ms |
| 4.7 ms | 33.9 Hz | 478 mV | 23.5 ms |
| 10 ms | 15.9 Hz | 225 mV | 50 ms |
| 22 ms | 7.2 Hz | 102 mV | 110 ms |
| 47 ms | 3.4 Hz | 48 mV | 235 ms |

Raising the PWM frequency is the better lever: the ripple falls almost in proportion to frequency without slowing the response.

## On-time versus duty register

Timers set either a fixed on-time or a fixed compare value relative to the period. Keeping the 0.25 ms pulse but moving to 2 kHz makes the duty **50%** and the average 6 V, while a fixed compare value keeps 25% at any frequency. Servos are the opposite case: they read the pulse width, so 1.5 ms at 50 Hz is centre position even though the duty is only 7.5%.

## Measuring PWM in practice

An oscilloscope shows the duty directly and reveals slow edges. Most digital multimeters on DC volts display the average, 3 V for the default signal, while a true RMS meter set to AC+DC reads 6 V. Many meters also have a duty-cycle % function, but it needs a clean signal above the meter's trigger level. For a 6 Ω heater the power at 25% is 6² ÷ 6 = 6 W, a quarter of the 24 W at full on.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting inverted drive logic.** A P-channel high-side switch or an active-low driver is on when the pin is low, so the load sees 100% minus the programmed duty.
- **Changing frequency with a fixed on-time.** As shown above, the duty and output double when the frequency doubles.
- **Setting the filter cutoff too close to the PWM frequency.** With τ = 1 ms at 1 kHz the ripple is still 2,216 mV, far from a clean DC level.
- **Ignoring edge time at high frequency.** At 20 kHz the period is 50 µs, so 2 µs rise and fall edges are a noticeable part of a 25% on-time of 12.5 µs.
- **Measuring with an average-responding AC meter.** On a pulse train it reads neither the average nor the RMS correctly.
