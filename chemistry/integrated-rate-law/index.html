    The integrated rate law tells you how the concentration of a reactant falls with time, and its form depends on the reaction order: linear for zero order, exponential for first order and hyperbolic for second order. This integrated rate law calculator gives the remaining concentration, the fraction converted, the half-life and the rate at any time for all three orders, and can solve for the time, rate constant or starting concentration instead.

    How to use the integrated rate law calculator

    Choose the reaction order, then enter the initial concentration, the rate constant (always per second) and the time elapsed. The calculator gives the concentration remaining, the fraction remaining and converted, the half-life measured from the starting concentration and the rate at that moment. To find the time to reach a given concentration, enter it in the [A] field and press × on the time; the same works for the rate constant or the starting concentration.

    Integrated rate law equations

    Order Rate law Integrated form Straight-line plot Half-life
    Zero rate = k [A] = [A]₀ − k t [A] vs t, slope −k [A]₀ ÷ 2k
    First rate = k[A] [A] = [A]₀ e−kt ln[A] vs t, slope −k ln 2 ÷ k
    Second rate = k[A]² 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + k t 1/[A] vs t, slope +k 1 ÷ (k[A]₀)

    The second-order form is for a single reactant (2A → products) or for A + B with equal starting concentrations.

    Worked example

    A first-order decomposition with k = 0.001 s⁻¹ starts at [A]₀ = 0.5 mol/L. After 10 min (600 s):

    • [A] = 0.5 × e−0.001 × 600 = 0.5 × e−0.6 = 0.27441 mol/L
    • Fraction remaining = 54.88%, so 45.12% has reacted
    • Half-life = 0.6931 ÷ 0.001 = 693.1 s = 11.55 min
    • Rate at 10 min = 0.001 × 0.27441 = 2.7441 × 10⁻⁴ mol/(L·s)

    For comparison, a zero-order reaction with k = 1 × 10⁻⁴ M/s would have [A] = 0.5 − 10⁻⁴ × 600 = 0.44 mol/L after the same time, and a second-order reaction with k = 2 × 10⁻³ M⁻¹s⁻¹ would have [A] = 1 ÷ (1/0.5 + 0.002 × 600) = 0.3125 mol/L.

    How the three orders differ

    All three start at 1 mol/L with a first half-life of 10 minutes:

    Time Zero order First order Second order
    0 min 1.000 M 1.000 M 1.000 M
    5 min 0.750 M 0.707 M 0.667 M
    10 min 0.500 M 0.500 M 0.500 M
    20 min 0.000 M 0.250 M 0.333 M
    30 min 0.000 M 0.125 M 0.250 M
    40 min 0.000 M 0.062 M 0.200 M

    A zero-order reaction runs at a constant rate and stops abruptly when the reactant is used up; a second-order reaction slows down most as the concentration falls.

    Practical notes

    • Rate constants depend on temperature — often roughly doubling for every 10 °C rise. Use the Arrhenius equation calculator to correct k to another temperature.
    • Pseudo-first-order: when one reactant is in large excess, its concentration hardly changes and a second-order reaction behaves as first order with k′ = k [B]₀.
    • Radioactive decay, drug elimination and many decompositions are first order; reactions on a saturated catalyst surface or limited by an enzyme at high substrate concentration are approximately zero order.
    • For radioactive decay with a known half-life, the half-life calculator works directly in years.

    Frequently asked questions

    What are the integrated rate laws?

    Zero order [A] = [A]₀ − k t; first order ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − k t, so [A] = [A]₀ e^(−kt); second order 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + k t. Plotting [A], ln[A] or 1/[A] against time gives a straight line of slope −k, −k or +k respectively, which is how the order is found from experimental data.

    How do you calculate the half-life of a reaction?

    For first order t½ = ln 2 ÷ k = 0.693 ÷ k, independent of concentration. For zero order t½ = [A]₀ ÷ (2k), and for second order t½ = 1 ÷ (k [A]₀) — both depend on the starting concentration, so successive half-lives get shorter (zero order) or longer (second order).

    How do I find the time to reach a certain concentration?

    Enter the target concentration in the [A] field and press × on the time — the calculator solves the rate law for t. For first order, t = ln([A]₀ ÷ [A]) ÷ k; for second order t = (1/[A] − 1/[A]₀) ÷ k.

    What are the units of the rate constant?

    They depend on the order so that the rate always comes out in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ — mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ for zero order, s⁻¹ for first order and L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ for second order. In general the units are (mol/L)^(1−n) s⁻¹.
