## How to use the pipe flow velocity calculator

Enter the **flow rate** and the pipe's **inner diameter** to get the velocity. You can also solve for the flow rate at a given velocity, or the diameter needed to keep velocity within limits — press × on the field you want calculated.

Units mix freely: flow in m³/h, L/min or US gpm; diameter in mm or inches; velocity in m/s or ft/s.

## Pipe flow velocity formula

<p class="formula">A = π D² ÷ 4   v = Q ÷ A</p>

## Worked example

50 m³/h of water in a pipe with 100 mm inner diameter:

- Q = 50 ÷ 3600 = 0.01389 m³/s
- A = π × 0.1² ÷ 4 = 0.007854 m²
- v = 0.01389 ÷ 0.007854 = **1.77 m/s**

## Recommended velocities (typical guidance)

| Service | Velocity |
|---|---|
| Water, pump suction | 0.6 – 1.5 m/s |
| Water, pump discharge | 1.5 – 3 m/s |
| Water, gravity drains | 0.6 – 1.2 m/s |
| Viscous oils | 0.5 – 1.5 m/s |
| Compressed air | 6 – 10 m/s |

Always check the design standards for your industry.

## Choosing a pipe size

Rearrange the formula to find the diameter for a target velocity:

<p class="formula">D = √(4Q ÷ (π × v))</p>

For 50 m³/h at 2 m/s: D = √(4 × 0.01389 ÷ 6.283) = **94 mm**, so DN100 is a suitable size.

## Water hammer

Fast valve closure in a pipe with high velocity creates a pressure surge. The Joukowsky equation gives the surge: ΔP = ρ × a × Δv, with a about 1,200–1,400 m/s in steel water pipes. Stopping 2 m/s instantly can create a surge of over 25 bar. Keep velocities moderate and close valves slowly.

## Why velocity matters for flow meters

Most flow meters have a velocity range. Electromagnetic meters typically need about 0.3–10 m/s, and many work best above 1 m/s. If the velocity is too low, a smaller meter (with reducers) often improves accuracy. Check the [Reynolds number](/engineering/reynolds-number/) too, since many meters need fully turbulent flow.
