## How to use the dBm to watts converter

Enter a value in **dBm** to get watts (choose W, mW or kW), dBW and the RMS voltage across a 50 Ω load. To convert from watts, enter the power and press × on dBm.

## dBm to watts formula

<p class="formula">P(mW) = 10<sup>dBm/10</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; dBm = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW) &nbsp;·&nbsp; dBW = dBm − 30</p>

## Link budgets

In dB terms, you simply add gains and subtract losses: transmit 20 dBm + 6 dBi antenna − 80 dB path loss + 3 dBi antenna = −51 dBm received.

## Worked example

At the default **20 dBm**:

- P = 10<sup>20/10</sup> ÷ 1,000 = **100 mW** (0.1 W)
- dBW = 20 − 30 = **-10 dBW**
- V = √(0.1 × 50) = **2.236 V RMS**, or 3.162 V peak

Going the other way, a handheld radio rated at 4 W puts out 10 × log₁₀(4,000) = **36.02 dBm**.

## Link budget with cable losses

A 2.4 GHz point to point link: transmitter 20 dBm, antenna gain 6 dBi, cable loss 1.5 dB, free space loss 90 dB, receive antenna 3 dBi, receive cable 1 dB.

- Received = 20 + 6 − 1.5 − 90 + 3 − 1 = **-63.5 dBm**
- That is 446.7 pW, about 0.4467 nW
- Against a receiver sensitivity of -85 dBm the fade margin is **21.5 dB**

Every 3 dB doubles the power and every 10 dB multiplies it by ten, so most conversions can be done in your head once these steps are familiar.

## How RF power is measured

A thermal or diode power sensor on an RF power meter reads average power directly in dBm and is the reference for transmitter checks. A spectrum analyser shows the level of each signal on screen, usually in dBm with a 50 Ω input. High power transmitters are measured through a calibrated attenuator or directional coupler, and its value is added back: a meter reading 6 dBm through a 30 dB attenuator means the source delivers 36 dBm, about 4 W.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding two dBm values directly.** Two 20 dBm signals combine to 23.01 dBm, not 40 dBm; convert to milliwatts, add, then convert back.
- **Mixing dB and dBm.** A gain or loss is in dB; only an absolute power is in dBm.
- **Using the 50 Ω voltage in a 75 Ω system.** Cable TV uses 75 Ω, where 20 dBm is 2.739 V, not 2.236 V.
- **Comparing peak and average power.** Pulsed radar and OFDM signals have peak power several dB above the average a thermal meter shows.
- **Forgetting the attenuator or coupler factor** when reading a transmitter through a test setup.
