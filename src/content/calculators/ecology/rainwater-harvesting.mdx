## How to use the rainwater harvesting calculator

Enter the **roof area**, **annual rainfall** and choose the **roof type**. Yearly and average daily collection are calculated.

## Rainwater harvesting formula

<p class="formula">Volume = area × rainfall × runoff coefficient</p>

## Example

100 m² concrete roof, 800 mm annual rain: 100 × 0.8 × 0.85 = 68 m³ = **68,000 litres** a year (about 186 L per day on average).

Discard the "first flush" of each rain to keep debris out of the tank, and check local rules on using rainwater.

## Runoff coefficients by surface

| Surface | Coefficient (k) |
|---|---|
| Metal or glazed tile roof | 0.85–0.95 |
| Concrete or asphalt roof | 0.80–0.90 |
| Clay tile roof | 0.75–0.85 |
| Paved yard | 0.60–0.80 |
| Lawn or garden | 0.10–0.30 |

## Sizing the tank

A tank does not need to hold a whole year's rain. A common approach is to store enough for the **longest dry spell** you want to cover, balanced against the rain you can catch. In monsoon climates, larger tanks or recharge pits capture more of the heavy rainfall.

## First-flush diverters

The first few millimetres of rain wash dust, leaves and bird droppings off the roof. A first-flush diverter discards this dirty water before the rest flows to the tank. A common rule is about 1–2 L per m² of roof.

## Uses

- Garden watering and toilet flushing need little treatment.
- Drinking requires filtration and disinfection.
- Groundwater recharge through pits or wells helps raise local water tables.

## Sizing a tank from monthly rainfall

An annual total hides the real problem in a monsoon climate: most rain falls in four months. Take the default roof (100 m², k = 0.85) and an illustrative 800 mm year, with a household using **150 L a day** for toilets, washing and garden:

The roof yields 68,000 L against an annual use of 54,750 L, so supply covers demand over the year. Running the monthly balance through two cycles shows that a tank of about **24.6 m³** (24,550 L) is needed to carry the monsoon surplus into the dry months with no shortfall. Most households settle for a smaller tank, accept municipal top-up from February to May, and send the overflow to a recharge pit.

## How the catchment is measured

Use the **plan area** of the roof, the footprint seen from above, not the sloped surface area: rain falls vertically, so a steep roof catches no more than a flat one of the same footprint. Only count roof sections whose gutters lead to the downpipe that feeds the tank. Take rainfall from the nearest India Meteorological Department or national weather station record, and design on a dry year rather than the average; at 75% of normal rainfall this roof gives about 51,000 L.

## First flush and filter losses

A first-flush diverter on this roof should hold 100 to 200 L (1 to 2 L per m²). It refills at every storm, so on a roof that sees many small showers the discarded volume adds up. Leaf screens and sand filters also spill water in heavy downpours. The runoff coefficient covers splash and evaporation, but not these losses.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the sloped roof area.** It overstates the yield; the horizontal footprint is the catchment.
- **Sizing the tank for the annual volume.** A 68 m³ tank would rarely fill and costs far more than needed; size it on the monthly balance.
- **Designing on average rainfall.** One year in four is well below average, so check the dry year too.
- **Mixing units.** One mm of rain on one m² is one litre; in inches and square feet, one inch on 1,000 ft² is about 623 US gallons.
- **Counting a green or gravel roof as a hard roof.** Their coefficient of about 0.5 can halve the expected yield.
