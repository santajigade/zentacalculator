## How to use the star-delta transformation calculator

Enter the three **delta resistances** between terminals a–b, b–c and c–a. The calculator gives the equivalent **star resistances** connected from each terminal to a common centre point. To go from star to delta, enter the star values and solve for the delta fields with the × buttons.

For more background, read [DOL vs Star-Delta Starter: Which to Use](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/dol-vs-star-delta-starter-which-to-use/).

## Delta to star

<p class="formula">R<sub>a</sub> = R<sub>ab</sub>R<sub>ca</sub> ÷ ΣR &nbsp;·&nbsp; R<sub>b</sub> = R<sub>ab</sub>R<sub>bc</sub> ÷ ΣR &nbsp;·&nbsp; R<sub>c</sub> = R<sub>bc</sub>R<sub>ca</sub> ÷ ΣR</p>

## Star to delta

<p class="formula">R<sub>ab</sub> = (R<sub>a</sub>R<sub>b</sub> + R<sub>b</sub>R<sub>c</sub> + R<sub>c</sub>R<sub>a</sub>) ÷ R<sub>c</sub></p>

and similarly for R<sub>bc</sub> (÷ R<sub>a</sub>) and R<sub>ca</sub> (÷ R<sub>b</sub>).

## Example

Delta 30, 60 and 90 Ω (sum 180 Ω):

- R<sub>a</sub> = 30 × 90 ÷ 180 = **15 Ω**
- R<sub>b</sub> = 30 × 60 ÷ 180 = **10 Ω**
- R<sub>c</sub> = 60 × 90 ÷ 180 = **30 Ω**

## When to use it

- Simplifying bridge networks that cannot be reduced with series and parallel rules alone.
- Converting delta-connected loads to star for per-phase calculations in three-phase systems.
- Analysing the [Wheatstone bridge](/electronics/wheatstone-bridge/) when it is unbalanced.

## Star-delta motor starting

A motor started in star sees 1/√3 of line voltage, so starting current and torque fall to one third of their direct-on-line values. After a few seconds, the starter switches to delta for full voltage.

## Checking the default conversion in reverse

Starting from the star values 15, 10 and 30 Ω, the sum of pairwise products is 15 × 10 + 10 × 30 + 30 × 15 = **900 Ω²**. Dividing by the opposite star resistor returns the original delta:

- R<sub>ab</sub> = 900 ÷ 30 = **30 Ω**
- R<sub>bc</sub> = 900 ÷ 15 = **60 Ω**
- R<sub>ca</sub> = 900 ÷ 10 = **90 Ω**

Running the conversion both ways like this is a quick way to catch a value typed into the wrong field.

## Worked example: solving an unbalanced bridge

Take the default delta (30, 60 and 90 Ω) as the top half of a bridge, add 40 Ω from b to d and 50 Ω from c to d, and apply 12 V between a and d. No resistor pair is in series or parallel, so convert the delta to star first:

1. The star gives R<sub>a</sub> = 15 Ω in the feed, then two branches: 10 + 40 = 50 Ω and 30 + 50 = 80 Ω.
2. The branches are now in parallel: 50 × 80 ÷ (50 + 80) = **30.769 Ω**.
3. Total resistance a to d = 15 + 30.769 = **45.769 Ω**, so the supply current is 12 ÷ 45.769 = **262.2 mA**.
4. The current divides 161.3 mA through the b branch and 100.8 mA through the c branch.

Notice that a delta with one very large resistor (the 1 / 1 / 1,000 Ω row) gives a star with one very small arm, which is why the transformation can turn an awkward network into a simple one.

## Practical notes

- In three-phase work, a balanced delta load of Z per phase behaves exactly like a star load of Z ÷ 3, so per-phase current and power can be found from line voltage ÷ √3.
- For AC networks with different phase angles, apply the same formulas with complex impedances; using magnitudes alone gives wrong answers.
- In measurement bridges, converting the ratio arms to star shows how lead and contact resistance add to the detector branch.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing up which resistors touch a node.** R<sub>a</sub> uses the two delta resistors connected to terminal a (R<sub>ab</sub> and R<sub>ca</sub>), not R<sub>bc</sub>.
- **Dividing by the wrong resistor in star to delta.** Each delta resistor is divided by the star arm opposite to it, the one not connected to either of its terminals.
- **Losing the terminals.** The star centre is a new internal node; the three outer terminals must stay connected to the rest of the circuit exactly as before.
- **Treating the star centre as the neutral of a supply.** It is a mathematical node, and its voltage is not zero unless the network is balanced.
