    a: It is valid only for water at roughly 5–25 °C in turbulent flow. For other liquids, hot water, viscous fluids or gases, use the Darcy–Weisbach equation.

## How to use the Hazen–Williams calculator

Enter the **flow rate**, the pipe **internal diameter**, its **length** and the **C factor** for the pipe material. You get the friction head loss, the equivalent pressure drop for water and the flow velocity. Add fittings with the [minor losses calculator](/engineering/minor-losses/).

## Hazen–Williams equation (SI)

<p class="formula">h<sub>f</sub> = 10.67 × L × Q<sup>1.852</sup> ÷ (C<sup>1.852</sup> × d<sup>4.87</sup>)</p>

<p class="formula">Δp = ρ g h<sub>f</sub> ≈ 9.81 kPa per metre of head</p>

## Example

50 m³/h of water through 100 m of 100 mm pipe, C = 130:

- Q = 50 ÷ 3600 = 0.01389 m³/s
- h<sub>f</sub> = **3.498 m** of head
- Δp = **34.30 kPa** (4.97 psi)
- Velocity = **1.77 m/s**

## Typical C factors

| Pipe material | C |
|---|---|
| PVC, HDPE, copper (smooth) | 140–150 |
| Cement-lined ductile iron | 130–140 |
| New welded steel | 120 |
| Galvanised steel | 120 |
| Old cast iron | 80–100 |

## Effect of the C factor

Same example (50 m³/h, 100 mm, 100 m). C falls as pipes age, scale and corrode, and head loss rises quickly as it does:

| C factor | Head loss | Pressure drop | vs C = 130 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 150 | 2.683 m | 26.31 kPa | 77% |
| 140 | 3.049 m | 29.90 kPa | 87% |
| 130 | 3.498 m | 34.30 kPa | 100% |
| 120 | 4.056 m | 39.78 kPa | 116% |
| 100 | 5.686 m | 55.76 kPa | 163% |
| 80 | 8.595 m | 84.29 kPa | 246% |

Designers often use a lower C than the new-pipe value to allow for 20 to 30 years of service, for example 100 to 120 for unlined steel.

## Head loss vs flow

Friction loss rises with Q<sup>1.852</sup>, so doubling the flow multiplies head loss by about 3.61. For 100 m of 100 mm pipe at C = 130:

| Flow | Velocity | Head loss | Pressure drop |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 m³/h | 0.88 m/s | 0.969 m | 9.50 kPa |
| 50 m³/h | 1.77 m/s | 3.498 m | 34.30 kPa |
| 75 m³/h | 2.65 m/s | 7.411 m | 72.68 kPa |
| 100 m³/h | 3.54 m/s | 12.626 m | 123.82 kPa |

## The US customary form

Fire sprinkler hydraulic calculations (NFPA 13) use p = 4.52 Q<sup>1.852</sup> ÷ (C<sup>1.852</sup> d<sup>4.87</sup>), with p in psi per foot, Q in gpm and d in inches. The example is 220.1 gpm in a 3.937 in bore: p = 0.0151 psi/ft, which agrees within rounding with the SI result of 0.3430 kPa/m (0.0152 psi/ft). Hydraulic software applies the formula pipe by pipe and adds fittings as equivalent lengths.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the nominal size instead of the bore.** A 100 mm (4 in) schedule 40 steel pipe has a 102.3 mm bore; at d<sup>4.87</sup> that 2.3% difference changes head loss by about 12%.
- **Using it for hot water, oil or gas.** The constants assume water near room temperature. Use the [Darcy-Weisbach calculator](/engineering/darcy-weisbach-head-loss/) for other fluids.
- **Forgetting the minor losses.** Valves, bends and tees can add as much loss as the straight pipe in short, fitting-rich runs.
- **Taking the new-pipe C for an old main.** Tuberculated cast iron can drop to C = 60 to 80, more than doubling friction loss.
- **Mixing units.** The 10.67 constant needs Q in m³/s and d in m; the 4.52 constant needs gpm and inches. Putting litres per second or millimetres into either gives nonsense.
