## How to use the Beer–Lambert law calculator

Enter the **molar absorptivity**, **path length** and **concentration** to get absorbance and transmittance — or enter a measured absorbance to find the concentration.

## Beer–Lambert law formulas

<p class="formula">A = ε l c   T = 10<sup>−A</sup></p>

## Worked example — NADH at 340 nm

ε = 6,220 L/(mol·cm), 1 cm cuvette, c = 1 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L:

- A = 6,220 × 1 × 0.0001 = **0.622**
- T = 10⁻⁰·⁶²² = **23.9%**

## Absorbance to transmittance table

| Absorbance | Transmittance | Light absorbed |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 89.1% | 10.9% |
| 0.1 | 79.4% | 20.6% |
| 0.2 | 63.1% | 36.9% |
| 0.3 | 50.1% | 49.9% |
| 0.5 | 31.6% | 68.4% |
| 1.0 | 10.0% | 90.0% |
| 1.5 | 3.2% | 96.8% |
| 2.0 | 1.0% | 99.0% |
| 3.0 | 0.1% | 99.9% |

Most spectrophotometers are most accurate between about **A = 0.1 and 1.0**. Dilute samples that read above this range.

## Building a calibration curve

1. Prepare standards of known concentration.
2. Measure absorbance at the chosen wavelength (λ<sub>max</sub>).
3. Plot absorbance against concentration — it should be a straight line through the origin.
4. Use the slope (ε × l) to find unknown concentrations.

## Common molar absorptivities

| Substance | Wavelength | ε (L/mol·cm) |
|---|---|---|
| NADH | 340 nm | 6,220 |
| DNA (per base, approx.) | 260 nm | about 6,600 |
| Potassium permanganate | 525 nm | about 2,400 |
| p-Nitrophenolate | 405 nm | about 18,000 |

## When Beer–Lambert breaks down

- **High concentrations** (above about 0.01 M): molecules interact and the line curves.
- **Scattering**: cloudy or turbid samples.
- **Stray light** and non-monochromatic light in the instrument.
- **Chemical changes**: equilibria that shift with dilution.

For nucleic acid samples, see the [DNA concentration calculator](/biology/dna-concentration/).

## Absorbance vs transmittance

| Absorbance | Light transmitted |
|---|---|
| 0 | 100% |
| 0.3 | 50% |
| 1 | 10% |
| 2 | 1% |

## From a reading back to concentration

Suppose the same NADH assay reads A = 0.450 in the 1 cm cuvette. Then c = A ÷ (ε l) = 0.450 ÷ (6,220 × 1) = **7.235 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L**, or 72.3 µmol/L. In a kinetic enzyme assay the same division turns a slope into a rate: a fall of 0.050 absorbance per minute at 340 nm means NADH is used at 8.04 µmol/L per minute.

## Effect of path length at the default concentration

| Path length | Absorbance | Transmittance |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2 cm | 0.124 | 75.09% |
| 0.5 cm | 0.311 | 48.87% |
| 1 cm | 0.622 | 23.88% |
| 2 cm | 1.244 | 5.70% |
| 5 cm | 3.110 | 0.08% |
| 10 cm | 6.220 | 0.00% |

Long path cells (5 to 10 cm) are used for trace analysis in clean water, while 0.1 to 0.2 cm cells or diluting the sample bring a strong absorber back into range.

## Why mid-range readings are most precise

A photometer's noise is roughly a fixed error in transmittance. For a 0.5% T reading error the relative error in concentration is 0.4343 ΔT ÷ (T A):

| Absorbance | Transmittance | Concentration error |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 89.1% | 4.87% |
| 0.2 | 63.1% | 1.72% |
| 0.4343 | 36.8% | 1.36% |
| 1 | 10.0% | 2.17% |
| 2 | 1.0% | 10.86% |

The minimum sits at A = 0.434 (T = 36.8%). The default reading of A = 0.622 gives about 1.46%, comfortably inside the good zone.

## How stray light bends the curve

Stray light is light that reaches the detector outside the selected band. With 0.1% stray light the instrument reads A = −log((T + s) ÷ (1 + s)):

| True absorbance | Reading with 0.1% stray light | Error |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.499 | -0.2% |
| 1 | 0.996 | -0.4% |
| 2 | 1.959 | -2.0% |
| 3 | 2.699 | -10.0% |

This is why calibration lines flatten at the top even when the chemistry is perfectly linear.

## Measuring in practice

Zero the instrument with a blank of the same solvent and reagents in a matched cuvette, then read at λ<sub>max</sub>, where small wavelength errors matter least. Polystyrene cuvettes absorb strongly in the deep UV, so 260 nm work needs quartz or UV-grade cells. In microplate readers the light passes vertically, so the path length depends on the fill volume; many readers correct it from the water absorbance band near 977 nm.

## Common mistakes

- **Entering percent transmittance as absorbance.** Convert first: A = −log₁₀(T), so 23.9% T is A = 0.622, not 23.9.
- **Mixing ε units.** NADH is often quoted as 6.22 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹, which is 6,220 L/(mol·cm); using 6.22 with mol/L gives a concentration a thousand times too high.
- **Assuming a 1 cm path in a microplate well.** A partly filled well has a much shorter path, so concentrations come out too low unless you correct it.
- **Skipping the blank.** Reagent colour and cuvette absorbance add to every reading and shift the whole calibration line upward.
- **Reading off the absorption peak.** On a steep shoulder a 1 nm wavelength drift changes ε noticeably and the results drift with it.
