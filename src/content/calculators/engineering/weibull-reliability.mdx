faq:
  - q: What is the Weibull reliability function?
    a: R(t) = exp(−(t/η)^β). At t = η the reliability is always e⁻¹ ≈ 36.8%, whatever β is — so η is the time by which 63.2% of units have failed.
  - q: What does the shape parameter β mean?
    a: β < 1 means a falling failure rate (infant mortality), β = 1 a constant failure rate (the exponential distribution, random failures) and β > 1 a rising failure rate (wear-out). Bearings are typically around 1.1–1.5 and fatigue or corrosion failures 2–4.
  - q: How is MTTF calculated for a Weibull distribution?
    a: MTTF = η × Γ(1 + 1/β), where Γ is the gamma function. For β = 1 it equals η; for β = 2 it is 0.886 η; for β = 3.5 it is about 0.9 η.
  - q: What is B10 life?
    a: The time by which 10% of the population has failed (90% reliability), B10 = η × (−ln 0.9)^(1/β). It is the usual rating life for rolling bearings.
  - q: "What is the chance a part that has already run for a while survives longer?"
    a: "Use conditional reliability, R(t + Δt) ÷ R(t). For β = 1.5 and η = 10,000 h, a seal that has run 5,000 h has a 89.5% chance of lasting another 1,000 h, compared with 96.9% for a new one."
  - q: "How many failures do I need to estimate the Weibull parameters?"
    a: "A rough β and η can come from 3 to 5 failures, but the estimate of β is uncertain until you have about 10 to 20. Include the units that are still running as suspensions, or η will be underestimated."

## How to use the Weibull reliability calculator

Enter the **shape parameter β** and the **characteristic life η** (from a Weibull plot of failure data or a supplier's life test) and the **operating time t**. The calculator returns the reliability and probability of failure at t, the hazard rate at t in failures per million hours, the mean life and the B10 life. To find the time for a target reliability, enter R and press × on t.

## Weibull reliability formulas

<p class="formula">R(t) = e<sup>−(t/η)<sup>β</sup></sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; F(t) = 1 − R(t)</p>

<p class="formula">h(t) = (β/η)(t/η)<sup>β−1</sup> &nbsp;·&nbsp; MTTF = η Γ(1 + 1/β) &nbsp;·&nbsp; B10 = η (−ln 0.9)<sup>1/β</sup></p>

## Worked example

A pump seal with β = 1.5 and η = 10,000 h, after t = 5,000 h:

- (t/η)<sup>β</sup> = 0.5<sup>1.5</sup> = 0.3536, so R = e<sup>−0.3536</sup> = **70.22%** and F = **29.78%**
- Hazard rate = (1.5 ÷ 10,000) × 0.5<sup>0.5</sup> = **106.1 per 10⁶ h** — and rising, because β > 1
- MTTF = 10,000 × Γ(1.667) = **9,027 h**; B10 life = **2,231 h**

## Practical notes

- With β > 1, **preventive replacement** before wear-out makes sense; with β ≤ 1 it does not — replacing a part early only resets the infant-mortality period.
- Fit β and η from field data with a Weibull probability plot (median ranks) or maximum likelihood; at least 5–10 failures give a usable β.
- A three-parameter Weibull adds a failure-free time γ: replace t with (t − γ) when no failures can occur before γ.
- For β = 1 the result equals the exponential model used for MTBF and SIL calculations, with η = MTBF.

## Reliability over the next mission, not from new

Reliability from new is not the same as the chance that a unit already in service survives a little longer. For the default seal (β = 1.5, η = 10,000 h) that has run 5,000 h, the chance of lasting a further 1,000 h is

- R(6,000) ÷ R(5,000) = 0.6283 ÷ 0.7022 = **89.48%**

A new seal over its first 1,000 h has R(1,000) = **96.89%**. The older seal is less likely to finish the next 1,000 h because β > 1 means the hazard rate climbs with age. With β = 1 the two numbers would be identical, which is the memoryless property of the exponential model. To reach 99% reliability from new (B1 life), the seal must be replaced by 466 h.

## Estimating β and η from failure records

Six seals from the same pump type failed at the hours below. Rank them, give each a median rank F = (i − 0.3) ÷ (n + 0.4) (Benard's approximation) and fit a straight line of ln(−ln(1 − F)) against ln(t).

| Order i | Failure time | Median rank F |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,100 h | 10.9% |
| 2 | 3,900 h | 26.6% |
| 3 | 5,600 h | 42.2% |
| 4 | 7,300 h | 57.8% |
| 5 | 9,800 h | 73.4% |
| 6 | 13,500 h | 89.1% |

The least squares slope is **β ≈ 1.59** and the line crosses 63.2% at **η ≈ 8,153 h**. Those two numbers go straight into the calculator. With only six points the β estimate has a wide confidence band, so treat a value near 1 with caution before deciding whether failures are random or age related. Units removed for other reasons while still working are suspensions and need a rank adjustment (Johnson's method) or a maximum likelihood fit; leaving them out makes η look shorter than it is.

For β below about 3.6 the mean life sits above the median, because a few long survivors pull the average up. For a small β the B1 life is a tiny fraction of η, which is why early failures dominate the warranty cost of such parts.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading η as the mean life.** η is the 63.2% failure point. The mean is η Γ(1 + 1/β), which is 9,027 h for the defaults, not 10,000 h.
- **Mixing failure modes in one fit.** Seal leaks and bearing seizures on the same pump have different β values. Fitting them together gives a bent plot and a β that describes neither.
- **Using calendar time for a part that ages with running hours or cycles.** Standby hours on a spare pump do not wear the seal the same way, so use operating hours, starts or cycles as the time axis.
- **Quoting the hazard rate at one time as a constant failure rate.** With β = 1.5 the rate at 5,000 h is 106.1 per 10⁶ h, but at 1,000 h it is only 47.4. A single λ is valid only when β is close to 1.
- **Replacing parts with β ≤ 1 on a fixed schedule.** It adds early failures instead of removing wear-out ones, and costs parts and downtime for no gain.
