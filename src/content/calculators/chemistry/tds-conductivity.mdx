## How to use the TDS to conductivity calculator

Enter the **measured conductivity** in µS/cm, the **water temperature** and the **temperature coefficient** (2 %/°C is typical; enter 0 if your meter already shows a 25 °C compensated value), then the **TDS factor**. The calculator gives the conductivity at 25 °C and the TDS in mg/L (ppm). To find the conductivity that corresponds to a target TDS, enter the TDS and press × on the measured conductivity.

## TDS and conductivity formulas

<p class="formula">EC<sub>25</sub> = EC<sub>T</sub> ÷ [1 + α (T − 25 °C)] &nbsp;·&nbsp; TDS = k × EC<sub>25</sub></p>

The factor k depends on which ions are dissolved: about 0.5 for NaCl, 0.55–0.7 for most fresh waters, and up to 0.8 for waters rich in sulphate. For accurate work, calibrate k against a gravimetric TDS test (evaporation at 180 °C) for your water.

## Worked example

A sample reads 750 µS/cm at 20 °C (α = 2 %/°C, k = 0.65):

- EC<sub>25</sub> = 750 ÷ [1 + 0.02 × (20 − 25)] = 750 ÷ 0.9 = **833.3 µS/cm**
- TDS = 0.65 × 833.3 = **542 mg/L** — rated "good" for taste and just above India's 500 mg/L acceptable limit

## Practical notes

- **1 mS/cm = 1,000 µS/cm**; some meters show EC in mS/cm or dS/m (1 dS/m = 1 mS/cm), and hydroponic "CF" units are EC in mS/cm × 10.
- Different TDS meters use different built-in factors (0.5 "NaCl", 0.7 "442"), so two meters can show different ppm for the same water — compare EC, not ppm.
- Conductivity is not a measure of purity against non-ionic contaminants: sugar, oils and many organics do not conduct and are not counted.
- Keep the conductivity cell clean and calibrated with a standard (e.g. 1,413 µS/cm KCl); the cell constant drifts as electrodes foul.

## Temperature correction at 2 %/°C

Conductivity readings change by about 2% for every degree away from 25 °C. The table shows the correction factor 1 ÷ [1 + 0.02 (T − 25)] and what a raw reading of 750 µS/cm means at each sample temperature with k = 0.65:

| Sample temperature | Factor | EC at 25 °C | TDS |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 °C | 1.6667 | 1,250.0 µS/cm | 812 mg/L |
| 10 °C | 1.4286 | 1,071.4 µS/cm | 696 mg/L |
| 15 °C | 1.2500 | 937.5 µS/cm | 609 mg/L |
| 20 °C | 1.1111 | 833.3 µS/cm | 542 mg/L |
| 25 °C | 1.0000 | 750.0 µS/cm | 488 mg/L |
| 30 °C | 0.9091 | 681.8 µS/cm | 443 mg/L |
| 35 °C | 0.8333 | 625.0 µS/cm | 406 mg/L |

A reading taken on a cold winter sample can therefore understate the TDS by a quarter or more if no compensation is applied.

## Working back from a TDS limit

To set an alarm at 500 mg/L with k = 0.65, the conductivity at 25 °C must be 500 ÷ 0.65 = **769.2 µS/cm**. An uncompensated meter reading a 20 °C sample would show 769.2 × [1 + 0.02 × (20 − 25)] = **692.3 µS/cm** at that point, so the alarm in the raw units has to be lower than the compensated value.

## How conductivity is measured in practice

A conductivity cell applies an AC voltage between two or four electrodes and measures the current. The cell constant K (in cm⁻¹) relates the measured conductance to conductivity, and it is chosen for the range: about 0.01 to 0.1 cm⁻¹ for RO permeate and boiler feed water, 1.0 cm⁻¹ for drinking and process water, and 10 cm⁻¹ or an inductive (toroidal) sensor for brines and sea water. Calibrate with a potassium chloride standard close to the working range, such as 84, 1,413 or 12,880 µS/cm at 25 °C, and rinse the cell with sample before reading.

## Common mistakes

- **Applying compensation twice.** Most handheld meters already show a 25 °C value; entering the sample temperature again with α = 2 corrects it a second time.
- **Comparing ppm from two meters with different factors.** A meter set to 0.5 and one set to 0.7 differ by 40% on the same water. Compare EC instead.
- **Using 0.65 for RO permeate or brine.** Very pure water and concentrated salt solutions follow different factors; calibrate k against a gravimetric TDS for those waters.
- **Using a low cell constant on brackish water.** A K = 0.1 cell saturates or polarises at high conductivity and reads low.
- **Measuring with air bubbles in the cell.** Bubbles on the electrodes block current paths and give a falsely low reading, common on fresh tap samples.
