## How to use the I2C pull-up resistor calculator

Enter the **pull-up supply**, choose the **bus speed** (which sets the maximum rise time from the I²C specification), and enter the **sink current** and **bus capacitance**. The calculator returns the allowed range of pull-up values. Enter the resistor you plan to fit to check its rise time and sink current.

## I2C pull-up formulas

<p class="formula">R<sub>p(min)</sub> = (V<sub>DD</sub> − V<sub>OL(max)</sub>) / I<sub>OL</sub> &nbsp;·&nbsp; R<sub>p(max)</sub> = t<sub>r</sub> / (0.8473 × C<sub>b</sub>)</p>

## Worked example

3.3 V bus, Fast mode (t<sub>r</sub> ≤ 300 ns), V<sub>OL</sub> = 0.4 V at 3 mA, 200 pF:

- R<sub>p(min)</sub> = (3.3 − 0.4) ÷ 3 mA = **967 Ω**
- R<sub>p(max)</sub> = 300 ns ÷ (0.8473 × 200 pF) = **1,770 Ω**
- A 2.2 kΩ resistor gives t<sub>r</sub> = **373 ns** — too slow; a 1.5 kΩ gives 254 ns and passes

| Mode | Clock | Max rise time | Max bus capacitance | I<sub>OL</sub> at V<sub>OL</sub> = 0.4 V |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | 100 kHz | 1000 ns | 400 pF | 3 mA |
| Fast | 400 kHz | 300 ns | 400 pF | 3 mA |
| Fast-mode Plus | 1 MHz | 120 ns | 550 pF | 20 mA |

## Notes

- Fit one pull-up per line (SDA and SCL) for the whole bus — module boards often add their own, which appear in parallel.
- If no value satisfies both limits, reduce capacitance, use a bus buffer, or use an active current-source pull-up.

For the default bus (3.3 V, Fast mode, 200 pF, 3 mA sink), the window is 967 Ω to 1,770 Ω:

Only 1 kΩ, 1.2 kΩ and 1.5 kΩ pass. The default 2.2 kΩ gives 373 ns, above the 300 ns limit, and 1.8 kΩ just misses at 305 ns.

## Pull-ups on breakout boards add in parallel

Many sensor modules carry their own 10 kΩ pull-ups. With three such modules and a 4.7 kΩ on the main board, the line sees 1 ÷ (3/10 kΩ + 1/4.7 kΩ) = **1,950 Ω**, a 330 ns rise time at 200 pF, fine for 100 kHz but too slow for 400 kHz. Keep adding modules and the problem flips: with nine of them the total is **899 Ω**, below the 967 Ω minimum, and a 3 mA driver can no longer pull the line under 0.4 V. Remove the extra resistors with a solder jumper so the bus has one deliberate pull-up per line.

## Power and supply voltage

While a line is held low the pull-up burns V² ÷ R. For 2.2 kΩ on 3.3 V that is 4.95 mW, or roughly 0.75 mA average per line at a 50% low duty. Battery designs therefore pick the highest value that still meets the rise time, often at 100 kHz.

On a 5 V bus the minimum rises to (5.0 − 0.4) ÷ 3 mA = **1,533 Ω**, while the maximum at 100 kHz and 200 pF is 5,901 Ω, so 2.2 kΩ to 4.7 kΩ is a common choice there.

## Checking the bus with an oscilloscope

Probe SDA or SCL with a 10x probe and trigger on a rising edge. Measure from 30% to 70% of V<sub>DD</sub> (0.99 V to 2.31 V on a 3.3 V bus) and compare with the mode limit. You can then work backwards: C<sub>b</sub> = t<sub>r</sub> ÷ (0.8473 × R<sub>p</sub>). Also check the low level stays under 0.4 V when the weakest device pulls down.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying 4.7 kΩ from an Arduino example.** That suits 100 kHz and short wiring; at 400 kHz with a few modules it is usually too slow.
- **Forgetting module pull-ups.** Three or four boards in parallel can drop the total below R<sub>min</sub> and the low level fails.
- **Measuring rise time 10% to 90%.** The I²C specification uses 30% to 70%; the 10% to 90% time is about 2.6 times longer and would wrongly suggest a failure.
- **Pulling up to the wrong rail.** A 5 V pull-up on a 3.3 V only device can damage it; use a level shifter or a 3.3 V pull-up when all devices accept it.
- **Long cables without a buffer.** Over about 1 m of cable the capacitance passes 400 pF; use a bus extender or differential I²C.
